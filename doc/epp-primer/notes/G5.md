## 1. Overview (plain language)

By p. 32 the review has built the full field-theoretic machinery: the phonon self-energy $\Pi$ (Eq. 145), the electron Fan–Migdal and Debye–Waller self-energies (Eqs. 157–161), and the spectral function (Eqs. 171–174). In principle that is everything needed. The trouble is practical. Every one of these expressions is a Brillouin-zone (BZ) integral with denominators like $\varepsilon_{m\mathbf k+\mathbf q}-\varepsilon_{n\mathbf k}\pm\hbar\omega_{\mathbf q\nu}$, which become nearly singular whenever an electronic energy difference matches a phonon energy. Phonon energies are tiny (meV) compared with band widths (eV), so the integrands vary on very fine momentum scales. Converging such integrals can need up to $10^6$ $\mathbf k$ or $\mathbf q$ points, while one density-functional perturbation theory (DFPT) phonon calculation per $\mathbf q$ costs about as much as a full ground-state calculation.

**Section VI** gives the fix that made modern e–ph calculations possible. If the matrix element $g_{mn\nu}(\mathbf k,\mathbf q)$ is rotated into a basis of localized Wannier functions, it becomes short-ranged in real space. A coarse DFPT grid then fixes it completely, and it can be Fourier-interpolated cheaply to any fine grid ("Wannier interpolation", the method behind the EPW code). Polar materials break this. Displacing ions with nonzero Born effective charges $Z^*_\kappa$ creates dipoles whose potential falls off only as $1/R^2$. The matrix element then diverges as $1/|\mathbf q|$ (the Fröhlich coupling) and cannot be interpolated. The cure (Verdi and Giustino 2015; Sjakste et al. 2015) is to treat the divergent long-range part analytically from electrostatics, using $Z^*$ and the clamped-ion permittivity $\epsilon^\infty$ (Eq. 180). The same idea gives the nonanalytic LO–TO splitting of phonons. For metals, "Fermi-surface harmonics" turn 3D BZ integrals into 1D energy integrals.

**Section VII** shows where the Born–Oppenheimer *adiabatic* phonon picture fails. In doped graphene, graphite intercalation compounds and other metals, electrons near the Fermi level cannot follow a fast lattice vibration with $\mathbf q\approx0$. Including the dynamical (nonadiabatic) phonon self-energy changes the frequency, by about 1% in graphene and up to about 300 cm$^{-1}$ in KC$_8$. It also explains the Raman G-band behaviour versus gate doping. Phonon linewidths are usually computed with Allen's "double-delta" formula.

**Section VIII** connects to angle-resolved photoemission (ARPES). The Fan–Migdal self-energy is rewritten in terms of the Eliashberg spectral function $\alpha^2F$. It produces the photoemission "kink": the band velocity drops by $1+\lambda$ within a phonon energy of $E_F$, and the lifetime broadening jumps once holes can emit phonons. The mass-enhancement parameter $\lambda$ links ARPES, the low-temperature electronic heat capacity $C_e=\tfrac23\pi^2k_B^2N_F(1+\lambda)T$, and (later) superconductivity.

## 2. Logical flow

1. **(p. 32, end of V.B.5; see G4)** The Brillouin–Wigner solution (Eq. 164) of the Dyson equation gives S-shaped dispersions near $E_F$ (the photoemission kink, Fig. 4a). In a degenerately doped semiconductor with $\varepsilon_F\sim\hbar\omega$ (Fig. 4b), Eq. (164) gives a heavier parabolic band but misses the polaron satellite. That needs the full spectral function.
2. **(p. 32, V.B.6)** Model Hamiltonians (Fröhlich, Holstein, SSH, Hubbard–Holstein, …) can be solved nonperturbatively. *Ab initio* self-energies use bare propagators (Migdal level). The cumulant expansion $G_{n\mathbf k}(t)=(i/\hbar)\exp[-i(\varepsilon_{n\mathbf k}/\hbar)t+C_{n\mathbf k}(t)]$ resums higher-order diagrams starting from the Fan–Migdal self-energy.
3. **(VI intro)** Denominators in Eqs. (145) and (157) become small when $|\varepsilon_m-\varepsilon_n|\to\hbar\omega$. Convergence therefore needs very fine BZ grids (up to $10^6$ points), and direct DFPT on such grids is prohibitive.
4. **(VI.A.1)** Bloch states of an isolated ("composite") band manifold are mixed by a unitary $U_{mn\mathbf k}$ and Fourier transformed into Wannier functions (Eq. 175, inverse Eq. 176). Maximal localization fixes the gauge. MLWFs decay exponentially in insulators.
5. **(VI.A.2)** Define the e–ph matrix element in the Wannier representation (Eq. 177). The generalized Fourier transforms (Eqs. 178–179) connect it to $g_{mn\nu}(\mathbf k,\mathbf q)$.
6. A bound shows that $g(\mathbf R_p,\mathbf R_{p'})$ decays in $\mathbf R_p$ like the MLWFs, and in $\mathbf R_{p'}$ like the *screened* potential of a displaced atom. That potential is at least quadrupolar ($R^{-3}$) in nonpolar insulators. In metals it shows Friedel oscillations, which smearing suppresses in practice.
7. **Algorithm:** DFPT on a coarse grid → MLWFs ($U_{mn\mathbf k}$) → Eq. (179) to real space → truncate to the Wigner–Seitz supercell → Eq. (178) back to fine grids, with $U$ on the fine grid from Wannier interpolation of the bands. Figs. 5–6 (diamond) show the decay and the accuracy.
8. **(VI.A.3)** In polar materials the dominant part of $\partial V^{\rm KS}/\partial\tau$ is a dipole ($R^{-2}$), so $g\propto|\mathbf q|^{-1}$ as $\mathbf q\to0$ (the Fröhlich singularity) and interpolation fails. The fix is to split $g=g^{\mathcal S}+g^{\mathcal L}$, with $g^{\mathcal L}$ given analytically by Eq. (180) from $Z^*_\kappa$ and $\epsilon^\infty$. Subtract on the coarse grid, interpolate $g^{\mathcal S}$, and add $g^{\mathcal L}$ back on the fine grid. The overlap factor is interpolated as $[U_{\mathbf k+\mathbf q}U^\dagger_{\mathbf k}]_{mn}$. Fig. 7 (anatase TiO$_2$) shows the result.
9. **(VI.B)** For metals, expand $\mathbf k$-dependent quantities in Fermi-surface harmonics (Eqs. 181–183), so that BZ sums become energy integrals. The Helmholtz FSHs of Eq. (184) form a complete basis.
10. **(VII)** The nonadiabatic phonon self-energy (Eq. 145, with $g^{\rm b}\to g$) shifts and broadens phonons. In graphene the Dirac cone allows "virtual" transitions with $\mathbf q=0$ and energies comparable to $\hbar\omega_{E_{2g}}$, which violates the adiabatic condition $|\varepsilon_{m\mathbf k+\mathbf q}-\varepsilon_{n\mathbf k}|\gg\hbar\omega$. The measured G-band frequency vs doping follows the nonadiabatic theory, not the adiabatic one (Fig. 8). Nanotubes behave the same way. Maksimov–Shulga showed that $\Pi^{\rm NA}$ matters only for $|\mathbf q|\sim\omega/v_F$.
11. Saitta et al. approximate $\hbar\,\mathrm{Re}\,\Pi^{\rm NA}_{\mathbf q=0}\simeq N_F\langle|g_{nn\nu}(\mathbf k,0)|^2\rangle$, the intraband Fermi-surface term. In graphite intercalation compounds this gives large corrections (up to about 300 cm$^{-1}$) and good agreement with experiment (Fig. 9). Mg and Ti get only a few cm$^{-1}$. In MgB$_2$ the theory overshoots (761 vs 600 cm$^{-1}$), which is attributed to finite electron lifetimes that wash out nonadiabatic effects.
12. Calandra–Profeta–Mauri use Wannier interpolation to get $\Pi^{\rm NA}$ over the whole BZ. For CaC$_6$ the correction is up to 7%, concentrated at small $\mathbf q$ (Fig. 10).
13. Linewidths are usually computed with Allen's double-delta formula (Eq. 185). It follows from Eq. (146) by neglecting $\hbar\omega$ in the delta and taking low $T$. It is positive definite and easy to converge, but loses the $T$ dependence. Measured widths range from about 1 meV (Nb) to about 30 meV (MgB$_2$). Anharmonicity is a separate source of shifts and widths, treated by the analogous many-body formalism (third and fourth derivatives of $U$).
14. **(VIII)** ARPES measures $A(\mathbf k,\omega)$ directly only for 2D or quasi-2D states, because only $\mathbf k_\parallel$ is conserved. Write the finite-$T$ Fan–Migdal self-energy in spectral form with the Eliashberg function (Eqs. 186–187).
15. With an isotropic $\alpha^2F$, phonon energies neglected next to electron energies, and particle–hole symmetry, one gets Eq. (188) for $|\mathrm{Im}\,\Sigma|$. Its step-like onset at the phonon energy explains the Be(0001) surface-state self-energy (Fig. 11). $\mathrm{Re}\,\Sigma$ vanishes far from $E_F$ under particle–hole symmetry. Without that symmetry (graphene, cuprates) it tends to a linear asymptote (Fig. 12).
16. The velocity renormalization is $\mathbf V=Z\mathbf v=\mathbf v/(1+\lambda)$, with $\lambda=-\hbar^{-1}\partial\mathrm{Re}\Sigma/\partial\omega$ (Eq. 189). Graphene has $\lambda\sim0.1$ and a kink at 150–200 meV. Extracting $\lambda$ from ARPES velocity ratios is risky, because the bare velocity is unknown.
17. The Debye–Waller self-energy is a static local potential (Eq. 190). In a homogeneous electron gas it is a constant. In general it is smooth within a band (so it can be neglected for kinks in metals) but differs across bands (so it matters for band gaps, Sec. IX).
18. **(VIII.A)** For a parabolic band, $m_{\rm ep}=m^*(1+\lambda)$. Grimvall's entropy formula (Eq. 191) with $\Sigma\simeq-\lambda\hbar\omega$ gives the low-$T$ heat capacity $C_e=\tfrac23\pi^2k_B^2N_F(1+\lambda)T$ (Eq. 192). At high $T$ the enhancement disappears.

## 3. Key equations (faithful transcriptions with the paper's numbering)

### 3.0 Conventions (state these on the page)

- **Units:** SI with $\hbar$ explicit. The Coulomb prefactor appears as $e^2/4\pi\varepsilon_0$ (Eq. 180), and $\varepsilon_0$ is the vacuum permittivity. **Careful:** in Eq. (180) the symbol $\epsilon^\infty$ (bold, a tensor) is the *relative* clamped-ion permittivity, while $\varepsilon_0$ is the vacuum permittivity. Elsewhere in the literature $\epsilon_0$ often denotes the static dielectric constant. The page should use $\epsilon_s$ (or $\epsilon^0$) for the static relative permittivity to avoid the clash.
- **Lattice and normalization (p. 9, Eq. 29):** $\psi_{n\mathbf k}(\mathbf r)=N_p^{-1/2}u_{n\mathbf k}(\mathbf r)e^{i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf r}$. $\psi$ is normalized in the Born–von Kármán (BvK) supercell of $N_p$ unit cells, and $u$ is normalized in one unit cell of volume $\Omega$. $\Omega_{\rm BZ}=(2\pi)^3/\Omega$. $N_p^{-1}\sum_{\mathbf k}\to\int d\mathbf k/\Omega_{\rm BZ}$. Lattice vectors are $\mathbf R_p$. The phonon BvK supercell can have $N_{p'}\neq N_p$ cells (Eq. 179).
- **Phonons (p. 9, Eqs. 21, 34, 35):** $l_{\mathbf q\nu}=[\hbar/(2M_0\omega_{\mathbf q\nu})]^{1/2}$ and $\Delta_{\mathbf q\nu}v^{\rm KS}=l_{\mathbf q\nu}\sum_{\kappa\alpha}(M_0/M_\kappa)^{1/2}e_{\kappa\alpha,\nu}(\mathbf q)\,\partial_{\kappa\alpha,\mathbf q}v^{\rm KS}$, with $\partial_{\kappa\alpha,\mathbf q}v^{\rm KS}=\sum_pe^{-i\mathbf q\cdot(\mathbf r-\mathbf R_p)}\,\partial V^{\rm KS}/\partial\tau_{\kappa\alpha}|_{\mathbf r-\mathbf R_p}$. In Sec. VI the combination $\mathrm u_{\kappa\alpha,\mathbf q\nu}=(\hbar/2M_\kappa\omega_{\mathbf q\nu})^{1/2}e_{\kappa\alpha,\nu}(\mathbf q)$ is used. It is a length: the mass-scaled zero-point amplitude of atom $\kappa$ in mode $\nu$.
- **e–ph matrix element [context, p. 9]:** $\hat H_{\rm ep}=N_p^{-1/2}\sum_{\mathbf k,\mathbf q,mn\nu}g_{mn\nu}(\mathbf k,\mathbf q)\hat c^\dagger_{m\mathbf k+\mathbf q}\hat c_{n\mathbf k}(\hat a_{\mathbf q\nu}+\hat a^\dagger_{-\mathbf q\nu})$ (37), with
  $$g_{mn\nu}(\mathbf k,\mathbf q)=\langle u_{m\mathbf k+\mathbf q}|\Delta_{\mathbf q\nu}v^{\rm KS}|u_{n\mathbf k}\rangle_{\rm uc}.\qquad(38)$$
  $g$ has units of energy and does not depend on $N_p$. The integral is over one unit cell ("uc"). "sc" denotes an integral over the BvK supercell.
- **Occupations (footnote 3, p. 25):** $f_{n\mathbf k}=f[(\varepsilon_{n\mathbf k}-\varepsilon_F)/k_BT]$ with $f(x)=1/(e^x+1)$. $n_{\mathbf q\nu}=n(\hbar\omega_{\mathbf q\nu}/k_BT)$ with $n(x)=1/(e^x-1)$. **In Eqs. (186)–(188) the electron energies $\varepsilon$ and $\hbar\omega$ are measured from the Fermi level.** This is implicit in writing $f(\varepsilon/k_BT)$.
- **Retarded self-energies:** $+i\eta$ (or $+i\hbar\eta$) in all denominators, $\eta\to0^+$, so $\mathrm{Im}\,\Sigma\le0$. Eq. (188) is written for $|\mathrm{Im}\,\Sigma|$.
- **Spin:** a factor 2 for spin is written explicitly in Eqs. (145), (146) and (185). $N(\varepsilon)$ and $N_F$ are per spin and per unit cell (Eq. 182 defines $N(\varepsilon)=N_p^{-1}\sum_{n\mathbf k}\delta(\varepsilon_{n\mathbf k}-\varepsilon)$ with spin omitted from $n$). This is consistent with the prefactor $\tfrac23\pi^2$ in Eq. (192). The paper does not say this explicitly.
- **Self-energy units:** $\Sigma$ is an energy. Its argument is a frequency $\omega$ in Eqs. (157), (164), (174), (189) and (191). Eqs. (186)–(188) contain $\hbar\omega$ explicitly.
- **Unit conversions for the page:** 1 meV = 8.0655 cm$^{-1}$; 1 cm$^{-1}$ = 0.12398 meV; 1 THz = 4.1357 meV; $e^2/4\pi\varepsilon_0=14.400$ eV·Å; $\hbar c=1973.3$ eV·Å.

### 3.1 V.B.6 (p. 32): cumulant expansion (unnumbered; details in G4)

$$G_{n\mathbf k}(t)=\frac{i}{\hbar}\exp\!\Big[-i\frac{\varepsilon_{n\mathbf k}}{\hbar}t+C_{n\mathbf k}(t)\Big].$$

$C_{n\mathbf k}(t)$ is the cumulant, obtained from a low-order self-energy (for example Fan–Migdal, Eq. 157). The exponential generates higher-order (beyond-Migdal) diagrams and gives polaron satellites (TiO$_2$: Verdi, Caruso and Giustino 2017). The prefactor $+i/\hbar$ (rather than $-i/\hbar$) matches the hole (occupied, $t<0$) branch measured in ARPES, given the convention $G=-(i/\hbar)\langle T\hat\psi\hat\psi^\dagger\rangle$. The paper does not state this.

### 3.2 VI.A.1 Maximally localized Wannier functions (p. 33)

$$\mathrm w_{mp}(\mathbf r)=N_p^{-1}\sum_{n\mathbf k}e^{i\mathbf k\cdot(\mathbf r-\mathbf R_p)}U_{nm\mathbf k}\,u_{n\mathbf k}(\mathbf r),\qquad(175)$$

- $n$ runs over a "composite" set of bands, isolated by gaps from all other bands. $U_{nm\mathbf k}$ is unitary in $(m,n)$. $\mathrm w_{mp}$ is Wannier function $m$ centred in cell $p$.
- Normalization: $\langle\mathrm w_{mp}|\mathrm w_{m'p'}\rangle_{\rm sc}=\delta_{mp,m'p'}$. Translation property: $\mathrm w_{mp}(\mathbf r)=\mathrm w_{m0}(\mathbf r-\mathbf R_p)$. Equivalent standard form: $\mathrm w_{mp}=N_p^{-1/2}\sum_{\mathbf k}e^{-i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf R_p}\sum_nU_{nm\mathbf k}\psi_{n\mathbf k}$.

$$u_{n\mathbf k}(\mathbf r)=\sum_{mp}e^{-i\mathbf k\cdot(\mathbf r-\mathbf R_p)}U^\dagger_{mn\mathbf k}\,\mathrm w_{mp}(\mathbf r).\qquad(176)$$

- This is the inverse, obtained from unitarity and $\sum_pe^{i(\mathbf k-\mathbf k')\cdot\mathbf R_p}=N_p\delta_{\mathbf k\mathbf k'}$ (Eq. A1). I checked it: it inverts (175) exactly.
- Gauge freedom: $U$ is arbitrary. $U_{nm,-\mathbf k}=U^*_{nm\mathbf k}$ gives real WFs. Marzari–Vanderbilt choose $U$ to minimize the spread, using the modern-theory-of-polarization position operator. For entangled bands (metals near $E_F$) the Souza–Marzari–Vanderbilt disentanglement is used.
- Localization (unnumbered): $|\mathrm w_{m0}(\mathbf r)|\sim|\mathbf r|^{-\alpha}\exp(-h|\mathbf r|)$ for large $|\mathbf r|$, with $\alpha,h>0$. This holds in insulators. It was proved in 1D by Kohn (1959a) and He–Vanderbilt (2001), and in 2D/3D by Brouder et al. (2007) under time-reversal symmetry. No exponential localization is expected in metals, but disentangled WFs are in practice highly localized. MLWFs are comparable in size to atomic orbitals, which makes Slater–Koster interpolation possible.

### 3.3 VI.A.2 Wannier interpolation of e–ph matrix elements (pp. 33–34)

$$g_{mn\kappa\alpha}(\mathbf R_p,\mathbf R_{p'})=\Big\langle\mathrm w_{m0}(\mathbf r)\Big|\frac{\partial V^{\rm KS}}{\partial\tau_{\kappa\alpha}}(\mathbf r-\mathbf R_{p'})\Big|\mathrm w_{n0}(\mathbf r-\mathbf R_p)\Big\rangle_{\rm sc},\qquad(177)$$

- This is the amplitude for an electron to hop from WF $n$ in cell $p$ to WF $m$ in cell 0, caused by displacing atom $\kappa$ of cell $p'$ along $\alpha$.
- Footnote 5: $g_{mn\kappa\alpha}(\mathbf R_p,\mathbf R_{p'})$ has units of energy/length, unlike Eq. (38). It differs from Giustino, Cohen and Louie (2007) by a factor $N_p$, which is "inconsequential".

$$g_{mn\nu}(\mathbf k,\mathbf q)=\sum_{pp'}e^{i(\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf R_p+\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf R_{p'})}\sum_{m'n'\kappa\alpha}U_{mm'\mathbf k+\mathbf q}\,g_{m'n'\kappa\alpha}(\mathbf R_p,\mathbf R_{p'})\,U^\dagger_{n'n\mathbf k}\,\mathrm u_{\kappa\alpha,\mathbf q\nu},\qquad(178)$$

with $\mathrm u_{\kappa\alpha,\mathbf q\nu}=(\hbar/2M_\kappa\omega_{\mathbf q\nu})^{1/2}e_{\kappa\alpha,\nu}(\mathbf q)$. It is obtained by inserting Eq. (176) into Eq. (38) and using Eqs. (34)–(35).

$$g_{mn\kappa\alpha}(\mathbf R_p,\mathbf R_{p'})=\frac{1}{N_pN_{p'}}\sum_{\mathbf k,\mathbf q}e^{-i(\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf R_p+\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf R_{p'})}\sum_{m'n'\nu}\mathrm u^{-1}_{\kappa\alpha,\mathbf q\nu}\,U^\dagger_{mm'\mathbf k+\mathbf q}\,g_{m'n'\nu}(\mathbf k,\mathbf q)\,U_{n'n\mathbf k},\qquad(179)$$

with $\mathrm u^{-1}_{\kappa\alpha,\mathbf q\nu}=(\hbar/2M_\kappa\omega_{\mathbf q\nu})^{-1/2}e^*_{\kappa\alpha,\nu}(\mathbf q)$.

- Eqs. (178)–(179) are a "generalized Fourier transform" between the Bloch (coarse-grid) and Wannier (real-space) representations. If $g(\mathbf R_p,\mathbf R_{p'})$ decays quickly, a few real-space elements determine $g(\mathbf k,\mathbf q)$ everywhere.

**Decay bound (unnumbered, p. 34):**
$$|g_{mn\kappa\alpha}(\mathbf R_p,\mathbf R_{p'})|\le\int_{\rm sc}d\mathbf r\,|\mathrm w^*_{m0}(\mathbf r)\mathrm w_{n0}(\mathbf r-\mathbf R_p)|\times\int_{\rm sc}d\mathbf r\,|\partial V^{\rm KS}/\partial\tau_{\kappa\alpha}(\mathbf r-\mathbf R_{p'})|.$$

- First factor: $g$ decays in $\mathbf R_p$ at least as fast as the MLWF overlap.
- Second factor (worst case $\mathbf R_p=0$): $|g(0,\mathbf R_{p'})|$ decays like the **screened** dipole potential of the displaced atom.
  - Nonpolar semiconductors and insulators: the analytic properties of the dielectric matrix (Pick, Cohen and Martin 1970) make it decay at least like a quadrupole, $|\mathbf R_{p'}|^{-3}$. All reciprocal-space elements are then finite as $\mathbf q\to0$ (Vogl 1976).
  - Metals: Fermi-surface nesting gives Friedel oscillations decaying as $|\mathbf R_{p'}|^{-4}$ (the text's claim; see Source issues). These are the real-space counterpart of Kohn anomalies. In practice Fermi–Dirac smearing turns them into Yukawa-type exponential decay.
  - Polar materials: dipole decay, $|\mathbf R_{p'}|^{-2}$ (Sec. VI.A.3).

**Algorithm (p. 34):**
1. DFPT matrix elements on a coarse $\mathbf k$ and $\mathbf q$ grid (Sec. III.B.3).
2. MLWFs, giving $U_{mn\mathbf k}$.
3. Eq. (179) to real space.
4. Neglect elements outside the Wigner–Seitz supercell of the coarse grid.
5. Eq. (178) to fine grids, with $U_{mn\mathbf k}$ on the fine grid from Wannier interpolation of the bands (Souza, Marzari and Vanderbilt 2001).

This is computationally inexpensive and yields millions of matrix elements (EPW: Noffsinger et al. 2010; Poncé et al. 2016). It is the same strategy as interpolating phonon dispersions from interatomic force constants (Gonze and Lee 1997).

### 3.4 VI.A.3 Electron–phonon matrix elements in polar materials (p. 35)

In polar materials (nonzero Born effective charges) the dominant part of $\partial V^{\rm KS}/\partial\tau$ in Eq. (177) is a dipole, $\propto|\mathbf R_{p'}|^{-2}$. Reciprocal-space elements then diverge as $|\mathbf q|^{-1}$ for $\mathbf q\to0$ and cannot be interpolated. This singularity is the "Fröhlich electron–phonon coupling" (Fröhlich 1954). Split $g_{mn\nu}=g^{\mathcal S}_{mn\nu}+g^{\mathcal L}_{mn\nu}$ (short range plus long range), with

$$g^{\mathcal L}_{mn\nu}(\mathbf k,\mathbf q)=i\,\frac{4\pi}{\Omega}\frac{e^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\sum_\kappa\Big(\frac{\hbar}{2N_pM_\kappa\omega_{\mathbf q\nu}}\Big)^{1/2}\sum_{\mathbf G\neq-\mathbf q}\frac{(\mathbf q+\mathbf G)\cdot\mathbf Z^*_\kappa\cdot\mathbf e_{\kappa\nu}(\mathbf q)}{(\mathbf q+\mathbf G)\cdot\boldsymbol\epsilon^\infty\cdot(\mathbf q+\mathbf G)}\times\langle\psi_{m\mathbf k+\mathbf q}|e^{i(\mathbf q+\mathbf G)\cdot(\mathbf r-\boldsymbol\tau_\kappa)}|\psi_{n\mathbf k}\rangle_{\rm sc}.\qquad(180)$$

- $\mathbf Z^*_\kappa$ is the Born effective charge tensor of atom $\kappa$ (dimensionless, in units of $e$). $\boldsymbol\epsilon^\infty$ is the electronic (clamped-nuclei, high-frequency) relative permittivity tensor. $\mathbf e_{\kappa\nu}(\mathbf q)$ is the phonon eigenvector, $\boldsymbol\tau_\kappa$ is the atom position in the cell, $\mathbf G$ a reciprocal lattice vector, and $\Omega$ the unit-cell volume.
- The $\mathbf G=-\mathbf q$ (i.e. $\mathbf q+\mathbf G=0$) term is excluded.
- The derivation is standard electrostatics (Verdi and Giustino 2015): the potential of all the Born charges displaced according to mode $\nu$, i.e. a lattice of dipoles $e\mathbf Z^*_\kappa\cdot\Delta\boldsymbol\tau_\kappa$ modulated by $e^{i\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf R_p}$ and screened by $\boldsymbol\epsilon^\infty$. The kernel $4\pi/[\Omega(\mathbf q+\mathbf G)\cdot\boldsymbol\epsilon^\infty\cdot(\mathbf q+\mathbf G)]$ is the Fourier transform of the *instantaneous, longitudinal* screened Coulomb interaction.
- It generalizes Fröhlich's model to anisotropic crystals with multiple phonon modes. It can also be derived from the analytic properties of the dielectric matrix (Pick, Cohen and Martin 1970; Vogl 1976). It is analogous to the nonanalytic term used for LO–TO splitting (Gonze and Lee 1997).
- **Practical scheme:**
  1. Subtract Eq. (180) from the coarse-grid DFPT $g$.
  2. Interpolate the smooth remainder $g^{\mathcal S}$.
  3. Add Eq. (180) back on the fine grid.

  The overlap is interpolated for small $\mathbf q+\mathbf G$ as $\langle\psi_{m\mathbf k+\mathbf q}|e^{i(\mathbf q+\mathbf G)\cdot\mathbf r}|\psi_{n\mathbf k}\rangle_{\rm sc}=[U_{\mathbf k+\mathbf q}U^\dagger_{\mathbf k}]_{mn}$, which tends to $\delta_{mn}$ as $\mathbf q+\mathbf G\to0$. The long-range coupling is therefore intraband and forward-scattering dominated.
- Footnote 7: Eq. (4) of Verdi and Giustino (2015) lacks the factor $e^{-i(\mathbf q+\mathbf G)\cdot\boldsymbol\tau_\kappa}$, which is needed for acoustic modes near $\mathbf q=0$. In practice the $\mathbf G$ sum is restricted to small $|\mathbf q+\mathbf G|$ by a cutoff factor $e^{-a|\mathbf q+\mathbf G|^2}$. Results do not depend on $a$, because whatever is cut off is short-ranged and ends up in $g^{\mathcal S}$.
- **Reduction to Fröhlich (derived here, consistent with Verdi–Giustino 2015).** Take a cubic diatomic crystal with isotropic $\mathbf Z^*_{1}=-\mathbf Z^*_2=Z^*\mathbb 1$, $\boldsymbol\epsilon^\infty=\epsilon^\infty\mathbb 1$, the LO mode $\mathbf e_{1}=\sqrt{M_2/(M_1+M_2)}\hat{\mathbf q}$, $\mathbf e_2=-\sqrt{M_1/(M_1+M_2)}\hat{\mathbf q}$, keep only $\mathbf G=0$, and use the Lyddane–Sachs–Teller (LST) relation $\omega_{\rm LO}^2-\omega_{\rm TO}^2=\frac{4\pi}{\Omega}\frac{e^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{Z^{*2}}{\mu\epsilon^\infty}=\omega_{\rm LO}^2(1-\epsilon^\infty/\epsilon_s)$ with $\mu=M_1M_2/(M_1+M_2)$. Then
  $$g^{\mathcal L}(\mathbf q)=\frac{i}{|\mathbf q|}\Big[\frac{e^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{4\pi}{N_p\Omega}\frac{\hbar\omega_{\rm LO}}{2}\Big(\frac{1}{\epsilon^\infty}-\frac{1}{\epsilon_s}\Big)\Big]^{1/2}\delta_{mn}.$$
  This is the classic Fröhlich vertex. The coupling strength is set by $1/\epsilon^\infty-1/\epsilon_s$, the ionic part of the screening. Drop $N_p$ to match the normalization of Eq. (38); see Source issues.
- **Context, not written in the review: Gonze–Lee nonanalytic dynamical matrix** (the "LO–TO" analog of Eq. 180):
  $$\tilde C^{\rm NA}_{\kappa\alpha,\kappa'\beta}(\mathbf q\to0)=\frac{4\pi}{\Omega}\frac{e^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{(\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf Z^*_\kappa)_\alpha(\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf Z^*_{\kappa'})_\beta}{\mathbf q\cdot\boldsymbol\epsilon^\infty\cdot\mathbf q}.$$
  The same $Z^*$, $\epsilon^\infty$ and the same direction-dependent but $|\mathbf q|$-independent kernel are responsible for the LO–TO splitting.

### 3.5 VI.B Fermi-surface harmonics (p. 36)

$$A_{n\mathbf k}=\sum_LA_L(\varepsilon_{n\mathbf k})\Phi_L(\mathbf k),\qquad(181)$$
$$N_p^{-1}\sum_{n\mathbf k}\delta(\varepsilon_{n\mathbf k}-\varepsilon)\Phi_L(\mathbf k)\Phi_{L'}(\mathbf k)=N(\varepsilon)\delta_{LL'},\qquad(182)$$
with $N(\varepsilon)=N_p^{-1}\sum_{n\mathbf k}\delta(\varepsilon_{n\mathbf k}-\varepsilon)$ the density of states (DOS).
$$A_L(\varepsilon)=N(\varepsilon)^{-1}N_p^{-1}\sum_{n\mathbf k}\delta(\varepsilon_{n\mathbf k}-\varepsilon)\Phi_L(\mathbf k)A_{n\mathbf k}.\qquad(183)$$

- Any band- and $\mathbf k$-dependent quantity is split into an energy part $A_L(\varepsilon)$ and a "direction on the constant-energy surface" part $\Phi_L(\mathbf k)$, orthonormal on each constant-energy surface with weight $\delta(\varepsilon_{n\mathbf k}-\varepsilon)$.
- Allen showed that the linear system $A_{n\mathbf k}=N_p^{-1}\sum_{n'\mathbf k'}M_{n\mathbf k,n'\mathbf k'}B_{n'\mathbf k'}$ becomes $A_L(\varepsilon)=\sum_{L'}\int d\varepsilon'N(\varepsilon')M_{LL'}(\varepsilon,\varepsilon')B_{L'}(\varepsilon')$. This is useful for the Boltzmann transport equation (Sec. X) and the anisotropic Eliashberg equations (Sec. XI.B). If a few harmonics suffice, the $\mathbf k$ integrals are absorbed into the expansion coefficients.
- Allen's original $\Phi_L$ were polynomials in the band velocities, with completeness not established. Eiguren and Gurtubay (2014) use eigenstates of a modified Helmholtz equation:
$$|\mathbf v_{\mathbf k}|\nabla^2_{\mathbf k}\Phi_L(\mathbf k)=\omega_L\Phi_L(\mathbf k),\qquad(184)$$
with $\mathbf v_{\mathbf k}=\hbar^{-1}\nabla_{\mathbf k}\varepsilon_{n\mathbf k}$ the band velocity on the Fermi surface and $\omega_L$ the eigenvalue. This basis is complete, and $L$ labels the Helmholtz eigenstates. It has been demonstrated for Cu, Li and MgB$_2$, and applied to the YBa$_2$Cu$_3$O$_7$ photoemission kink (Heid et al. 2008) and the Seebeck coefficient of Li (Xu and Verstraete 2014).
- Pedagogical limit: for a circular 2D Fermi line with constant $|\mathbf v|$, the Laplacian restricted to the line is $k_F^{-2}\partial_\theta^2$. The Helmholtz FSHs are then $\cos L\theta,\sin L\theta$ with $\omega_L=-|\mathbf v|L^2/k_F^2$, i.e. ordinary angular Fourier harmonics.

### 3.6 VII. Nonadiabatic phonons: context equations (pp. 23–25)

**[context, p. 23]** For nondegenerate modes, the complex poles $\tilde\Omega_{\mathbf q\nu}=\Omega_{\mathbf q\nu}-i\gamma_{\mathbf q\nu}$ obey
$$\tilde\Omega_{\mathbf q\nu}^2=\omega_{\mathbf q\nu}^2+2\omega_{\mathbf q\nu}\Pi^{\rm NA}_{\mathbf q\nu\nu}(\tilde\Omega_{\mathbf q\nu}),\qquad(137)$$
$$\gamma_{\mathbf q\nu}=-\frac{\omega_{\mathbf q\nu}}{\Omega_{\mathbf q\nu}}\mathrm{Im}\,\Pi^{\rm NA}_{\mathbf q\nu\nu}(\Omega_{\mathbf q\nu}-i\gamma_{\mathbf q\nu}),\qquad(138)$$
$$\Omega_{\mathbf q\nu}^2=\omega_{\mathbf q\nu}^2+\gamma_{\mathbf q\nu}^2+2\omega_{\mathbf q\nu}\mathrm{Re}\,\Pi^{\rm NA}_{\mathbf q\nu\nu}(\Omega_{\mathbf q\nu}-i\gamma_{\mathbf q\nu}).\qquad(139)$$
For small corrections, $\gamma\simeq-\mathrm{Im}\,\Pi^{\rm NA}(\omega)$, $\Omega\simeq\omega+\mathrm{Re}\,\Pi^{\rm NA}(\omega)$, and the phonon lifetime is $\tau^{\rm ph}_{\mathbf q\nu}=(2\gamma_{\mathbf q\nu})^{-1}$. Here $\omega_{\mathbf q\nu}$ is the adiabatic (Born–Oppenheimer) frequency. **$\gamma$ is therefore a half width at half maximum (HWHM) in angular frequency.**

**[context, p. 25]** The nonadiabatic phonon self-energy in the RPA, with vertex $\Gamma=1$, Kohn–Sham $G_0$ and frequency-independent couplings:
$$\hbar\Pi^{\rm NA}_{\mathbf q\nu\nu'}(\omega)=2\sum_{mn}\int\frac{d\mathbf k}{\Omega_{\rm BZ}}g^{\rm b}_{mn\nu}(\mathbf k,\mathbf q)g^*_{mn\nu'}(\mathbf k,\mathbf q)\Big[\frac{f_{m\mathbf k+\mathbf q}-f_{n\mathbf k}}{\varepsilon_{m\mathbf k+\mathbf q}-\varepsilon_{n\mathbf k}-\hbar(\omega+i\eta)}-\frac{f_{m\mathbf k+\mathbf q}-f_{n\mathbf k}}{\varepsilon_{m\mathbf k+\mathbf q}-\varepsilon_{n\mathbf k}}\Big].\qquad(145)$$
The first term is the dynamical response and the second subtracts the static one, already contained in the adiabatic $\omega_{\mathbf q\nu}$. One vertex is bare ($g^{\rm b}$, from the variation of the ionic pseudopotential only) and one is screened ($g$). Footnote 8: all works reviewed in Sec. VII use $g^{\rm b}\to g$.
$$\frac{1}{\tau^{\rm ph}_{\mathbf q\nu}}=\frac{2\pi}{\hbar}2\sum_{mn}\int\frac{d\mathbf k}{\Omega_{\rm BZ}}|g_{mn\nu}(\mathbf k,\mathbf q)|^2(f_{n\mathbf k}-f_{m\mathbf k+\mathbf q})\,\delta(\varepsilon_{m\mathbf k+\mathbf q}-\varepsilon_{n\mathbf k}-\hbar\omega_{\mathbf q\nu}).\qquad(146)$$

### 3.7 VII. Equations in the range (pp. 36–38)

**Adiabatic condition (p. 36):** $|\varepsilon_{m\mathbf k+\mathbf q}-\varepsilon_{n\mathbf k}|\gg\hbar\omega_{\mathbf q\nu}$. This fails in zero-gap graphene for $|\mathbf q|=0$ near the Dirac points, and for any metal at small $\mathbf q$.

**Maksimov–Shulga criterion (p. 37):** for metals with linear bands crossing $E_F$, $\Pi^{\rm NA}$ is significant only for $|\mathbf q|\sim\omega/v_F$, where $\omega$ is the phonon frequency and $v_F$ the Fermi velocity.

**Saitta et al. (2008) approximation (unnumbered, p. 37):**
$$\hbar\,\mathrm{Re}\,\Pi^{\rm NA}_{\mathbf q=0,\nu\nu}\simeq N_F\langle|g_{nn\nu}(\mathbf k,\mathbf q=0)|^2\rangle_{\rm BZ}.$$
It is obtained from Eq. (145) by $g^{\rm b}\to g$ and by dropping interband ($m\neq n$) terms. **Why it works (derivation for the page):** at $\mathbf q=0$ exactly and $m=n$, the dynamical term is $(f_{n\mathbf k}-f_{n\mathbf k})/(0-\hbar\omega)=0$. The static term, taken as $\mathbf q\to0$, is $-(f_{n\mathbf k+\mathbf q}-f_{n\mathbf k})/(\varepsilon_{n\mathbf k+\mathbf q}-\varepsilon_{n\mathbf k})\to-\partial f/\partial\varepsilon=\delta(\varepsilon_{n\mathbf k}-\varepsilon_F)$. The bracket is therefore $+\delta(\varepsilon_{n\mathbf k}-\varepsilon_F)$, a Fermi-surface average, and $\Pi^{\rm NA}>0$: the phonon stiffens. The adiabatic phonon contains a Fermi-surface softening, obtained by taking $\omega\to0$ first and then $\mathbf q\to0$. A fast vibration ($\omega\gg v_Fq$) cannot rearrange the Fermi sea, so the softening is absent and the nonadiabatic frequency is higher. The whole of Sec. VII is the **non-commuting limits $\lim_{\mathbf q\to0}\lim_{\omega\to0}\neq\lim_{\omega\to0}\lim_{\mathbf q\to0}$** of the intraband Lindhard function.

**Allen's double-delta linewidth:**
$$\frac{\gamma_{\mathbf q\nu}}{\pi\omega_{\mathbf q\nu}}\simeq2\sum_{mn}\int\frac{d\mathbf k}{\Omega_{\rm BZ}}|g_{mn\nu}(\mathbf k,\mathbf q)|^2\delta(\varepsilon_{n\mathbf k}-\varepsilon_F)\delta(\varepsilon_{m\mathbf k+\mathbf q}-\varepsilon_F).\qquad(185)$$

- It is obtained from Eq. (146) by neglecting $\hbar\omega_{\mathbf q\nu}$ inside the delta function and taking the low-$T$ limit (Allen 1972b). Derivation: $f(\varepsilon)-f(\varepsilon+\hbar\omega)\simeq\hbar\omega\,\delta(\varepsilon-\varepsilon_F)$, so $1/\tau^{\rm ph}\simeq2\pi\omega\cdot2\sum\int|g|^2\delta\delta$. With $\tau^{\rm ph}=(2\gamma)^{-1}$ (p. 23) this gives exactly Eq. (185). Eq. (185) is internally consistent with $\gamma$ = HWHM $=1/(2\tau^{\rm ph})$.
- "Oddly enough, this is a sort of adiabatic approximation to the nonadiabatic theory." Advantages: positive definite and easier to converge than Eq. (146). Disadvantages: no temperature dependence, and no fine features on the scale of a phonon energy. It is implemented in Quantum ESPRESSO and ABINIT and used routinely.
- It is the input to the mode coupling $\lambda_{\mathbf q\nu}=\gamma_{\mathbf q\nu}/(\pi N_F\omega_{\mathbf q\nu}^2)$ used in superconductivity (Sec. XI). That formula is not written in this range.

### 3.8 VIII. Photoemission: spectral representation of the Fan–Migdal self-energy (pp. 38–39)

**[context, pp. 27–28]** The zero-$T$ Fan–Migdal self-energy:
$$\Sigma^{\rm FM}_{nn'\mathbf k}(\omega)=\frac1\hbar\sum_{m\nu}\int\frac{d\mathbf q}{\Omega_{\rm BZ}}g^*_{mn\nu}(\mathbf k,\mathbf q)g_{mn'\nu}(\mathbf k,\mathbf q)\Big[\frac{1-f_{m\mathbf k+\mathbf q}}{\omega-\varepsilon_{m\mathbf k+\mathbf q}/\hbar-\omega_{\mathbf q\nu}+i\eta}+\frac{f_{m\mathbf k+\mathbf q}}{\omega-\varepsilon_{m\mathbf k+\mathbf q}/\hbar+\omega_{\mathbf q\nu}-i\eta}\Big].\qquad(157)$$
At finite $T$ and retarded, the brackets become $\big[\frac{1-f+n}{\cdots+i\eta}+\frac{f+n}{\cdots+i\eta}\big]$ (Eq. 158).

**In the range:**
$$\Sigma^{\rm FM}_{nn\mathbf k}(\omega,T)=\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}d\varepsilon\int_0^\infty d\varepsilon'\,\alpha^2F_{n\mathbf k}(\varepsilon,\varepsilon')\Big[\frac{1-f(\varepsilon/k_BT)+n(\varepsilon'/k_BT)}{\hbar\omega-\varepsilon-\varepsilon'+i\hbar\eta}+\frac{f(\varepsilon/k_BT)+n(\varepsilon'/k_BT)}{\hbar\omega-\varepsilon+\varepsilon'+i\hbar\eta}\Big],\qquad(186)$$
$$\alpha^2F_{n\mathbf k}(\varepsilon,\varepsilon')=\sum_{m\nu}\int\frac{d\mathbf q}{\Omega_{\rm BZ}}|g_{nm\nu}(\mathbf k,\mathbf q)|^2\,\delta(\varepsilon-\varepsilon_{m\mathbf k+\mathbf q})\,\delta(\varepsilon'-\hbar\omega_{\mathbf q\nu}).\qquad(187)$$

- $\varepsilon$ is the intermediate electron energy (from $E_F$) and $\varepsilon'$ the phonon energy. The first term is phonon emission by an electron (or absorption via $n$). The second involves occupied intermediate states (holes).
- $\alpha^2F$ is dimensionless, positive, temperature independent, and contains all the material-specific parameters. Physically it is proportional to the scattering rate from $|n\mathbf k\rangle$ to any state at energy $\varepsilon$ by emitting or absorbing one phonon of energy $\varepsilon'$. Many variants exist in the literature (Grimvall 1981).

**Eiguren et al. (2003) approximations:**
- (i) Isotropic average: $\alpha^2F_n(\varepsilon,\varepsilon')=\int d\mathbf k\,\delta(\varepsilon_{n\mathbf k}-\varepsilon)\,\alpha^2F_{n\mathbf k}(\varepsilon,\varepsilon')/\int d\mathbf k\,\delta(\varepsilon_{n\mathbf k}-\varepsilon)$.
- (ii) Phonon energies neglected next to electron energies (adiabatic).
- (iii) Particle–hole symmetry.

With these,
$$|\mathrm{Im}\,\Sigma^{\rm FM}_n(\omega,T)|=\pi\int_0^\infty d\varepsilon'\,\alpha^2F_n(\hbar\omega,\varepsilon')\{1+2n(\varepsilon'/k_BT)+f[(\hbar\omega+\varepsilon')/k_BT]-f[(\hbar\omega-\varepsilon')/k_BT]\}.\qquad(188)$$

- Derivation check: take $\mathrm{Im}$ of (186) with $1/(x+i0)\to-i\pi\delta(x)$, evaluate $\alpha^2F$ at $\varepsilon\approx\hbar\omega$, and use $\varepsilon=\hbar\omega\mp\varepsilon'$ in the two terms. This gives $(1-f(\hbar\omega-\varepsilon')+n)+(f(\hbar\omega+\varepsilon')+n)$, which is exactly the bracket of (188). I verified this.
- **$T=0$ limit:** $n=0$ and $f(x)=\theta(-x)$, so the bracket is $\theta(|\hbar\omega|-\varepsilon')$ for either sign of $\omega$, and
  $$|\mathrm{Im}\,\Sigma(\omega,0)|=\pi\int_0^{|\hbar\omega|}d\varepsilon'\,\alpha^2F(\varepsilon').$$
  This is a cumulative integral: it rises from 0 at $E_F$ and saturates beyond $\omega_{\max}$.
- **Einstein replacement (p. 39):** with $\alpha^2F\propto\delta(\varepsilon'-\hbar\omega_{\rm ph})$, the hole self-energy is $\propto f[(\hbar\omega+\hbar\omega_{\rm ph})/k_BT]$. At $T=0$ this is a step function with onset at $-\hbar\omega_{\rm ph}$.
- The real part vanishes for $|\omega|\gg\omega_{\max}$, the largest phonon frequency. This is a consequence of particle–hole symmetry (approximation iii).

### 3.9 VIII. Velocity renormalization, $\lambda$, Debye–Waller (pp. 40–41)

**Velocity renormalization (unnumbered):** with $\mathbf v_{n\mathbf k}=\hbar^{-1}\nabla_{\mathbf k}\varepsilon_{n\mathbf k}$ (DFT) and $\mathbf V_{n\mathbf k}=\hbar^{-1}\nabla_{\mathbf k}E_{n\mathbf k}$ (renormalized), Eq. (164) with $\Gamma_{n\mathbf k}=0$ gives
$$\mathbf V_{n\mathbf k}=Z_{n\mathbf k}\mathbf v_{n\mathbf k}=\mathbf v_{n\mathbf k}/(1+\lambda_{n\mathbf k}),$$
$$\lambda_{n\mathbf k}=Z^{-1}_{n\mathbf k}-1=-\hbar^{-1}\partial\,\mathrm{Re}\,\Sigma_{nn\mathbf k}(\omega)/\partial\omega|_{\omega=E_{n\mathbf k}/\hbar}.\qquad(189)$$

- $Z_{n\mathbf k}$ is the quasiparticle strength of Eq. (174): $Z=[1-\hbar^{-1}\partial\mathrm{Re}\Sigma/\partial\omega]^{-1}$. $\lambda_{n\mathbf k}$ is the "mass enhancement parameter" or "electron–phonon coupling strength" (Grimvall 1981).
- Hidden assumption: differentiating $E=\varepsilon+\mathrm{Re}\Sigma(E)$ with respect to $\mathbf k$ gives $\mathbf V=\mathbf v/(1-\hbar^{-1}\partial_\omega\mathrm{Re}\Sigma)$ **only if the explicit $\mathbf k$ dependence of $\Sigma$ is neglected**. That is standard for e–ph (Migdal), because $\Sigma$ varies fast in $\omega$ (on the phonon scale) and slowly in $\mathbf k$.
- Consistency with $\alpha^2F$ (standard, not written here): at $T=0$ with particle–hole symmetry, $\mathrm{Re}\,\Sigma(E)=\int d\varepsilon'\alpha^2F(\varepsilon')\ln|(\varepsilon'-E)/(\varepsilon'+E)|$. Hence $-\partial_E\mathrm{Re}\Sigma|_0=2\int d\varepsilon'\alpha^2F(\varepsilon')/\varepsilon'\equiv\lambda$, the usual Eliashberg definition of $\lambda$.
- For graphene: kink visible between 150 and 200 meV, "not very pronounced, since in this case $\lambda_{n\mathbf k}\sim0.1$" (Park et al. 2009). This agrees with ARPES (Bostwick et al. 2007; Zhou et al. 2007).
- Caveat: experimentalists often take $\lambda$ from the ratio of band velocities above and below the kink. The bare velocity is unknown and must be fitted with *ad hoc* models. Near van Hove singularities this leads to significant overestimation (Park, Giustino, McChesney et al. 2008; Bianchi et al. 2010).

**Debye–Waller self-energy in metals (p. 41):** combining Eqs. (160) and (40),
$$\Sigma^{\rm DW}_{nn\mathbf k}=\langle u_{n\mathbf k}|V_{\rm DW}|u_{n\mathbf k}\rangle_{\rm uc},\qquad V_{\rm DW}(\mathbf r)=\Omega^{-1}_{\rm BZ}\sum_\nu\int d\mathbf q\,(n_{\mathbf q\nu}+1/2)\,\Delta_{\mathbf q\nu}\Delta_{-\mathbf q\nu}v^{\rm KS}(\mathbf r).\qquad(190)$$

- I checked consistency: with $g^{\rm DW}_{nn\nu\nu}(\mathbf k,\mathbf q,-\mathbf q)=\tfrac12\langle u|\Delta_{\mathbf q\nu}\Delta_{-\mathbf q\nu}v^{\rm KS}|u\rangle$ (Eq. 40) and the finite-$T$ factor $(2n+1)$ (Eq. 161), Eq. (190) follows exactly.
- $V_{\rm DW}$ is a **static local potential**: a thermal and zero-point average of the second derivative of the crystal potential. The earliest DW calculations modified the ionic pseudopotentials directly (Antončík 1955; Walter et al. 1970).
- Homogeneous electron gas: $|u_{n\mathbf k}|^2=1/\Omega$, so $\Sigma^{\rm DW}$ is a $\mathbf k$-independent constant. Contrast Fan–Migdal, which has strong structure near $E_F$ (Fig. 4).
- Doped semiconductors: $\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf p$ theory shows $\Sigma^{\rm DW}$ is smooth within a band but varies strongly across *different* bands. This matters for temperature-dependent band gaps (Sec. IX.A.1).

### 3.10 VIII.A Electron mass enhancement in metals (p. 41)

- Parabolic band near $E_F$: $\varepsilon_{n\mathbf k}=\hbar\mathbf k_F\cdot\hbar(\mathbf k-\mathbf k_F)/m^*$ (linearized), so $\mathbf v_{n\mathbf k}=\hbar\mathbf k_F/m^*$.
- With $\mathbf V=\mathbf v/(1+\lambda)$ and $k_F$ unchanged (Luttinger theorem; see the caption of Fig. 4), the band mass becomes $m_{\rm ep}=m^*(1+\lambda_{n\mathbf k})$.
- This ignores Debye–Waller and applies to metals with parabolic bands and to doped semiconductors near band extrema.

**Grimvall's entropy (relation to the Fan–Migdal self-energy):**
$$S_{\rm e}=\frac{N_Fk_B\hbar^3}{(k_BT)^2}\int_0^\infty\frac{\omega[\omega-\hbar^{-1}\mathrm{Re}\,\Sigma^{\rm FM}_{nn\mathbf k}(\omega,T)]}{\cosh^2(\hbar\omega/2k_BT)}\,d\omega.\qquad(191)$$

- Derived from the thermodynamic potential of the coupled e–ph system in terms of propagators and self-energies, neglecting terms of order $(m_e/M_0)^{1/2}$ and e–e interactions (Grimvall 1969, Appendix). It follows Eliashberg (1963), based on Luttinger–Ward (1960).
- Below the Debye temperature, $\cosh^{-2}(\hbar\omega/2k_BT)$ is nonvanishing only for $\hbar\omega\lesssim2k_BT$. In that range Eq. (189) gives $\Sigma^{\rm FM}_{nn\mathbf k}(\omega,T)\simeq-\lambda_{n\mathbf k}\hbar\omega$. Therefore
$$C_{\rm e}=T\frac{\partial S_{\rm e}}{\partial T}=\frac23\pi^2k_B^2N_F(1+\lambda_{n\mathbf k})T.\qquad(192)$$
- Check (done here): $\int_0^\infty x^2\,\mathrm{sech}^2x\,dx=\pi^2/12$ gives $S_{\rm e}=\tfrac{2\pi^2}{3}k_B^2N_F(1+\lambda)T$, which equals $C_{\rm e}$, consistent with (192). The prefactor shows $N_F$ is per spin. $\lambda=0$ recovers the free-electron (Sommerfeld) result.
- At high $T$ the full frequency-dependent $\Sigma^{\rm FM}$ must be used. The electronic heat capacity is then no longer renormalized by EPIs (Grimvall 1969, 1981). Early DFT comparisons: Dacorogna, Cohen and Lam (1985); Savrasov, Savrasov and Andersen (1994); Liu and Quong (1996).

### 3.11 Useful closed forms derived for the visualizations (not in the paper; all checked numerically)

For an isotropic $\alpha^2F(\varepsilon')$, a constant DOS and particle–hole symmetry (approximations i–iii), with energies $E=\hbar\omega$ measured from $E_F$:

- **Einstein:** $\alpha^2F(\varepsilon')=\frac{\lambda\hbar\omega_E}{2}\delta(\varepsilon'-\hbar\omega_E)$. **Debye:** $\alpha^2F(\varepsilon')=\lambda(\varepsilon'/\hbar\omega_D)^2\theta(\hbar\omega_D-\varepsilon')$. Both satisfy $\lambda=2\int\alpha^2F/\varepsilon'$.
- **$|\mathrm{Im}\,\Sigma|$:** from Eq. (188). For Einstein, $|\mathrm{Im}\Sigma(E,T)|=\frac{\pi\lambda\hbar\omega_E}{2}[1+2n(\hbar\omega_E)+f(E+\hbar\omega_E)-f(E-\hbar\omega_E)]$, which at $T=0$ is $\frac{\pi\lambda\hbar\omega_E}{2}\theta(|E|-\hbar\omega_E)$. For Debye at $T=0$: $\pi\lambda|E|^3/(3\hbar^2\omega_D^2)$ for $|E|<\hbar\omega_D$, and $\pi\lambda\hbar\omega_D/3$ above.
- **$\mathrm{Re}\,\Sigma$ at any $T$** (Kramers–Kronig partner of 188; I checked against direct principal-value integration of Eq. 186):
  $$\mathrm{Re}\,\Sigma(E,T)=\int_0^\infty d\varepsilon'\,\alpha^2F(\varepsilon')\Big\{\mathrm{Re}\,\psi\Big(\tfrac12+i\tfrac{\varepsilon'-E}{2\pi k_BT}\Big)-\mathrm{Re}\,\psi\Big(\tfrac12+i\tfrac{\varepsilon'+E}{2\pi k_BT}\Big)\Big\},$$
  where $\psi$ is the digamma function. As $T\to0$, $\mathrm{Re}\psi(\tfrac12+iy)\to\ln|y|$, giving $\int\alpha^2F\ln|(\varepsilon'-E)/(\varepsilon'+E)|$. For Einstein at $T=0$ this is $\frac{\lambda\hbar\omega_E}{2}\ln\big|\frac{\hbar\omega_E-E}{\hbar\omega_E+E}\big|$, with slope $-\lambda$ at $E=0$ and a log peak at $|E|=\hbar\omega_E$.
- **JS implementation of $\mathrm{Re}\,\psi(\tfrac12+iy)$:** use recurrence plus asymptotics with $N=8$. Set $w=(N+\tfrac12)+iy$ and $\psi(w)\approx\ln w-\frac{1}{2w}-\frac{1}{12w^2}+\frac{1}{120w^4}-\frac{1}{252w^6}$ (complex arithmetic). Then $\mathrm{Re}\,\psi(\tfrac12+iy)=\mathrm{Re}\,\psi(w)-\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\frac{k+\frac12}{(k+\frac12)^2+y^2}$. This reproduces $\psi(1/2)=-\gamma_E-2\ln2=-1.96351$ to $10^{-10}$. For a general $\alpha^2F$, discretize into 100–300 bins and sum the Einstein expression with weights $\alpha^2F(\varepsilon_i)\Delta\varepsilon$.
- **Heat capacity from (191):** in energy variables, $S_{\rm e}/(N_Fk_B)=(k_BT)^{-2}\int_0^\infty E[E-\mathrm{Re}\Sigma(E,T)]\,\mathrm{sech}^2(E/2k_BT)\,dE$. Then $C_{\rm e}=T\,\partial S_{\rm e}/\partial T$ by finite differences.
  - My numerical result for an Einstein spectrum with $\lambda=1$: $C_{\rm e}/C_{\rm e}^{(0)}=2.00$ at $k_BT/\hbar\omega_E=0.01$; 2.07 at 0.05; about 2.39 at 0.10–0.15 (overshoot); 1.87 at 0.2; 1.03 at 0.3; 0.73 at 0.5; 0.87 at 1.0; 0.96 at 2.0. Here $C^{(0)}_{\rm e}=\tfrac23\pi^2k_B^2N_FT$.
  - So the enhancement $1+\lambda$ holds only for $k_BT\lesssim0.05\hbar\omega_E$. It overshoots, dips below 1, and dies out at high $T$, as the text states. A Debye spectrum gives a milder overshoot.

## 4. Concepts and physical intuition

**(a) Why fine grids? Energy-scale mismatch.** Phonon energies (10–200 meV) are 10–100 times smaller than electronic band widths. The integrands of Eqs. (145), (146), (157) and (187) contain resonant denominators or delta functions selecting states within $\hbar\omega$ of each other, or within $\hbar\omega$ of $E_F$. Resolving a shell of thickness $\hbar\omega$ in $\mathbf k$ needs $\Delta k\lesssim\hbar\omega/(\hbar v)$. For $v\sim10^6$ m/s and $\hbar\omega=50$ meV, $\Delta k\sim0.008$ Å$^{-1}$, i.e. hundreds of points per BZ direction, or $10^6$–$10^7$ points in 3D. Standard DFT uses about $10^3$ points.

**(b) Wannier interpolation as "tight binding for the e–ph vertex".** In the Wannier basis an electron hops between localized orbitals, and a displaced atom modifies hopping amplitudes only nearby (Fig. 5: four orders of magnitude decay over about 12 Å in diamond). A coarse grid of $N_c$ points per direction captures real-space couplings out to $N_c/2$ cells. If the couplings have died off by then, the Fourier series reproduces $g(\mathbf k,\mathbf q)$ everywhere. This is the same logic as Slater–Koster band interpolation and as interatomic force constants for phonon dispersions. Figure 6 shows a $4^3$ grid already captures the main features in diamond, and $6^3$–$8^3$ are converged.

**(c) Polar materials: the long-range dipole breaks locality.** When an ion with Born charge $Z^*$ moves, it creates a dipole. A lattice of dipoles modulated with wave vector $\mathbf q$ produces a macroscopic longitudinal electric field $\propto\mathbf q(\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf p)/q^2$. Its potential is $\propto\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf p/q^2\sim1/q$. In real space the coupling decays as $1/R^2$, so no finite Wigner–Seitz cutoff can capture it. A Fourier series of a $1/q$ function has Gibbs-like errors near every $\Gamma$ point. The cure is to handle the *nonanalytic* part analytically from electrostatics: it depends only on $Z^*$, $\epsilon^\infty$ and the phonon eigenvectors, all available from DFPT. The same nonanalyticity makes the LO frequency at $\Gamma$ depend on the direction of approach, $\hat{\mathbf q}\cdot\mathbf Z^*\cdots$, i.e. the LO–TO splitting.

**(d) Fröhlich coupling strength and the factor $1/\epsilon^\infty-1/\epsilon_s$.** The electron feels the *difference* between screening by electrons alone and by electrons plus ions, i.e. the ionic polarization. This is the polar-optical-phonon contribution to the screened interaction $W$. Typical magnitudes in the Eq. (38) convention (computed here):
- GaAs ($\epsilon^\infty=10.9$, $\epsilon_s=12.9$, $\hbar\omega_{\rm LO}=36.2$ meV, $\Omega=45.2$ Å$^3$): $|g^{\mathcal L}|\,|\mathbf q|\approx0.032$ eV·Å, so $|g|\approx0.32$ eV at $q=0.1$ Å$^{-1}$.
- MgO ($\epsilon^\infty=2.96$, $\epsilon_s=9.8$, $\hbar\omega_{\rm LO}=89$ meV, $\Omega=18.7$ Å$^3$): $\approx0.32$ eV·Å, ten times larger.
- LiF ($\epsilon^\infty=1.93$, $\epsilon_s=9.0$, 82 meV, 16.4 Å$^3$): $\approx0.43$ eV·Å.
- Anatase TiO$_2$ (rough: $\epsilon^\infty\approx5.8$, $\epsilon_s\approx40$, highest LO $\approx105$ meV, primitive $\Omega\approx68$ Å$^3$): $\approx0.14$ eV·Å, i.e. about 700 meV at $q=0.2$ Å$^{-1}$. That is the scale of Fig. 7.
- LST check: GaAs $(292/268)^2=1.19\approx12.9/10.9$; MgO $(725/401)^2=3.27\approx9.8/2.96$.

**(e) Nonanalytic vs analytic: why nonpolar insulators are fine.** In a nonpolar insulator the screened potential of a displaced atom is at most quadrupolar ($R^{-3}$). The vertex is then finite and continuous at $\mathbf q\to0$, and Wannier interpolation works (Vogl 1976). Post-2017 note: dynamical quadrupoles produce a direction-dependent, $|\mathbf q|$-independent but nonanalytic term that is now also treated analytically (Brunin et al. 2020; Jhalani et al. 2020; Park et al. 2020). This is beyond the review, but it is the next term in the same multipole hierarchy.

**(f) Metals: Friedel oscillations and Kohn anomalies.** A sharp Fermi surface gives the static response a nonanalyticity at $|\mathbf q|=2k_F$, or more generally at nesting vectors. That produces real-space Friedel oscillations in $\partial V/\partial\tau$ and kinks in phonon dispersions (Kohn anomalies, Kohn 1959b). Smearing in DFT kills the long-range tail. The nesting is directly visible in Eq. (185): the double-delta integrand counts pairs of Fermi-surface points separated by $\mathbf q$. For a 2D circular Fermi surface, $\int\delta\delta\propto1/[q\sqrt{1-(q/2k_F)^2}]$, which is singular at $2k_F$. In 3D it is $\propto\theta(2k_F-q)/q$.

**(g) Adiabatic vs nonadiabatic: who is faster?** Born–Oppenheimer assumes electrons respond instantaneously (static response) to nuclear motion. That needs the electronic excitation energies coupled by the phonon to greatly exceed $\hbar\omega$. In a metal, intraband excitations at small $\mathbf q$ have energies $\sim\hbar v_Fq$. When $q\lesssim\omega/v_F$ they are *slower* than the phonon, and the static response is wrong.
- Graphene numbers: $\hbar v_F\approx6.6$–$7.2$ eV·Å, $\hbar\omega_G=196$ meV (1585 cm$^{-1}$), so $\omega/v_F\approx0.03$ Å$^{-1}$, about 2% of the BZ size. That is tiny, but it contains the Raman-active $\mathbf q=0$ mode.
- In doped graphene the dynamical frequency jumps by about 1% (≈5 cm$^{-1}$ over a few $10^{12}$ cm$^{-2}$, Fig. 8), whereas the adiabatic frequency barely moves.
- The doping dependence has two parts: (i) intraband (Fermi-surface) stiffening $\propto|\varepsilon_F|$, the Saitta term, since $N_F\propto|\varepsilon_F|$ in graphene; (ii) interband vertical transitions at energy $2|\varepsilon|$, which resonate with the phonon at $|\varepsilon_F|=\hbar\omega_G/2\approx0.1$ eV and give a logarithmic dip (the two minima in Fig. 8). For $2|\varepsilon_F|>\hbar\omega_G$ the resonant transitions are Pauli-blocked, so the phonon also narrows.
- Material numbers quoted:
  - Typical nonadiabatic renormalization: about 1% of the adiabatic frequency.
  - Graphite intercalation compounds: up to about 300 cm$^{-1}$ (KC$_8$: adiabatic about 1230 vs nonadiabatic about 1540 vs experiment about 1520 cm$^{-1}$; Fig. 9).
  - Mg, Ti: a few cm$^{-1}$.
  - MgB$_2$: nonadiabatic 761 cm$^{-1}$ vs experiment 600 cm$^{-1}$. The resolution proposed is that finite electron lifetimes (e–e, e–impurity, e–ph scattering; an imaginary part of Eq. 157 in the dressed $G$) wash out nonadiabatic effects (Maksimov–Shulga). When $1/\tau_{\rm el}\gtrsim\omega$ the electrons diffuse and partially follow the lattice.
  - CaC$_6$ dispersion: up to 7% at small $\mathbf q$ (Fig. 10: highest branch at $\Gamma$ ≈177 meV adiabatic vs ≈189.5 meV nonadiabatic).

**(h) Phonon linewidths.** In metals the e–ph linewidth is Landau damping: the phonon decays into electron–hole pairs across $E_F$. Eq. (185) says $\gamma/\omega\propto$ (Fermi-surface average of $|g|^2$) × (number of FS pairs connected by $\mathbf q$). Ranges quoted: about 1 meV (Nb) to about 30 meV (MgB$_2$, the $E_{2g}$ mode responsible for its superconductivity). There is reasonable agreement with neutron and Raman data. In semiconductors (no Fermi surface) e–ph linewidths of zone-centre optical phonons vanish at this level, and anharmonic three-phonon decay dominates.

**(i) Anharmonicity (p. 38).** Third- and higher-order terms in the expansion of $U$ couple the harmonic oscillators. Three-phonon processes (up- and down-conversion) reduce lifetimes. They are described by a field theory in complete analogy with Sec. IV (Cowley 1963). Third-order coefficients come from DFPT (Debernardi, Baroni and Molinari 1995, and others). Self-consistent phonon methods (Hooten 1955; Koehler 1966) are used when the harmonic approximation fails completely (Errea et al. 2011, 2014; Hellman et al. 2011, 2013; Monserrat et al. 2013).

**(j) ARPES kinks: what the self-energy does.** The quasiparticle peak sits where $\hbar\omega-\varepsilon_{\mathbf k}-\mathrm{Re}\Sigma(\omega)=0$. Its width is $|\mathrm{Im}\Sigma|$.
- Within $\hbar\omega_{\rm ph}$ of $E_F$: $\mathrm{Re}\Sigma\approx-\lambda\hbar\omega$, so the dispersion flattens by $1/(1+\lambda)$, i.e. the mass is enhanced. Also $\mathrm{Im}\Sigma\approx0$ at $T=0$, because a hole with less energy than a phonon cannot emit one.
- At $|\hbar\omega|=\hbar\omega_{\rm ph}$: $\mathrm{Re}\Sigma$ peaks (a log singularity for an Einstein mode) and $|\mathrm{Im}\Sigma|$ steps up to $\pi\lambda\hbar\omega_{\rm ph}/2$ (Einstein). The band "snaps back" to the bare dispersion, broadened. This is the kink.
- Be(0001) surface state (Fig. 11): emission threshold 40–80 meV, $|\mathrm{Im}\Sigma|$ saturates around 70–100 meV, $\mathrm{Re}\Sigma$ peaks around 50 meV at $E-E_F\approx-55$ meV (12 K), and $\lambda_0\approx0.9$ (inset). From the inset of Fig. 11(a), $\hbar v\approx3.6$ eV·Å.
- Temperature smears the step over $\sim k_BT$ and adds the $2n(\varepsilon')$ phonon-absorption term. Increasing $T$ from 12 to 40 K lowers the $\mathrm{Re}\Sigma$ peak from about 50 to about 34 meV.
- Without particle–hole symmetry (graphene; Fig. 12) $\mathrm{Re}\Sigma$ does not decay beyond $\omega_{\max}$ but tends to a *linear asymptote*. This is because the electron DOS ($\propto|\varepsilon-E_D|$) is asymmetric about $E_F$.

**(k) $\lambda$ connects many observables.** It appears in the velocity or mass (ARPES, de Haas–van Alphen), the specific heat ($C_e\propto1+\lambda$ at low $T$; the $\gamma$-coefficient), and superconductivity ($T_c$ via McMillan/Allen–Dynes, Sec. XI). Comparing the band-structure DOS with the measured specific-heat coefficient was the classic way to measure $\lambda$ in simple metals (Grimvall 1975).

**(l) Debye–Waller vs Fan–Migdal in metals.** Fan–Migdal is dynamical: it depends on $\omega$ through resonant denominators, which gives the kink. DW is static (Eq. 190), a thermal/zero-point "smearing" of the crystal potential. It shifts a band nearly rigidly, with no kink and no change of the Fermi velocity, which is why it is dropped in kink analyses. Across different bands (valence vs conduction) it does not cancel, which is why it is essential for band-gap renormalization (Sec. IX, Allen–Heine).

### Viz 1. Why Wannier interpolation works, and why polar materials need Eq. (180): a 1D Fourier-interpolation laboratory (Eqs. 178–180)

  - Toggle "polar" ($C=0$ or $C>0$).
  - Toggle "subtract long-range": interpolate $g-g^{\mathcal L}$, then add $g^{\mathcal L}$ back.
  - Cutoff $\alpha$ (0.1–2).
  - Optional "shift the coarse grid" to avoid sampling $q=0$ (when $q_j=0$ set $g^{\mathcal L}(0)=0$, as $\mathbf G\neq-\mathbf q$ prescribes).

  - Nonpolar: the error drops exponentially with $N_c$, and the log inset shows exponential decay.

- **Optional: the extraction pitfall.** Give the bare band curvature, $\varepsilon_k=\hbar v(k-k_F)+\hbar^2(k-k_F)^2/2m_b$, and show that the naive ratio of high- to low-energy slopes misestimates $\lambda$.
- Complements G4's Viz V3 (parabolic band plus Einstein mode, satellites). Merge if desired.

### Viz 5 (optional). Double-delta linewidth, nesting and Fermi-surface harmonics (Eqs. 181–185)

## 7. Connections

### 7.1 What in this range is the electron–phonon-only (electrostatic, instantaneous-Coulomb) baseline that EPP generalizes

1. **The Fröhlich vertex, Eq. (180), is purely electrostatic.** Its kernel $\frac{4\pi}{\Omega}\frac{e^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{1}{(\mathbf q+\mathbf G)\cdot\boldsymbol\epsilon^\infty\cdot(\mathbf q+\mathbf G)}$ is the instantaneous, longitudinal Coulomb interaction screened by the clamped-ion electronic permittivity, taken at $\omega\to0$ for the electrons but used at the phonon frequency. No transverse field, no vector potential, no $1/c$.
   - EPP replaces this with the full photon propagator: a longitudinal (Coulomb) part plus a transverse (retarded) part.
   - Its "electrodynamic Fröhlich vertex" should reduce to Eq. (180) for $q\gg\sqrt{\epsilon}\,\omega_{\rm LO}/c$ (the electrostatic limit) and differ for $q\lesssim\omega_{\rm LO}/c$, where LO and TO phonons mix with photons (polaritons).
   - The $1/|\mathbf q|$ divergence highlighted in Sec. VI.A.3 is precisely the feature EPP revisits.
2. **$Z^*_\kappa$ and $\epsilon^\infty$ are the electrostatic connection quantities.** In Eq. (180), $\boldsymbol\epsilon^\infty$ is "the permittivity evaluated at clamped nuclei", i.e. the electronic RPA (+xc) dielectric response at $\mathbf q\to0$. In EPP language this is the *electronic* part of the longitudinal photon self-energy. The ionic part (Born charges dressing the phonon propagator) builds $\epsilon(\omega)=\epsilon^\infty+\sum_\nu\frac{S_\nu}{\omega_{{\rm TO},\nu}^2-\omega^2}$ and the LST relation $\epsilon_s/\epsilon^\infty=\omega_{\rm LO}^2/\omega_{\rm TO}^2$. The combination $1/\epsilon^\infty-1/\epsilon_s$ in the Fröhlich strength is exactly "ionic screening". EPP's connection formulas between photon self-energies and $Z^*$, $\epsilon^\infty$ should reproduce these.
3. **LO–TO splitting / nonanalytic terms.** The text says the long-range split is "analogous to that in use for calculating the splitting between LO and TO phonons in polar materials (Gonze and Lee 1997)". In the electrostatic theory the LO–TO splitting comes from the longitudinal Coulomb field only, and the TO mode is unaffected. In full electrodynamics the TO mode couples to transverse photons (polariton), and the LO mode is the zero of $\epsilon(\omega)$. EPP's "electrodynamic phonon self-energy" contains both, with the Gonze–Lee nonanalytic term as its static, instantaneous limit.
4. **Phonon self-energy and nonadiabaticity (Sec. VII; Eqs. 140, 145).** $\Pi^{\rm NA}$ is built from $W_{\rm e}(\omega)-W_{\rm e}(0)$ (Eq. 140; see G3/G4), i.e. the electronic screened Coulomb interaction. Two different "retardation" notions appear:
   - *electronic* nonadiabaticity: the finite response time of electrons, $\omega$ vs $v_Fq$. This is the subject of Sec. VII and already present in the e–ph theory.
   - *electromagnetic* retardation: the finite speed of light, $\omega$ vs $cq$. This is absent in Giustino and is the subject of EPP.

   The relevant scales are well separated: $\omega/c\sim10^{-4}$ Å$^{-1}$ ≪ $\omega/v_F\sim10^{-2}$ Å$^{-1}$. EPP generalizes the *same* Dyson-equation structure (Eq. 137, $\tilde\Omega^2=\omega^2+2\omega\Pi$) by adding the photon-mediated (transverse) contribution to $\Pi$. The non-commuting $\mathbf q\to0$ and $\omega\to0$ limits of Sec. VII have an electrodynamic analog: longitudinal and transverse responses coincide at $\mathbf q\to0$ in an isotropic medium only when $\omega\neq0$ is kept.
5. **$W$ and its nuclear (phonon) contribution.** The Fan–Migdal self-energy (Eqs. 157, 186–187) is the $iGW_{\rm ph}$ part of Hedin's $\Sigma=iGW\Gamma$, with $W=W_{\rm e}+W_{\rm ph}$ (G3). In the Eliashberg language $\alpha^2F$ is the spectral function of the phonon-mediated part of $W$ projected on the Fermi surface. In EPP the boson exchanged is the full electromagnetic field dressed by electrons and ions. The phonon-mediated $W_{\rm ph}$ becomes the ionic part of the longitudinal photon propagator, and transverse photon exchange adds current–current (magnetic and Breit-type) terms of order $(v/c)^2$. For ARPES kinks these are negligible, but the polar LO contribution to $W_{\rm ph}$ is exactly the Fröhlich part, and it is that part which EPP renormalizes electrodynamically at small $q$.
6. **Debye–Waller (Eq. 190) ↔ diamagnetic ($A^2$) term (analogy, my interpretation).** DW is second order in the nuclear displacement at a single vertex (a "tadpole/seagull"). It is a static local potential. In minimal coupling $(\mathbf p+e\mathbf A)^2/2m$, the $A^2$ term plays the analogous role for photons: a two-photon contact vertex required for gauge invariance (the $f$-sum rule). If EPP includes it, the parallel is pedagogically useful.
7. **Adiabatic vs nonadiabatic.** The adiabatic (Born–Oppenheimer) phonon uses the static electronic response, $\omega\to0$. The electrostatic Coulomb interaction is the $c\to\infty$ limit of the photon propagator. Both are "instantaneous" limits of a retarded boson, and EPP restores the second one.
8. **Hedin–Baym equations.** Every self-energy in this range comes from the Hedin–Baym closed set with $\Gamma\approx1$, KS $G_0$ and adiabatic $D$. EPP extends the set so that the boson line is the photon propagator, whose self-energy (polarization) has electronic and ionic parts. Sec. VI's machinery (Wannier interpolation, long-range subtraction) is what would make an *ab initio* EPP calculation feasible: the electrodynamic Fröhlich vertex would be added analytically like $g^{\mathcal L}$.
9. **Relevance to 2D materials (the user's field; beyond Giustino).** In 2D the long-range part of $g$ is not $1/q$. It behaves like $1/(1+r_{\rm eff}q)$, finite at $q\to0$ (Sohier, Calandra and Mauri 2016), because the 2D Coulomb kernel is $2\pi/(q\,\epsilon_{\rm 2D}(q))$ with Keldysh-type screening. This is the same screening that enters $W$ in GW and BSE for 2D excitons. Eq. (180) is therefore the 3D electrostatic special case. Both the 2D and the electrodynamic generalizations modify its small-$q$ behaviour.

## 8. Source issues

1. **p. 32, Sec. VI intro, wording slip (confident).** "there the denominators become large whenever the difference between two electronic eigenvalues approaches a phonon energy." It should read that the denominators become **small** (the integrands become large). For Eq. (157) at $\hbar\omega=\varepsilon_{n\mathbf k}$ the denominator is $(\varepsilon_{n\mathbf k}-\varepsilon_{m\mathbf k+\mathbf q}\mp\hbar\omega_{\mathbf q\nu})/\hbar\to0$.
2. **Eq. (180), normalization factor $N_p$ (fairly confident).**
   - Eq. (180) has $N_p$ inside the square root, and its bra–ket $\langle\psi_{m\mathbf k+\mathbf q}|e^{i(\mathbf q+\mathbf G)\cdot\mathbf r}|\psi_{n\mathbf k}\rangle_{\rm sc}$ is intensive (it equals $[U_{\mathbf k+\mathbf q}U^\dagger_{\mathbf k}]_{mn}\to\delta_{mn}$). So $g^{\mathcal L}\propto N_p^{-1/2}$.
   - The review's own $g_{mn\nu}(\mathbf k,\mathbf q)$ (Eq. 38, with $\hat H_{\rm ep}=N_p^{-1/2}\sum g\cdots$, Eq. 37) is independent of $N_p$.
   - Eq. (180) therefore follows the convention of Verdi and Giustino (2015), where the $N_p^{-1/2}$ is absorbed into $g$. To add $g^{\mathcal L}$ to $g^{\mathcal S}$ in the Eq. (38) convention, **drop $N_p$** (set $N_p\to1$).
   - The magnitudes of Fig. 7 (about 700 meV at $q\approx0.2$ Å$^{-1}$) are consistent with $N_p\to1$.
3. **Eq. (180), phase factor (flagged by the author).** Footnote 7 notes that Verdi and Giustino (2015), Eq. (4), lacks $e^{-i(\mathbf q+\mathbf G)\cdot\boldsymbol\tau_\kappa}$. Eq. (180) here includes it. Builders copying from VG2015 should use the review's form.
4. **Notation clash in Eq. (180).** $\varepsilon_0$ (vacuum permittivity) sits next to $\boldsymbol\epsilon^\infty$ (relative permittivity tensor). The static dielectric constant needed for the Fröhlich reduction is not named in the review. Use $\epsilon_s$ on the page.
5. **Saitta formula (p. 37), average and spin factor (moderately confident).** "$\hbar\mathrm{Re}\Pi^{\rm NA}_{\mathbf q=0,\nu\nu}\simeq N_F\langle|g_{nn\nu}(\mathbf k,\mathbf q=0)|^2\rangle_{\rm BZ}$, where $\langle\cdots\rangle_{\rm BZ}$ stands for the average taken over the wave vectors $\mathbf k$ in the Brillouin zone."
   - Derived from Eq. (145), the intraband static term gives $\delta(\varepsilon_{n\mathbf k}-\varepsilon_F)$. The average is therefore a **Fermi-surface** average, $\langle|g|^2\rangle_{\rm FS}=\sum_n\int\frac{d\mathbf k}{\Omega_{\rm BZ}}|g_{nn}|^2\delta(\varepsilon_{n\mathbf k}-\varepsilon_F)/N_F$, not a BZ average.
   - Eq. (145) also carries an explicit spin factor 2. The formula holds as written only if $N_F$ includes both spins, whereas Eqs. (191)–(192) use $N_F$ per spin.
6. **Eq. (185), linewidth convention (clarification, not an error).** $\gamma_{\mathbf q\nu}$ in Eq. (185) is the HWHM ($=-\mathrm{Im}\Pi=1/2\tau^{\rm ph}$; p. 23). It is consistent with Eq. (146), as I checked. Many papers and codes call $2\gamma$ the "linewidth" (FWHM), and Allen's original definition sums spin into $\mathbf k$. Factor-of-2 confusion is common; state the convention on the page.
7. **Friedel oscillation exponent (p. 34; low confidence, flag only).** The text says $\partial V^{\rm KS}/\partial\tau$ in metals has Friedel oscillations decaying as $|\mathbf R_{p'}|^{-4}$. For a 3D free-electron metal the screened potential of a point charge decays as $\cos(2k_Fr)/r^3$, and its gradient (a displaced ion) keeps a leading $2k_F\sin(2k_Fr)/r^3$ term. That suggests $r^{-3}$ asymptotically, with $r^{-4}$ only for the non-oscillating subleading part. Builders should not emphasize the exponent.
8. **Eq. (189), unstated assumption.** "Using Eq. (164) with $\Gamma_{n\mathbf k}=0$ we find … $\mathbf V=Z\mathbf v$" holds only if the explicit $\mathbf k$ dependence of $\Sigma$ is neglected ($\partial_{\mathbf k}\Sigma=0$). Also, Eq. (189) uses $\Sigma_{nn\mathbf k}$ without the "ep"/"FM" superscript; it means $\Sigma^{\rm ep}$ (Fan–Migdal in practice).
9. **Eqs. (186)–(188), implicit zero of energy.** $f(\varepsilon/k_BT)$ and $\hbar\omega$ in Eqs. (186)–(188) are measured from $\varepsilon_F$. Eq. (187) therefore implicitly uses $\varepsilon_{m\mathbf k+\mathbf q}-\varepsilon_F$ in $\delta(\varepsilon-\varepsilon_{m\mathbf k+\mathbf q})$. This is not stated, and it differs from Eq. (157), where $f_{m\mathbf k+\mathbf q}$ carries $\varepsilon_F$ explicitly.
10. **Eq. (188), dropped indices.** The isotropic average $\alpha^2F_n(\varepsilon,\varepsilon')$ drops the $\mathbf k$ label, and the left side is written $\Sigma^{\rm FM}_n$ (band index only). Fine, but note that it is the self-energy averaged over the constant-energy surface "as for the Eliashberg function".
11. **Eq. (184), sign of $\omega_L$ (low confidence).** With $\nabla^2_{\mathbf k}$ negative semidefinite on normalizable functions, the eigenvalues $\omega_L$ in $|\mathbf v_{\mathbf k}|\nabla^2_{\mathbf k}\Phi_L=\omega_L\Phi_L$ are $\le0$, unless a minus sign or a different operator (Laplace–Beltrami on the Fermi surface) is intended.
