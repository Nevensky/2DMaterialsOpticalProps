## 1. Overview (plain language)

The review's first job is to fix the *object* every later section talks about: the **canonical electron–phonon Hamiltonian, Eq. (1)**. It contains non-interacting quasiparticle electrons (band energies $\varepsilon_{n\mathbf k}$), harmonic phonons ($\hbar\omega_{\mathbf q\nu}$), and a coupling that is linear in the atomic displacements (matrix element $g_{mn\nu}(\mathbf k,\mathbf q)$) plus a quadratic Debye–Waller-type coupling ($g^{\rm DW}$). Giustino states the thesis of the historical section directly: *"the history of the study of electron-phonon interactions is really the history of how to calculate the parameters entering Eq. (1)."*

The historical tour is then a sequence of better and better guesses for one quantity. Bloch's 1929 matrix element, Eq. (2), is $g \propto \sqrt{\hbar/2M\omega}\,(\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf e)\,V_0$, and the whole story is about what goes in place of the "effective potential" $V_0$:

- **Bloch (1929):** a constant, from a deformable-continuum argument.
- **Nordheim (1931):** the bare ionic Coulomb potential $V_\kappa(\mathbf q)$ (rigid ions). It diverges as $q^{-2}$.
- **Mott & Jones (1936):** the ionic potential truncated at the Wigner–Seitz cell, a crude first form of screening.
- **Bardeen (1937):** the ionic potential screened by the static Lindhard dielectric function, Eqs. (3)–(4). This is a self-consistent linear-response (Hartree) calculation, the ancestor of DFPT.
- **Bardeen & Pines (1955):** *dynamical* screening $\epsilon(q,\omega_{\mathbf q\nu})$, Eq. (5). This is the first retarded electron–phonon vertex and came out of a field-theoretic treatment.
- **Bardeen & Shockley (1950), semiconductors:** the deformation potential $E_1 = \Omega\,\partial\varepsilon/\partial\Omega$, Eq. (6), fitted to experiment.
- **Fröhlich (1937–1954), ionic crystals:** a long-range polarization field gives $V_0 \propto (1/\epsilon^\infty-1/\epsilon^0)^{1/2}/q^2$, Eq. (7), so $g\propto 1/q$.

Section II.B then covers the pseudopotential idea (OPW, then Phillips–Kleinman, then PAW, Eqs. (8)–(10)). Smooth, weak effective potentials made semi-quantitative e-ph calculations possible (Sham, Shuey, and others). Section II.C covers the DFT era: frozen-phonon calculations (Dacorogna, Cohen, Lam 1985), the modern matrix element $g=\langle u_{m\mathbf k+\mathbf q}|\Delta_{\mathbf q\nu}v^{\rm KS}|u_{n\mathbf k}\rangle_{\rm uc}$ (Eq. (11)), and finally DFPT (1987–1992), which gives phonons and couplings at arbitrary $\mathbf q$.

For a reader coming from RPA/BSE/$W$: almost every step in this history is a statement about **how the electron–ion potential is screened**. It goes from unscreened, to Lindhard/RPA ($\epsilon=1-v\chi^0$), to dynamically screened, to screened by the lattice polarization (Fröhlich's $1/\epsilon^\infty-1/\epsilon^0$, which is the *ionic* part of the screened Coulomb interaction). Throughout, the field doing the screening is the **instantaneous, longitudinal Coulomb field**. No transverse photon appears anywhere. That is exactly the gap the EPP formalism fills.

## 2. Logical flow

1. **Abstract and Sec. I (motivation):** EPIs (electron–phonon interactions) control resistivity, mobility, conventional superconductivity, indirect optical absorption, hot-carrier thermalization, the temperature dependence of band structures, kinks in ARPES, Kohn anomalies in phonon dispersions, and spin lifetimes. *Ab initio* EPI calculations lagged DFT total-energy work (early 1980s) until DFPT appeared (Baroni, Giannozzi & Testa 1987; Gonze, Allan & Teter 1992; Savrasov 1992). The review has three parts: elementary theory plus DFT (Secs. II–III), field theory (IV–V), and applications (VI–XII).
2. **Sec. II, Eq. (1):** the standard coupled Hamiltonian. Line 1 holds the separate subsystems, line 2 the first-order coupling, lines 3–4 (in square brackets) the second-order Debye–Waller coupling. Giustino lists its hidden assumptions: well-defined electronic quasiparticles, harmonic and adiabatic phonons, and no prescription for computing $\varepsilon_{n\mathbf k}$, $\omega_{\mathbf q\nu}$, $g$ or $g^{\rm DW}$.
3. **II.A.1, metals:** free-electron bands $\varepsilon_{n\mathbf k}=\hbar^2k^2/2m_e-\varepsilon_F$ and Debye phonons $\omega_{\mathbf q\nu}=v_s|\mathbf q|$. Bloch's matrix element is Eq. (2), with the polarization factor $\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf e$ (only longitudinal waves scatter if umklapp is ignored).
   - Choices for $V_0$: Bloch's constant, then Nordheim's rigid-ion $V_\kappa(\mathbf q)$ (diverges as $q^{-2}$), then the Mott–Jones Wigner–Seitz truncation.
   - Then Bardeen's Lindhard screening, Eqs. (3)–(4), which removes the divergence because $\epsilon\to(k_{TF}/q)^2$. Giustino calls this the precursor of DFPT. What it misses is exchange and correlation in the screening (Bardeen & Pines 1955; Hone 1960).
   - Bardeen–Pines also obtained a retarded vertex, Eq. (5). Giustino notes that "this development was not followed up in the literature on *ab initio* calculations of EPIs."
4. **II.A.2, semiconductors:** carriers sit near band extrema, so only long-wavelength phonons matter. Bardeen and Shockley showed that $V_0\to E_1=\Omega\,\partial\varepsilon_{n\mathbf k}/\partial\Omega$ (Eq. (6)), with $E_1$ fitted to mobility data (e.g. Si) and shear deformations added by Dumke (1956). The approach is semi-empirical and not predictive.
5. **II.A.3, ionic crystals:** displacements create long-range electric fields, which open a new scattering channel. In an insulator there are few free carriers, so one considers a single electron in the lattice polarization field. Fröhlich, Pelzer & Zienau (1950) give Eq. (7). Combined with Eq. (2) this yields $g\propto|\mathbf q|^{-1}$ whenever $\epsilon^0>\epsilon^\infty$, which leads to strong coupling and polarons (Pekar 1946; Lee, Low & Pines 1953; Fröhlich 1954).
6. **II.B, pseudopotentials:** start from OPWs (Herring 1940), $|\mathbf k+\mathbf G\rangle_{\rm OPW}$. Written in PAW notation, the all-electron and pseudo wavefunctions are related by $\hat T=1-\sum_c|\phi_c\rangle\langle\phi_c|$ (Eq. (8)). This gives a generalized eigenproblem, Eq. (9), and the Phillips–Kleinman equation (10) with a repulsive, core-localized $\hat V^{\rm rep}$.
   - Cohen–Heine cancellation explains why valence electrons in metals behave as nearly free.
   - Sham (1961) showed that $g$ can be computed with pseudopotentials and pseudo wavefunctions (an improved rigid-ion approximation). Many applications followed (resistivity, mass enhancement, $T_c$, mobility, $T$-dependent bands), mostly using phonon dispersions from neutron data.
   - The freedom in choosing $\hat T$ leads to PAW (Blöchl 1994), from which norm-conserving and ultrasoft pseudopotentials follow as special cases.
7. **II.C, *ab initio* SCF:** DFT (HK 1964, KS 1965) together with good xc parametrizations. Dacorogna, Cohen & Lam (1985) did a frozen-phonon calculation for Al that needed only $Z$ and the atomic mass as input. The modern matrix element is Eq. (11); it includes the self-consistent rearrangement of the electrons, going beyond the rigid-ion approximation. DFPT gives arbitrary $\mathbf q$. The first DFPT EPI calculations (1994–1998) covered elemental metals. By the late 1990s all the ingredients were in place.
8. **III.A (start), lattice dynamics:** harmonic expansion of the clamped-nuclei Born–Oppenheimer energy, Eq. (12), then force constants (13), dynamical matrix (14), normal modes (15)–(18), and the quantum nuclear Hamiltonian (19). This rests on two approximations, harmonic and adiabatic. It continues on p. 8 with ladder operators (Eqs. (20)–(22)).

## 3. Key equations

### Conventions (state these on the page)

- **Units:** SI with $\hbar$ explicit. The Coulomb potential is written $e^2/4\pi\epsilon_0$ (e.g. Eq. (26), p. 8: $V_\kappa(\mathbf r)=-\frac{e^2}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{Z_\kappa}{|\mathbf r|}$). Here $\epsilon_0$ (subscript) is the vacuum permittivity, and $\epsilon^0$, $\epsilon^\infty$ (superscripts) are the static and high-frequency *relative* permittivities.
  - For comparison, the EPP manuscript uses Gaussian units ($e^2/4\pi\epsilon_0\equiv e^2$).
- **Supercell and normalization:** Born–von Kármán (BvK) supercell of $N_p$ unit cells, with lattice vectors $\mathbf R_p$, $p=1,\dots,N_p$. $\Omega$ is the unit-cell volume. There are $N_p$ $\mathbf q$-points on a uniform grid, and Appendix A gives $\sum_{\mathbf q}e^{i\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf R_p}=N_p\delta_{p0}$ and $\sum_p e^{i\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf R_p}=N_p\delta_{\mathbf q0}$.
  - Bloch functions: $\psi_{n\mathbf k}=N_p^{-1/2}u_{n\mathbf k}e^{i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf r}$ **[context, p. 9, Eq. (29)]**. $\psi$ is normalized in the supercell and $u$ in the unit cell.
- **Fourier transform [context, p. 10]:** $f(\mathbf G)=\Omega^{-1}\int_{\rm uc}d\mathbf r\,e^{-i\mathbf G\cdot\mathbf r}f(\mathbf r)$, so $v^{C}(\mathbf q)=\Omega^{-1}\int d\mathbf r\,e^{-i\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf r}\,e^2/4\pi\epsilon_0|\mathbf r|=e^2/(\epsilon_0\Omega q^2)$. Ionic form factors $V_\kappa(\mathbf q)$ follow the same per-cell normalization.
- **Momentum convention in Eq. (1):** $\hat c^\dagger_{m\mathbf k+\mathbf q}\hat c_{n\mathbf k}$ with $(\hat a_{\mathbf q\nu}+\hat a^\dagger_{-\mathbf q\nu})$: absorb $\mathbf q$ or emit $-\mathbf q$.
  - EPP instead writes $c^\dagger_{n\mathbf k}c_{m\mathbf k-\mathbf q}$ and $g_{nm}$, with the $N^{-1/2}$ placed inside $g$ (see Source issues).
- **Phase convention for normal modes:** Maradudin and Vosko (1968). Eq. (18) gives $e_{\kappa\alpha,\nu}(-\mathbf q)=e^*_{\kappa\alpha,\nu}(\mathbf q)$.

### Eq. (1): canonical electron–phonon Hamiltonian

$$
\hat H=\sum_{n\mathbf k}\varepsilon_{n\mathbf k}\hat c^\dagger_{n\mathbf k}\hat c_{n\mathbf k}+\sum_{\mathbf q\nu}\hbar\omega_{\mathbf q\nu}\left(\hat a^\dagger_{\mathbf q\nu}\hat a_{\mathbf q\nu}+1/2\right)
+N_p^{-1/2}\sum_{\substack{\mathbf k,\mathbf q\\ mn\nu}}g_{mn\nu}(\mathbf k,\mathbf q)\,\hat c^\dagger_{m\mathbf k+\mathbf q}\hat c_{n\mathbf k}\left(\hat a_{\mathbf q\nu}+\hat a^\dagger_{-\mathbf q\nu}\right)
$$
$$
+\left[N_p^{-1}\sum_{\substack{\mathbf k,\mathbf q,\mathbf q'\\ mn\nu\nu'}}g^{\rm DW}_{mn\nu\nu'}(\mathbf k,\mathbf q,\mathbf q')\,\hat c^\dagger_{m\mathbf k+\mathbf q+\mathbf q'}\hat c_{n\mathbf k}\left(\hat a_{\mathbf q\nu}+\hat a^\dagger_{-\mathbf q\nu}\right)\left(\hat a_{\mathbf q'\nu'}+\hat a^\dagger_{-\mathbf q'\nu'}\right)\right].\qquad(1)
$$

**Symbols:**
- $\varepsilon_{n\mathbf k}$ is the single-particle eigenvalue for crystal momentum $\mathbf k$ in band $n$.
- $\omega_{\mathbf q\nu}$ is the phonon frequency at wavevector $\mathbf q$ in branch $\nu$.
- $\hat c^\dagger,\hat c$ are fermionic and $\hat a^\dagger,\hat a$ bosonic creation and destruction operators.
- $N_p$ is the number of unit cells in the BvK supercell.
- $g_{mn\nu}(\mathbf k,\mathbf q)$ and $g^{\rm DW}_{mn\nu\nu'}(\mathbf k,\mathbf q,\mathbf q')$ are the coupling strengths. Both have the **dimension of energy**.

**Meaning:**
- Line 1 holds the two separate subsystems.
- Line 2 is the coupling to first order in the atomic displacements.
- Lines 3–4 are the coupling to second order ("DW" stands for Debye–Waller and relates to the Debye–Waller self-energy, Sec. V.B.2). This term is rarely found in the early literature, hence the brackets, but it is essential for $T$-dependent band structures (Sec. V.B.1).

**Hidden assumptions:**
- Electrons are well-defined quasiparticles.
- Phonons are harmonic and adiabatic.
- There is **no explicit electron–electron interaction** and **no electromagnetic field**: the Coulomb interaction is hidden inside $\varepsilon_{n\mathbf k}$, $\omega_{\mathbf q\nu}$ and the screened $g$.
- The Hamiltonian provides no recipe for the parameters.

The derivation is given in Sec. III (Eqs. (22), (30), (37), (39)).

### Eq. (2): Bloch's matrix element (1929, in modern notation)

$$
g_{mn\nu}(\mathbf k,\mathbf q)=-i\left(\frac{\hbar}{2N_pM_\kappa\omega_{\mathbf q\nu}}\right)^{1/2}\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf e_{\kappa\nu}(\mathbf q)\,V_0.\qquad(2)
$$

**Symbols:**
- $M_\kappa$ is the mass of nucleus $\kappa$.
- $\mathbf e_{\kappa\nu}(\mathbf q)$ is the polarization of the acoustic mode.
- $V_0$ is the unit-cell average of the "effective" potential felt by the electrons.

**What it describes:** scattering $\mathbf k\to\mathbf k+\mathbf q$ by an acoustic phonon $(\mathbf q,\omega_{\mathbf q\nu})$ in a free-electron metal.
- Umklapp processes ($\mathbf k\to\mathbf k+\mathbf q+\mathbf G$) are neglected.
- The "polarization factor" $\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf e$ means only longitudinal sound waves scatter electrons.
- Bloch estimated $V_0=\hbar^2/(16m_ea_0^2)$, where $a_0$ is the Bohr radius. This is $E_{\rm Ha}/16\approx1.70$ eV (my evaluation).

The structure $\sqrt{\hbar/2M\omega}$ is the zero-point amplitude (cf. Eq. (21), $l_{\mathbf q\nu}=[\hbar/(2M_0\omega_{\mathbf q\nu})]^{1/2}$). Multiplying it by $\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf e\,V$ gives the potential gradient times displacement.

Derived in Sec. III.B.5 as Eq. (58) **[context, p. 12]**: $g_{mn\nu}(\mathbf k,\mathbf q)=-i[\hbar/(2N_pM_\kappa\omega_{\mathbf q\nu})]^{1/2}\,\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf e_{\kappa\nu}(\mathbf q)V_\kappa(\mathbf q)$. That derivation assumes an unscreened bare pseudopotential, one atom per cell, free-electron $u_{n\mathbf k}=\Omega^{-1/2}e^{i\mathbf G_n\cdot\mathbf r}$, and no umklapp.

The general umklapp form, Eq. (57), is $-i[\hbar/(2N_pM_\kappa\omega)]^{1/2}V_\kappa(\mathbf q+\mathbf G_m-\mathbf G_n)(\mathbf q+\mathbf G_m-\mathbf G_n)\cdot\mathbf e_{\kappa\nu}$.

### Free-electron and Debye models (inline, Sec. II.A.1)

$$\varepsilon_{n\mathbf k}=\hbar^2\mathbf k^2/2m_e-\varepsilon_F,\qquad \omega_{\mathbf q\nu}=v_s|\mathbf q|,$$

where $m_e$ is the electron mass, $\varepsilon_F$ the Fermi energy and $v_s$ the speed of sound. Both are reasonable for the alkali and noble metals that were studied at the time.

### Nordheim (1931) and Mott–Jones (1936) (inline)

- **Nordheim:** $V_0\to V_\kappa(\mathbf q)$, the Fourier component of the bare ionic Coulomb potential. This is the **rigid-ion approximation**: the effective potential is a sum of bare ionic potentials that shift rigidly with each nucleus. Problem: $V_\kappa(q)\propto q^{-2}$ as $q\to0$, giving unrealistically strong EPIs.
- **Mott–Jones:** truncate $V_\kappa(\mathbf r)$ at the Wigner–Seitz cell boundary, i.e. take the Fourier integral over one WS cell only. The result is non-singular at long wavelengths and is the first rudimentary form of electronic screening.
  - My closed form, for a WS *sphere* of radius $R_{\rm WS}$ (with $\Omega=4\pi R_{\rm WS}^3/3$): $V_{\rm MJ}(q)=-\frac{Z e^2}{\epsilon_0\Omega q^2}\left[1-\cos(qR_{\rm WS})\right]$, which tends to $-\frac{3}{2}\frac{Ze^2}{4\pi\epsilon_0R_{\rm WS}}$ as $q\to0$.

### Eq. (3): Bardeen's screened potential (1937)

$$V_0\to V_\kappa(\mathbf q)/\epsilon(q).\qquad(3)$$

### Eq. (4): static Lindhard function

$$\epsilon(q)=1+(k_{TF}/q)^2F(q/2k_F),\qquad F(x)=\frac12+\frac{1}{4x}(1-x^2)\log\frac{|1+x|}{|1-x|}.\qquad(4)$$

**Symbols:**
- $k_{TF}$ is the Thomas–Fermi screening wavevector and $k_F$ the Fermi wavevector.
- From p. 13 **[context]**: $k_{TF}=[4me^2k_F/(4\pi\epsilon_0\pi\hbar^2)]^{1/2}$, i.e. $k_{TF}^2=4k_F/(\pi a_0)$.

**Behaviour:** $F(0)=1$ and $F(1)=1/2$, and $F'$ has a logarithmic singularity at $x=1$ ($q=2k_F$), which is the origin of Kohn anomalies. As $q\to0$, $\epsilon\to(k_{TF}/q)^2$, which cancels the $q^{-2}$ of $V_\kappa$ and removes the singularity.

**Approximations:** RPA (linearized Hartree, i.e. "test-charge" screening), no exchange-correlation, static limit, jellium.

Bardeen's result is re-derived in Sec. III.B.5 **[context]**:
- Eq. (59): $\chi^0_{\mathbf G\mathbf G'}(\mathbf q)=-\frac{m_ek_F}{\pi^2\hbar^2\Omega}F(|\mathbf q+\mathbf G|/2k_F)\delta_{\mathbf G\mathbf G'}$.
- Eq. (60): $\epsilon_{\mathbf G\mathbf G'}(\mathbf q)=\delta_{\mathbf G\mathbf G'}[1+(k_{TF}^2/|\mathbf q+\mathbf G|^2)F(|\mathbf q+\mathbf G|/2k_F)]$.
- Eq. (61): $g=-i[\hbar/(2N_pM_\kappa\omega)]^{1/2}\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf e\,V_\kappa(\mathbf q)/\epsilon(q)$.

**A useful result (mine, standard):** for a bare ion $V_\kappa(q)=-Ze^2/(\epsilon_0\Omega q^2)$ in jellium with $n\Omega=Z$, $\lim_{q\to0}V_\kappa(q)/\epsilon(q)=-\tfrac23\varepsilon_F$. This is Bardeen's famous long-wavelength deformation potential (Al: $-7.8$ eV; Na: $-2.2$ eV).

### Eq. (5): Bardeen–Pines dynamically screened (retarded) vertex (1955)

$$V_0\to V_\kappa(\mathbf q)/\epsilon(q,\omega_{\mathbf q\nu}).\qquad(5)$$

Here $\epsilon(q,\omega)$ is the frequency-dependent Lindhard function, evaluated at the phonon frequency. This came from the first field-theoretic e-ph treatment (with Nakajima 1954). The "retarded" vertex means the potential felt by electrons depends on how fast the displacement happens.

Bardeen & Pines also included screened exchange (their Appendix B). Giustino remarks that this was "somewhat surprisingly ... not followed up" in *ab initio* work. On p. 12 he notes that DFPT screening is static (adiabatic), and that retardation is formalized in Sec. IV.

### Eq. (6): deformation potential (Bardeen and Shockley 1950)

$$V_0\to E_{1,n\mathbf k}=\Omega\,\partial\varepsilon_{n\mathbf k}/\partial\Omega,\qquad(6)$$

where $\Omega$ is the unit-cell volume and $\varepsilon_{n\mathbf k}$ is a valence or conduction band extremum.

**Assumptions:**
- Long-wavelength acoustic waves, related to the elastic strain.
- Effective-mass theory.
- $E_1$ is obtained empirically (e.g. Si, by fitting mobility data). Anisotropic surfaces require shear deformation potentials (Dumke 1956).

**Physics:** a long acoustic wave is a local uniform dilation, $\Delta\Omega/\Omega=\nabla\cdot\mathbf u\sim i\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf u$, which shifts the band edge by $E_1\,\Delta\Omega/\Omega$.

### Eq. (7): Fröhlich (Fröhlich, Pelzer & Zienau 1950)

$$V_0\to-\left[\frac{e^2M_\kappa\omega_{\mathbf q\nu}^2}{\epsilon_0\Omega}\left(\frac{1}{\epsilon^\infty}-\frac{1}{\epsilon^0}\right)\right]^{1/2}\frac{1}{|\mathbf q|^2}.\qquad(7)$$

**Symbols:** $e$ is the electron charge, $\epsilon_0$ the vacuum permittivity, and $\epsilon^0$, $\epsilon^\infty$ the static and high-frequency relative permittivities. The text says it applies to isotropic ionic crystals and is "derived in Sec. VI.A.3".

**Combined with Eq. (2):** take the LO mode with $\omega_{\mathbf q\nu}=\omega_{\rm LO}$ and $\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf e=|\mathbf q|$. The mass cancels:
$$
|g^{\rm Fr}(\mathbf q)|=\frac{1}{|\mathbf q|}\left[\frac{e^2\hbar\omega_{\rm LO}}{2\epsilon_0N_p\Omega}\left(\frac1{\epsilon^\infty}-\frac1{\epsilon^0}\right)\right]^{1/2}.
$$
This diverges as $|\mathbf q|^{-1}$ whenever $\epsilon^0>\epsilon^\infty$, i.e. whenever the lattice contributes to the screening. It provides "the physical basis for the phenomenon of electron self-trapping in polarons".

The general anisotropic, multi-mode form is Eq. (180) **[context, p. 35]**:
$$
g^{\mathcal L}_{mn\nu}(\mathbf k,\mathbf q)=i\frac{4\pi}{\Omega}\frac{e^2}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\sum_\kappa\left(\frac{\hbar}{2N_pM_\kappa\omega_{\mathbf q\nu}}\right)^{1/2}\sum_{\mathbf G\neq-\mathbf q}\frac{(\mathbf q+\mathbf G)\cdot\mathbf Z^*_\kappa\cdot\mathbf e_{\kappa\nu}(\mathbf q)}{(\mathbf q+\mathbf G)\cdot\boldsymbol\epsilon^\infty\cdot(\mathbf q+\mathbf G)}\langle\psi_{m\mathbf k+\mathbf q}|e^{i(\mathbf q+\mathbf G)\cdot(\mathbf r-\boldsymbol\tau_\kappa)}|\psi_{n\mathbf k}\rangle_{\rm sc}.
$$
- $\mathbf Z^*_\kappa$ are the Born effective charge tensors.
- $\boldsymbol\epsilon^\infty$ is the clamped-ion electronic permittivity tensor.
- The EPP manuscript reproduces exactly this expression (its Eq. 131) and an electrodynamic generalization.

I checked that Eq. (180) reduces to Eq. (2)+(7) for a cubic diatomic crystal. The ingredients are $\mathbf Z^*_{1}=-\mathbf Z^*_2=Z^*$, the LO eigenvector $(\sqrt{M_2/M},-\sqrt{M_1/M})\hat{\mathbf q}$, $\mathbf G=0$, the relation $\omega_{\rm LO}^2-\omega_{\rm TO}^2=Z^{*2}e^2/(\epsilon_0\mu\Omega\epsilon^\infty)$, and Lyddane–Sachs–Teller, $\omega_{\rm LO}^2/\omega_{\rm TO}^2=\epsilon^0/\epsilon^\infty$.

### Eq. (8): all-electron vs pseudo wavefunction (OPW written in PAW notation)

OPW basis: $|\mathbf k+\mathbf G\rangle_{\rm OPW}=|\mathbf k+\mathbf G\rangle-\sum_c|\phi_c\rangle\langle\phi_c|\mathbf k+\mathbf G\rangle$, where the $|\phi_c\rangle$ are the core states of all atoms. Then $|\psi_{n\mathbf k}\rangle=\sum_{\mathbf G}c_{\mathbf k}(\mathbf G)|\mathbf k+\mathbf G\rangle_{\rm OPW}$ (the all-electron wavefunction) and $|\tilde\psi_{n\mathbf k}\rangle=\sum_{\mathbf G}c_{\mathbf k}(\mathbf G)|\mathbf k+\mathbf G\rangle$ (the pseudo wavefunction). The two are related by

$$|\psi_{n\mathbf k}\rangle=\hat T|\tilde\psi_{n\mathbf k}\rangle,\qquad \hat T=1-\sum_c|\phi_c\rangle\langle\phi_c|.\qquad(8)$$

$\hat T$ is a projector, so $\hat T^\dagger=\hat T=\hat T^2$. By construction $|\tilde\psi\rangle$ has no rapid oscillations near the cores.

### Eq. (9): generalized eigenvalue problem

$$\hat T^\dagger\hat H\hat T|\tilde\psi_{n\mathbf k}\rangle=\varepsilon_{n\mathbf k}\hat T^\dagger\hat T|\tilde\psi_{n\mathbf k}\rangle.\qquad(9)$$

This follows from $\hat H|\psi\rangle=\varepsilon|\psi\rangle$ and Eq. (8). $\hat H$ can be, for example, the Kohn–Sham Hamiltonian.

### Eq. (10): Phillips–Kleinman pseudo-Hamiltonian (1959)

$$(\hat H+\hat V^{\rm rep})|\tilde\psi_{n\mathbf k}\rangle=\varepsilon_{n\mathbf k}|\tilde\psi_{n\mathbf k}\rangle,\qquad \hat V^{\rm rep}=\sum_c(\varepsilon_{n\mathbf k}-\varepsilon_c)|\phi_c\rangle\langle\phi_c|,\qquad(10)$$

where $\varepsilon_c$ is a core eigenvalue.

**Properties:**
- Since $\varepsilon_{n\mathbf k}>\varepsilon_c$, $\hat V^{\rm rep}$ is **repulsive**, localized at the cores, nonlocal and energy dependent.
- It largely cancels the attractive nuclear potential (Cohen and Heine 1961), which is why valence electrons in metals behave almost free.
- The result is smooth "pseudopotentials" whose form factors have only a few Fourier components (Phillips 1958; Heine and Abarenkov 1964; Animalu and Heine 1965; Cohen and Bergstresser 1966).

I checked the derivation from Eq. (9) using $\hat H|\phi_c\rangle=\varepsilon_c|\phi_c\rangle$: it is correct.

### Eq. (11): DFT electron–phonon matrix element (Chang et al., Dacorogna et al., Lam et al., 1985–1986)

$$g_{mn\nu}(\mathbf k,\mathbf q)=\langle u_{m\mathbf k+\mathbf q}|\Delta_{\mathbf q\nu}v^{\rm KS}|u_{n\mathbf k}\rangle_{\rm uc}.\qquad(11)$$

**Symbols:**
- $u_{n\mathbf k}$ and $u_{m\mathbf k+\mathbf q}$ are the lattice-periodic Bloch parts of the Kohn–Sham states.
- $\Delta_{\mathbf q\nu}v^{\rm KS}$ is the phonon-induced variation of the *self-consistent* KS potential. It includes the screening response of all electrons: Hartree plus xc, i.e. "RPA+$xc$", or test-electron $\epsilon^{\rm H\it xc}=1-(v^C+f^{xc})\chi^0$ (Eq. (54)).
- "uc" means the integral runs over one unit cell.

This is identical to Eq. (38) of Sec. III.B. From p. 9 **[context]**: $\Delta_{\mathbf q\nu}v^{\rm KS}=l_{\mathbf q\nu}\sum_{\kappa\alpha}(M_0/M_\kappa)^{1/2}e_{\kappa\alpha,\nu}(\mathbf q)\,\partial_{\kappa\alpha,\mathbf q}v^{\rm KS}$, with $\partial_{\kappa\alpha,\mathbf q}v^{\rm KS}=\sum_pe^{-i\mathbf q\cdot(\mathbf r-\mathbf R_p)}\partial V^{\rm KS}/\partial\tau_{\kappa\alpha}|_{\mathbf r-\mathbf R_p}$.

**Approximations:** adiabatic (static) screening, the KS potential as the scattering potential, and the specific xc functional.

**Historical ladder recap:** bare $V_\kappa$ (Nordheim) → $V_\kappa/\epsilon^{\rm RPA}$ (Bardeen) → $\Delta v^{\rm KS}$, i.e. bare potential screened by $\epsilon^{\rm H\it xc}$ (Eq. (53): $\partial v^{\rm KS}=(\epsilon^{\rm H\it xc})^{-1}\partial v^{\rm en}$).

### III.A (p. 7): lattice vibrations, needed to make sense of the phonon line of Eq. (1)

**Setup:**
- $M$ nuclei per unit cell, at positions $\boldsymbol\tau_\kappa$ with Cartesian components $\tau_{\kappa\alpha}$.
- The nucleus $\kappa$ in cell $p$ sits at $\boldsymbol\tau_{\kappa p}=\mathbf R_p+\boldsymbol\tau_\kappa$.
- $U(\{\boldsymbol\tau_{\kappa p}\})$ is the total potential energy of the BvK supercell with electrons in their ground state and nuclei *clamped* as classical particles. Every DFT code outputs it.

**Eq. (12), harmonic expansion:**
$$U=U_0+\frac12\sum_{\substack{\kappa\alpha p\\ \kappa'\alpha'p'}}\frac{\partial^2U}{\partial\tau_{\kappa\alpha p}\partial\tau_{\kappa'\alpha'p'}}\Delta\tau_{\kappa\alpha p}\Delta\tau_{\kappa'\alpha'p'}.\qquad(12)$$
Here $U_0$ is the energy at equilibrium $\boldsymbol\tau^0_{\kappa p}$, and the derivatives are evaluated there.

**Eq. (13), interatomic force constants:**
$$C_{\kappa\alpha p,\kappa'\alpha'p'}=\partial^2U/\partial\tau_{\kappa\alpha p}\partial\tau_{\kappa'\alpha'p'}.\qquad(13)$$

**Eq. (14), dynamical matrix:**
$$D^{\rm dm}_{\kappa\alpha,\kappa'\alpha'}(\mathbf q)=(M_\kappa M_{\kappa'})^{-1/2}\sum_pC_{\kappa\alpha0,\kappa'\alpha'p}\exp(i\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf R_p).\qquad(14)$$
The superscript "dm" distinguishes it from the phonon propagators $D(12)$ and $D_{\kappa\alpha p,\kappa'\alpha'p'}$ of Sec. IV.B. $D^{\rm dm}$ is Hermitian.

**Eq. (15), normal modes:**
$$\sum_{\kappa'\alpha'}D^{\rm dm}_{\kappa\alpha,\kappa'\alpha'}(\mathbf q)e_{\kappa'\alpha',\nu}(\mathbf q)=\omega^2_{\mathbf q\nu}e_{\kappa\alpha,\nu}(\mathbf q).\qquad(15)$$

**Eqs. (16)–(17), completeness and orthonormality** ($\nu=1,\dots,3M$):
$$\sum_\nu e^*_{\kappa'\alpha',\nu}(\mathbf q)e_{\kappa\alpha,\nu}(\mathbf q)=\delta_{\kappa\kappa'}\delta_{\alpha\alpha'},\qquad(16)$$
$$\sum_{\kappa\alpha}e^*_{\kappa\alpha,\nu}(\mathbf q)e_{\kappa\alpha,\nu'}(\mathbf q)=\delta_{\nu\nu'}.\qquad(17)$$

**Eq. (18), time reversal and inversion of $\mathbf q$** (Maradudin–Vosko phase choice):
$$\omega^2_{-\mathbf q\nu}=\omega^2_{\mathbf q\nu},\qquad e_{\kappa\alpha,\nu}(-\mathbf q)=e^*_{\kappa\alpha,\nu}(\mathbf q).\qquad(18)$$

**Eq. (19), quantum nuclear Hamiltonian:**
$$\hat H_p=\frac12\sum_{\substack{\kappa\alpha p\\ \kappa'\alpha'p'}}C_{\kappa\alpha p,\kappa'\alpha'p'}\Delta\tau_{\kappa\alpha p}\Delta\tau_{\kappa'\alpha'p'}-\sum_{\kappa\alpha p}\frac{\hbar^2}{2M_\kappa}\frac{\partial^2}{\partial\tau^2_{\kappa\alpha p}}.\qquad(19)$$
This rests on two approximations: (i) harmonic (truncation of Eq. (12) at second order) and (ii) Born–Oppenheimer adiabatic (force constants computed with the electrons in their ground state; see Sec. V.A.1).

**[context, p. 8]** Ladder operators (Appendix B) give
$$\Delta\tau_{\kappa\alpha p}=\left(\frac{M_0}{N_pM_\kappa}\right)^{1/2}\sum_{\mathbf q\nu}e^{i\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf R_p}e_{\kappa\alpha,\nu}(\mathbf q)\,l_{\mathbf q\nu}(\hat a_{\mathbf q\nu}+\hat a^\dagger_{-\mathbf q\nu}),\qquad(20)$$
$$l_{\mathbf q\nu}=[\hbar/(2M_0\omega_{\mathbf q\nu})]^{1/2},\qquad(21)$$
$$\hat H_p=\sum_{\mathbf q\nu}\hbar\omega_{\mathbf q\nu}(\hat a^\dagger_{\mathbf q\nu}\hat a_{\mathbf q\nu}+1/2),\qquad(22)$$
- $M_0$ is an arbitrary reference mass, typically the proton mass.
- The three $\mathbf q=0$ acoustic modes ($\omega=0$) are skipped in sums over zero-point amplitudes.
- Eq. (22) is exactly the phonon line of Eq. (1).

## 4. Concepts and physical intuition

**1. Eq. (1) is a model with screened, effective parameters.** Electrons and ions interact through Coulomb forces, but in Eq. (1) there is no $e$–$e$ term and no field. Everything electromagnetic has been absorbed into three parameter sets: band energies (KS or quasiparticle), adiabatic phonon frequencies (force constants of the electronic ground state), and couplings (screened derivatives of the potential).
- This "integrating out" of the Coulomb field is valid only for the **instantaneous longitudinal** Coulomb interaction and only at the level of static (adiabatic) screening.
- Sec. IV re-derives this properly (Hedin–Baym). EPP goes further and re-derives it with the full electromagnetic field.

**2. The anatomy of $g$: amplitude × polarization × potential.**
$$g\sim\underbrace{\sqrt{\hbar/2M\omega}}_{\text{zero-point amplitude}}\times\underbrace{(\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf e)}_{\text{gradient/polarization}}\times\underbrace{V_0}_{\text{screened ionic potential}}.$$
- Orders of magnitude: $\sqrt{\hbar/2M\omega}$ is about 0.05–0.1 Å for $M\sim$ 10–100 amu and $\hbar\omega\sim$ 10–50 meV (the text gives ~0.1 Å for the zero-point amplitude, p. 10). With $q\sim1$ Å$^{-1}$ and $V_0\sim$ eV, $g\sim0.1$ eV, consistent with the "meV–hundreds of meV" values in later figures.
- Only $\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf e\neq0$ couples in the free-electron, normal-process picture. Transverse acoustic phonons couple only via umklapp or band-structure effects.

**3. Screening, step by step.** The history is a sequence of screening models.
- **Unscreened rigid ion** (Nordheim): $V(q)=-Ze^2/(\epsilon_0\Omega q^2)$ diverges, which gives absurdly strong coupling: $|g|^2\propto q^2|V|^2/\omega\propto q^{-3}$ for acoustic phonons.
- **Mott–Jones:** each ion's potential acts only inside its own WS cell, as if the other electrons neutralize it outside. It is finite at $q=0$.
- **Bardeen/Lindhard (RPA):** $V\to V/\epsilon(q)$, with $\epsilon\approx1+k_{TF}^2/q^2$ at small $q$. The screened potential tends to the constant $-\frac23\varepsilon_F$. The physics: a slowly varying ionic potential is perfectly screened by the Fermi sea, and what remains is a local Fermi-level shift.
  - Numbers (free-electron, my evaluation): Al ($r_s=2.07$) $k_F=1.75$ Å$^{-1}$, $\varepsilon_F=11.7$ eV, $k_{TF}=2.05$ Å$^{-1}$, so $V_0(0)=-7.8$ eV. Na ($r_s=3.93$) $k_F=0.92$ Å$^{-1}$, $\varepsilon_F=3.24$ eV, $k_{TF}=1.49$ Å$^{-1}$, so $V_0(0)=-2.2$ eV. Bloch's constant, 1.7 eV, is the same order.
- **Bardeen–Pines (dynamic RPA):** $\epsilon(q,\omega_{\mathbf q})$. Since $\omega_{\mathbf q}=v_sq$ and $v_s/v_F\sim10^{-3}$–$10^{-2}$ (Al: $v_s\approx6.4$ km/s, $v_F\approx2.0\times10^3$ km/s), the phonon sits at $\omega/qv_F\ll1$, deep in the quasi-static region. There $\mathrm{Re}\,\epsilon(q,\omega)\approx\epsilon(q,0)$, and a small $\mathrm{Im}\,\epsilon\approx(\pi/2)(k_{TF}/q)^2(\omega/qv_F)$ describes Landau damping, i.e. a phonon linewidth.
  - So static screening (DFPT) is excellent for metals except where $\omega\gtrsim qv_F$ (very small $q$), e.g. the nonadiabatic Kohn-anomaly physics of Sec. VII.
- **DFT/DFPT:** RPA plus $xc$ (test-electron $\epsilon^{\rm H\it xc}$), with the full crystal (local fields, band structure) and self-consistency. This is Eq. (11).

**4. Kohn anomaly seed.** $F(x)$ is continuous, but $dF/dx\to-\infty$ logarithmically at $x=1$. The screened ionic potential, and therefore the force constants (phonon frequencies), have a weak singularity at $q=2k_F$, which shows up as a kink in phonon dispersions (Kohn anomalies; mentioned in Sec. I).

**5. Deformation potentials.** A long acoustic wave is a slowly varying strain. Locally the crystal is uniformly compressed, so the band edge moves by $E_1\times$ (fractional volume change). Hence $g_{\rm ac}\propto\sqrt{\hbar/2M\omega_q}\,qE_1\propto\sqrt q$ for $\omega=v_sq$.
- Typical $E_1$ values are a few eV to about 10 eV. The paper quotes no numbers here; Si values were fitted to mobility.
- The deformation potential is an "adiabatic band-edge shift". The same idea (derivative of a band energy with respect to a lattice coordinate) reappears in Allen–Heine–Cardona theory (Sec. IX).

**6. Polar (Fröhlich) coupling: the long-range channel.** In an ionic crystal an LO phonon is a wave of oppositely displaced charged sublattices. That is a macroscopic polarization $\mathbf P\parallel\mathbf q$, which produces a longitudinal macroscopic field ($\nabla\cdot\mathbf D=0$ gives $\mathbf E=-\mathbf P/\epsilon_0\epsilon^\infty$). The electron couples to its potential $\phi\propto P/q$.
- **Why $(1/\epsilon^\infty-1/\epsilon^0)$?** Slow external charges are screened by electrons plus ions ($1/\epsilon^0$); fast ones only by electrons ($1/\epsilon^\infty$). The difference is the ionic (phonon) part of the screening. Equivalently, it is the strength of the phonon-mediated part of the screened interaction $W$.
  - Identity (mine, exact for one LO mode): $N_p|g^{\rm Fr}|^2\frac{2\hbar\omega_{\rm LO}}{(\hbar\omega)^2-(\hbar\omega_{\rm LO})^2}=v(q)\left(\frac1{\epsilon^\infty}-\frac1{\epsilon^0}\right)\frac{\omega_{\rm LO}^2}{\omega^2-\omega_{\rm LO}^2}$, with $v(q)=e^2/(\epsilon_0\Omega q^2)$.
  - Combined with $W=v/\epsilon^\infty+(\ldots)$ this gives $W(q,\omega)=v(q)/\epsilon(\omega)$, where $\epsilon(\omega)=\epsilon^\infty(\omega_{\rm LO}^2-\omega^2)/(\omega_{\rm TO}^2-\omega^2)$ via LST. "$|g|^2\times$ phonon propagator = ionic part of $W$" is precisely what Sec. IV.B.3 (nuclear contribution to $W$) formalizes and what EPP generalizes with retardation.
- **$1/q$ divergence:** $|g|^2\propto1/q^2$, so the coupling is dominated by small $q$, with forward scattering and polaron formation.
  - Fröhlich constant (standard, not in this range): $\alpha=(1/\epsilon^\infty-1/\epsilon^0)\sqrt{\mathrm{Ry}\,m^*/(m_e\hbar\omega_{\rm LO})}$.
  - Illustrative values: GaAs ~0.07, GaN ~0.4–0.5, MgO ~1.7, NaCl ~5 (approximate literature parameters; not from Giustino).
- **Why it matters for 2D materials** (the user's field): in 2D the Fröhlich coupling changes form (finite as $q\to0$ with $\propto\mathrm{erfc}$-type and $\epsilon_{2D}(q)$ screening). That lies beyond this range, but it is the same physics: the long-range Coulomb field of Born charges, screened by $\epsilon^\infty$.

**7. The pseudopotential idea.**
- **Cause:** orthogonality to the cores forces wiggles in valence wavefunctions.
- **Rewriting:** write $\psi=\hat T\tilde\psi$; the smooth $\tilde\psi$ satisfies a Schrödinger equation with an extra repulsive potential.
- **Cancellation:** the repulsion cancels most of the deep Coulomb attraction (Cohen–Heine), leaving a weak effective potential. That justifies the nearly free electron model and Bloch-type matrix elements with a weak $V_0$.
- **Non-uniqueness:** adding any combination of core states to $\tilde\psi$ leaves $\psi$ unchanged, because $\hat T|\phi_c\rangle=0$. This freedom is exploited to make $\tilde\psi$ as smooth as possible, which leads to PAW.
- **Rigid-ion limit:** with local pseudopotentials moving rigidly with the ions (Sham 1961), $g$ is computed from pseudo quantities. This is an improved rigid-ion approximation.

**8. Rigid ion vs self-consistent response.** In the rigid-ion picture the potential of a displaced ion shifts rigidly. In reality the electron cloud rearranges (screening, bond charges). Dacorogna–Cohen–Lam captured this with frozen phonons; DFPT does it by linear response at any $\mathbf q$.
- DFPT costs a single unit cell versus $N_p$ cells: the saving is a factor of about $N_p^2$, typically more than $10^3$ (p. 10).

**9. Adiabatic vs nonadiabatic.**
- The phonon term in Eq. (1) is "meaningful only within the harmonic and adiabatic approximations". Force constants are computed with electrons instantaneously in their ground state for each nuclear configuration (Born–Oppenheimer).
- Bardeen–Pines' $\epsilon(q,\omega_{\mathbf q\nu})$ is the first appearance of nonadiabatic (retarded) screening of the e-ph vertex.
- Born–Oppenheimer is not strictly needed in multicomponent DFT (Kreibich & Gross 2001, p. 8).

**10. History and numbers quoted in the text.**
- Bloch 1929 (first quantum theory of $T$-dependent resistivity; Sec. V of his paper is titled "The interaction of the electrons and the elastic waves of the lattice"). Frenkel 1932 coined "phonon".
- First DFT e-ph calculation: Al, 1985 (Dacorogna, Cohen, Lam). The quote: "This calculation is ab initio since only information about the Al atom, i.e., the atomic number and atomic mass, is used as input." They computed $\lambda_{\mathbf q\nu}$ and phonon linewidths, and the average coupling agreed with the value extracted from $T_c$.
- First DFPT e-ph calculations, 1994–1998: resistivity, thermal conductivity, mass enhancement and $T_c$ for Al, Au, Cu, Mo, Nb, Pb, Pd, Ta, V and Te, all in good agreement with experiment.
- Pseudopotential-era applications: resistivity of metals, mass enhancement, $T_c$ within McMillan's formalism, semiconductor mobility (Ralph 1970), and $T$-dependent band structures (Allen and Cardona 1981, 1983). Most used phonon dispersions from neutron scattering.

## 7. Connections (the electron–phonon-only baseline that EPP generalizes)

**1. Eq. (1) vs the EPP Hamiltonian.**
- Eq. (1) has no explicit $e$–$e$ interaction and no electromagnetic field.
- EPP's starting Hamiltonian (EPP Eq. 109) has the same three lines (with $c^\dagger_{n\mathbf k}c_{m\mathbf k-\mathbf q}$ and $g$ containing $\sqrt{\hbar/2NM\omega}$) plus the explicit Coulomb term $\tfrac12\int\Psi^\dagger\Psi^\dagger V\Psi\Psi$.
- EPP then adds transverse photons (gauge $\Phi=0$, minimal coupling) and a longitudinal Coulomb field for electrons and ions.
- **Pedagogical message:** Eq. (1) is the "everything electromagnetic integrated out, statically and longitudinally" limit.

**2. Instantaneous, longitudinal Coulomb only.** Every screening model in Sec. II uses the scalar Coulomb potential ($V_\kappa\propto1/q^2$, $v^C=e^2/\epsilon_0\Omega q^2$) acting instantaneously:
- Nordheim and Mott–Jones (bare, truncated)
- Bardeen (Lindhard/RPA)
- Bardeen–Pines (dynamical but still longitudinal)
- Fröhlich (macroscopic longitudinal field)
- DFT/DFPT (Hartree + xc)

No vector potential and no transverse current response appear. The EPP formalism replaces $v$ by the full photon propagator $D^0=D^{0,l}+D^{0,t}$ (longitudinal + transverse) and the density response $\chi^0$ by an RPA *photon self-energy* (current–current) with electronic and ionic parts.

**3. Retardation.** Bardeen–Pines' Eq. (5) is the only retarded (frequency-dependent) element in this range, and Giustino stresses it was not followed up *ab initio*. DFPT screening is static (adiabatic); see p. 12. Retardation in EPP is twofold:
- dynamical electronic and ionic polarizabilities, as in Bardeen–Pines;
- finite speed of light ($\omega$ vs $ck$ in the photon propagator). Its electrostatic limit $c\to\infty$ recovers Giustino's formulas.

**4. Polar / Fröhlich coupling.**
- Eq. (7) is the isotropic electrostatic Fröhlich vertex. Eq. (180) (Sec. VI.A.3; Verdi & Giustino 2015) is its first-principles form with Born charges $\mathbf Z^*_\kappa$ and $\boldsymbol\epsilon^\infty$.
- EPP re-derives exactly Eq. (180) (EPP Eq. 131), then builds an electrodynamic Fröhlich vertex in which $\mathbf q\cdot\boldsymbol\epsilon^\infty\cdot\mathbf q$ becomes $\mathbf q\cdot\hat\epsilon(\mathbf q,\omega)\cdot\mathbf q$ (and transverse parts). It recovers Verdi–Giustino in the electrostatic limit (EPP Sec. 7.2, 7.3).

**5. LO–TO splitting, non-analytic terms, Born charges.**
- Not developed in pp. 1–7, but implicit in Eq. (7). The factor $(1/\epsilon^\infty-1/\epsilon^0)$ is tied to LO–TO splitting via LST ($\omega_{\rm LO}^2/\omega_{\rm TO}^2=\epsilon^0/\epsilon^\infty$) and via the non-analytic term $\propto(\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf Z^*)(\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf Z^*)/(\mathbf q\cdot\boldsymbol\epsilon^\infty\cdot\mathbf q)$ in the dynamical matrix (Gonze & Lee 1997, cited in Sec. VI.A.3).
- In EPP the LO–TO splitting emerges as the lowest-order electrodynamic phonon self-energy (EPP Sec. 7.1). With transverse photons the TO branch becomes a phonon-polariton (V3 panel C).

**6. Dielectric screening and $W$.**
- Bardeen's $V_\kappa/\epsilon$ is the RPA-screened electron–ion vertex. DFPT's $\Delta v^{\rm KS}$ is the bare vertex screened by $\epsilon^{\rm H\it xc}$ (Eqs. (53)–(55), p. 12).
- Fröhlich's $(1/\epsilon^\infty-1/\epsilon^0)$ is the ionic part of the screened interaction: $W=W^{\rm el}+W^{\rm ph}$ with $W^{\rm ph}=|g|^2D$ (see V3 and Sec. 4 item 6). This is formalized as the "nuclear contribution to the screened Coulomb interaction" in Giustino Sec. IV.B.3 and the Hedin–Baym equations, Sec. IV.D.
- EPP generalizes this to the electronic plus ionic RPA photon self-energies and their "connection formulas".

**7. Phonon self-energy, and adiabatic vs nonadiabatic.**
- Eq. (19) relies on the Born–Oppenheimer adiabatic approximation (force constants in the electronic ground state).
- Nonadiabatic corrections are the subject of Secs. V.A.2–3 and VII.
- EPP addresses double counting explicitly: the DFT electronic bubble already renormalizes phonons adiabatically, so only dynamic corrections should be added (EPP p. ~20). EPP then derives an *electrodynamic* phonon self-energy.

**8. Debye–Waller term (lines 3–4 of Eq. (1)).** It is second order in displacements and is needed for $T$-dependent band gaps (Sec. V.B.1, IX.A). This is relevant to the user's optical-spectra work (temperature renormalization of excitons). EPP's focus is the first-order vertex and the photon sector.

**9. Links inside Giustino:**

- Eqs. (2)–(4) are derived in Sec. III.B.5 (Eqs. (57)–(61)).
- Eq. (7) connects to Sec. VI.A.3 (Eq. (180)) and to polarons in Sec. V.B.6.

## 8. Source issues

**1. $N_p$ counted twice (Eq. (1) vs Eq. (2)).**
- Eq. (1) carries an explicit $N_p^{-1/2}$ in front of the first-order coupling, and $g$ is defined (Eqs. (11)/(38)) as an $N_p$-independent unit-cell integral. Eqs. (20), (32)–(34) confirm that the single $N_p^{-1/2}$ comes from the displacement expansion and that $\Delta_{\mathbf q\nu}v^{\rm KS}$ has no $N_p$.
- Yet Eq. (2) (and likewise Eqs. (57), (58), (61), (180)) puts $N_p$ inside the square root, $(\hbar/2N_pM_\kappa\omega)^{1/2}$. Inserted into Eq. (1), this would give an overall $N_p^{-1}$.
- **Corrected form** for use with Eq. (1): drop $N_p$ from Eq. (2), i.e. $g=-i(\hbar/2M_\kappa\omega_{\mathbf q\nu})^{1/2}\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf e\,V_0$. Alternatively, keep Eq. (2) as written and drop the explicit $N_p^{-1/2}$ in Eq. (1), which is the older textbook convention and also the one used in the EPP manuscript.
- I am confident about the inconsistency; I verified it with Eqs. (20), (32)–(34), (38), (43). For the page, use one convention consistently. **Recommended:** EPP-style $g$ including $1/\sqrt{N_p}$, with no prefactor in $H$.

**2. Notation clash in Eq. (7).** $\epsilon_0$ (subscript, vacuum permittivity) and $\epsilon^0$ (superscript, static relative permittivity) appear in the same formula, and $\varepsilon_{n\mathbf k}$ (energies) and $\epsilon(q)$ (dielectric function) also coexist. On the page, write $\epsilon_{\rm vac}$ or $\varepsilon_0$ for the vacuum and $\epsilon_{\rm s}$ (or $\epsilon^0$) for the static value.

**3. Eq. (7) assumes a single LO mode and a monatomic-looking Eq. (2).**
- Eq. (2) has a single $M_\kappa$ and $\mathbf e_{\kappa\nu}$ with no $\sum_\kappa$; it is a one-atom-per-cell formula (as stated for Eq. (57)).
- In Eq. (7) the factor $M_\kappa\omega^2_{\mathbf q\nu}$ is there only to cancel $M_\kappa$. For a real diatomic polar crystal the mass dependence enters through the reduced mass and the LO eigenvector, and cancels in the final $|g|$.
- To present Eq. (7) honestly, show the combined, mass-free $|g^{\rm Fr}|=\frac1{|\mathbf q|}[\frac{e^2\hbar\omega_{\rm LO}}{2\epsilon_0N_p\Omega}(\frac1{\epsilon^\infty}-\frac1{\epsilon^0})]^{1/2}$ (with the $N_p$ caveat of issue 1), valid for $\omega_{\mathbf q\nu}=\omega_{\rm LO}$ and $\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf e=|\mathbf q|$.

**4. "This result is derived in Sec. VI.A.3" (for Eq. (7)).** Sec. VI.A.3 gives only the general Eq. (180) and refers to Verdi & Giustino (2015) for the derivation. The reduction to Eq. (7) (LST plus the Born-charge expression for $\omega_{\rm LO}^2-\omega_{\rm TO}^2$) is not shown. Not an error, but a gap; I checked that the reduction works.

**5. Footnote 7 (p. 35, context).** Giustino notes that Eq. (4) of Verdi & Giustino (2015) is missing a factor $e^{-i(\mathbf q+\mathbf G)\cdot\boldsymbol\tau_\kappa}$, which is included in Eq. (180). Relevant because EPP compares against that PRL equation.
- The EPP manuscript also contains a note, in Croatian, that relative to Giustino a factor $e^2$ seems to be missing, "probably in $D$ or in $Z^*$". In SI-vs-Gaussian terms, Giustino's $\frac{4\pi}{\Omega}\frac{e^2}{4\pi\epsilon_0}$ with dimensionless $\mathbf Z^*$ equals EPP's $\frac{4\pi e}{\Omega}$ with $\hat Z^*$ carrying the charge.

**6. Bloch's $V_0=\hbar^2/(16m_ea_0^2)$.** With $a_0$ = Bohr radius this is a material-independent 1.70 eV. That is as stated in the review. I could not verify against Bloch's original whether $a_0$ there might be a lattice-related length. Present it "as quoted".

**7. Eq. (4) log notation.** "$\log|1+x|/|1-x|$" is to be read as $\ln\left(|1+x|/|1-x|\right)$ (natural log), multiplied by $(1-x^2)/(4x)$. Standard Lindhard; no error. The phrase "$\epsilon(q)\to(k_{TF}/q)^2$ for $q\to0$" uses $F(0)=1$ and drops the 1.

**8. Eq. (5).** $\epsilon(q,\omega)$ is complex for $\omega>0$ (Landau damping inside the particle–hole continuum). The text does not mention the imaginary part or how it should be used in $g$. Not an error, but worth noting when building V2.

**9. Eq. (6).** It is written with $\Omega$ as both the unit-cell volume and the variable of differentiation, and without the strain/polarization factor. In the Bloch form, $\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf e$ times displacement gives the dilation. Fine as a replacement rule $V_0\to E_1$ inside Eq. (2).

**10. Eq. (14) sign convention.** $\exp(+i\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf R_p)$ with $C_{\kappa\alpha0,\kappa'\alpha'p}$ is the Maradudin–Vosko convention and is consistent with Eq. (18). EPP may use the opposite sign or a $\mathbf k-\mathbf q$ convention, so the page should state one convention explicitly.

**11. Eqs. (8)–(10).** $\hat T$ is not invertible (it annihilates core states), so Eq. (9) is a singular generalized eigenproblem and $\tilde\psi$ is defined only up to core admixtures. This is the "freedom" the text mentions but does not spell out.
