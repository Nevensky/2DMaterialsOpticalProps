Dictionary between the two formalisms

Part A and Part B describe the same crystal with different bookkeeping: in Part A charges interact through the instantaneous Coulomb interaction, in Part B currents interact through the full electromagnetic field. This section translates between the two. Three substitutions, all consequences of charge conservation, carry most of the translation; a concept map places each object of one part in the other; dictionaries of units and of diagrams finish the job. The section ends with what the EPP formalism does not contain yet.

What you need: the screened interaction $W$, the irreducible polarizability $\chi^{\mathrm{irr}}$ and the phonon propagator $F$ of Section A.5; the Born charges and the long-range electron–phonon vertex of Section A.4; the phonon self-energy of Section A.6; and, from Section X.1, the temporal gauge, the free photon propagator with its longitudinal and transverse parts, the photon propagator $D$, the photon self-energy $\Pi$ and the light cone. From electrodynamics you need the continuity equation and Maxwell's equations; from quantum mechanics, the Heisenberg equation of motion and the electron operators $c^\dagger_{n\bk}$, $c_{n\bk}$ of the second-quantization box in Section A.2. A background box in the first subsection recalls how Feynman diagrams are read. Part B derives every translation used here, and Section X.1 already derived some of them. This section collects them, shows why each holds, and points to where each is derived.1

The picture: one crystal, two bookkeepings

Part A treats a crystal as electrons and nuclei that interact only through the instantaneous Coulomb interaction $v$. The vibrating nuclei appear as phonons, and the electrons screen every interaction. The result is the screened interaction $W=W^{\mathrm e}+W^{\mathrm{ph}}$ of Section A.5, : a part $W^{\mathrm e}$ screened by the electrons with the nuclei clamped, plus a part $W^{\mathrm{ph}}$ added by the moving nuclei. Every coupling in Part A is the energy of a charge in a scalar potential $A_0$, the electrostatic potential of Section X.1. In a diagram it is a charge vertex: a point where a Coulomb line ends on a charge. Light enters only from outside, as a classical field that drives the crystal (Section A.9).

Part B lets the same electrons and nuclei interact through the full electromagnetic field. It describes the field by the vector potential $\bA$ in the temporal gauge of Section X.1, the choice of potentials in which $A_0=0$ and the electric field is $\bE=-\frac1c\partial_t\bA$ (Section B.2). With no scalar potential, a charge couples to the field only through its current $\bj$, by the energy $-\frac1c\int\dd^3r\,\bj\cdot\bA$, so every coupling is a current in a vector potential. In a diagram it is a current vertex: a point where a photon line ends on a current. The field carries two things: the instantaneous Coulomb interaction, as its longitudinal part, and light, as its transverse part. As for the lattice waves of Section A.1 and the fields of Section X.1, longitudinal means along the wavevector $\bq$ and transverse means perpendicular to it. The phonons of Part B are built from the short-range force constants alone; the long-range forces between the dipoles of the ions are regenerated by the field.

Because the primer uses one notation in both parts, the dictionary maps objects and concepts rather than letters. Its backbone is charge conservation, which ties every charge to a current and gives three substitutions:

  1. the Coulomb line $v$ becomes the photon propagator $D$;
  2. the irreducible density response $\chi^{\mathrm{irr}}$ becomes the photon self-energy $\Pi$;
  3. the charge vertex becomes the current vertex.

The next subsection derives them, and collects them. The rest of the section places every object in a concept map, converts units between Gaussian and SI, matches the diagrams of the two parts, and lists what has no counterpart yet. The symbols of the two papers, which this primer replaces, appear in footnotes and, in this section only, in a source map at the end. Lines, bubbles and vertices are the elements of a Feynman diagram; the background box recalls what each stands for.

Reading a Feynman diagram skip if familiar

A Feynman diagram is a picture of one term of a perturbation series. Each element stands for a factor:

  • a line is a propagator, the amplitude for a particle or a disturbance of a field to travel from one point and time to another: the electron propagator $G$ (arrowed), the phonon propagator $F$ (zigzag), the Coulomb interaction $v$ (dashed) or the photon propagator $D$ (wavy, drawn over a dashed line when it carries both polarizations);
  • a vertex, where lines meet, is a coupling: the matrix element that turns the incoming particles into the outgoing ones, such as the electron–phonon matrix element $g$, where an electron absorbs or emits a phonon;
  • a bubble, a closed electron loop between two vertices, is a sum over electron–hole pairs: a response function such as the independent-particle response $\chi^0$ of Section A.3 or the electronic photon self-energy $\Pi^{\mathrm e}$;
  • a dressed line, heavy or doubled, is the sum of the bare line and the same line with one, two or more bubbles inserted; it obeys a Dyson equation, such as $W^{\mathrm e}=v+v\chi^{\mathrm{irr}}W^{\mathrm e}$.

Crystal momentum and energy are conserved at every vertex, and those of internal lines are summed over. This section uses diagrams as bookkeeping: the dictionary says which line, bubble and vertex of one part corresponds to which of the other.

Three substitutions from charge conservation

All three substitutions rest on one fact: charge is conserved. A charge density can change only if a current carries charge in or out, and for a wave this ties the charge to one part of the current.

Charge is the longitudinal part of a current

Take a current that oscillates as a plane wave. Where more current flows into a small region than out of it, charge piles up; where more flows out, charge drains away. A current along the wavevector changes its size along its own direction, so in every half wavelength the inflow and the outflow differ and a wave of charge builds up. A current perpendicular to the wavevector flows along the wavefronts. Along its own direction it has the same size everywhere, so inflow and outflow balance and no charge appears. shows both cases and every direction in between.

In equations, write the charge density and the current density of one Fourier component as $\rho(\br,t)=\mathrm{Re}\big[\rho(\bq,\omega)\,\ee^{\ii(\bq\cdot\br-\omega t)}\big]$ and $\bj(\br,t)=\mathrm{Re}\big[\bj(\bq,\omega)\,\ee^{\ii(\bq\cdot\br-\omega t)}\big]$, and insert them into the law of charge conservation:

  1. Charge conservation in differential form is the continuity equation, $\partial_t\rho+\nabla\cdot\bj=0$.

    The charge in a small volume changes only by what the current carries through its surface.

  2. For one Fourier component, $\partial_t$ acts as $-\ii\omega$ and $\nabla$ as $\ii\bq$, so $-\ii\omega\,\rho(\bq,\omega)+\ii\bq\cdot\bj(\bq,\omega)=0$.

    The equation is linear, so each Fourier component obeys it on its own, and the common factor $\ee^{\ii(\bq\cdot\br-\omega t)}$ cancels. The sign of $\omega$ follows the time dependence $\ee^{-\ii\omega t}$ used throughout this primer.

  3. Hence

    $$\begin{gathered}\omega\,\rho(\bq,\omega)=\bq\cdot\bj(\bq,\omega)\\=q\,\hat\bq\cdot\bj(\bq,\omega) .\end{gathered} \tag{X2.1}$$
    Only the component of the current along $\hat\bq$ appears.

Lines: the Coulomb line becomes the photon propagator

In Part A a charge creates a scalar potential and a second charge sits in it. By Poisson's equation, Gauss's law with $\bE=-\nabla A_0$, $q^2A_0(\bq)=4\pi\rho(\bq)$, so the potential per unit charge density is $4\pi/q^2=v(\bq)/e^2$, where $v(\bq)=4\pi e^2/q^2$ is the Coulomb energy of two charges $e$ (Section A.3). In Part B a current creates a vector potential and a second current couples to it. The photon propagator $D$ of Section X.1 is the field per unit current: an external current $\bj^{\mathrm{ext}}$ of wavevector $\bq$ and frequency $\omega$ produces the vector potential $A_\alpha(\bq,\omega)=\sum_\beta D_{\alpha\beta}(\bq,\omega)\,j^{\mathrm{ext}}_\beta(\bq,\omega)$, the response of the medium included. Without matter, $D$ is the free propagator $D^0$, which follows from Maxwell's equations. Section X.1 obtained it from the wave equation of $\bA$. Here its longitudinal part comes from the longitudinal component of the Ampère–Maxwell law, which shows that Gauss's law sets it:

  1. Take the component along $\hat\bq$ of $\nabla\times\bB=\frac{4\pi}{c}\bj+\frac1c\partial_t\bE$. In Fourier space the curl, $\ii\bq\times\bB$, is perpendicular to $\bq$, so

    $$0=\frac{4\pi}{c}\,\bj^{\mathrm L}-\frac{\ii\omega}{c}\,\bE^{\mathrm L},\qquad \bE^{\mathrm L}=-\frac{4\pi\ii}{\omega}\,\bj^{\mathrm L} .$$
    This is Gauss's law in another form: with , $\ii\bq\cdot\bE=4\pi\rho$ gives the same field. The longitudinal field is fixed by the charge, hence by the longitudinal current.

  2. In the temporal gauge $\bE=-\frac1c\partial_t\bA$, which for one Fourier component reads $\bE=\frac{\ii\omega}{c}\bA$.

    With $A_0=0$ the vector potential carries the whole field, the longitudinal part included.

  3. Combine the two: $\bA^{\mathrm L}=\frac{c}{\ii\omega}\bE^{\mathrm L}=-\frac{4\pi c}{\omega^2}\,\bj^{\mathrm L}$. Written as a tensor that acts on the whole current, this is the free longitudinal propagator $D^{0\mathrm L}$:

    $$D^{0\mathrm L}_{\alpha\beta}(\bq,\omega)=-\frac{4\pi c}{(\omega+\ii\eta)^2}\,\hat q_\alpha\hat q_\beta . \tag{X2.2}$$
    The projector $\hat q_\alpha\hat q_\beta$ keeps only $\bj^{\mathrm L}$. The replacement $\omega\to\omega+\ii\eta$, with $\eta\to0^+$ the positive infinitesimal of retarded functions, makes the field follow the current that produces it, as in Section X.1. The factor $1/\omega^2$ is not a resonance: in the temporal gauge the vector potential of a static charge grows linearly in time, $\bA^{\mathrm L}=-c\,\bE^{\mathrm L}t$, and the Fourier transform of a linear growth goes as $1/\omega^2$.

Now compare energies. A second current $\bj_2$ in the field $\bA_1=D\bj_1$ of a first one has the coupling energy $-\frac1c\,\bj_2^*\cdot\bA_1$ per Fourier component; this is the term $-\frac1c\int\dd^3r\,\bj\cdot\bA$ of the Hamiltonian (Section B.4). A charge $\rho_2$ in the scalar potential $A_{0,1}=(4\pi/q^2)\rho_1$ of a first charge has the Coulomb energy $\rho_2^*A_{0,1}$, with the same normalization. The frequency has dropped out, as it must for an instantaneous interaction. The longitudinal photon line between currents is therefore exactly the Coulomb line between charges. This is the first substitution, $D^{0\mathrm L}_{\alpha\beta}=-(c/\omega^2)\,q_\alpha q_\beta\,v(\bq)/e^2$. The EPP manuscript introduces it as a rule; Section B.4 derives it from Gauss's law, which turns the energy of the longitudinal field into the Coulomb energy.3

The transverse part of the same law gives the transverse propagator $D^{0\mathrm T}_{\alpha\beta}(\bq,\omega)$$\,=-4\pi c\,(\delta_{\alpha\beta}-\hat q_\alpha\hat q_\beta)/[(\omega+\ii\eta)^2-c^2q^2]$, met in Section X.1 and derived from the quantized field in Section B.5, . Its poles at $\omega=\pm cq$ are photons, waves that propagate on their own. Between two currents it carries the energy

$$-\frac1c\,\bj_2^*\cdot D^{0\mathrm T}\cdot\bj_1=\frac{4\pi\,\bj_2^{\mathrm T*}\cdot\bj_1^{\mathrm T}}{(\omega+\ii\eta)^2-c^2q^2} . \tag{X2.4}$$

Far outside the light cone of Section X.1, the region $q\lt\omega/c$ bounded by the light line $\omega=cq$, that is for $cq\gg\omega$, this is $-(4\pi/c^2q^2)\,\bj_2^{\mathrm T*}\cdot\bj_1^{\mathrm T}$: the magnetic attraction of parallel currents described by Ampère's law. Compared with the Coulomb energy $(4\pi/\omega^2)\,|\hat\bq\cdot\bj|^2$ of currents of similar size it is smaller by $(\omega/cq)^2$. For a phonon of 36 meV, $\omega/c=1.8\times10^{-5}$ Å⁻¹, so at $q=0.1$ Å⁻¹ the factor is $3\times10^{-8}$. Transverse photons matter only near and inside the light cone, $q\lesssim\omega/c$, the region that draws to scale. The sum $D^0=D^{0\mathrm L}+D^{0\mathrm T}$ is the propagator of Maxwell's equations in vacuum, in the temporal gauge (Section X.1).

With electrons present, the photon line is dressed by the electronic photon self-energy $\Pi^{\mathrm e}$. The dressed propagator $D^{\mathrm e}$ of Section X.1 obeys $D^{\mathrm e}=D^0+D^0\Pi^{\mathrm e}D^{\mathrm e}$, , just as the Coulomb line is dressed into $W^{\mathrm e}=v+v\chi^{\mathrm{irr}}W^{\mathrm e}$, .

Part B can also let a charge be the source. Section B.6 writes the propagator in the bookkeeping of the Coulomb gauge, with a scalar potential for the charges, and sorts it into four sectors: the scalar or the vector potential produced per unit external charge or per unit external current. The charge–charge sector $D^{\mathrm e}_{\rho\rho}$ is the scalar potential per unit external charge. An electron creates the potential $-eD^{\mathrm e}_{\rho\rho}$, so the energy between two electrons is $e^2D^{\mathrm e}_{\rho\rho}$, the electronically screened Coulomb interaction: $e^2D^{\mathrm e}_{\rho\rho}=W^{\mathrm e}$, exactly in an isotropic medium and in any crystal as $c\to\infty$.

Section X.1 found the longitudinal part of $D^{\mathrm e}$ equal to $W^{\mathrm e}$ in an isotropic medium, at any speed of light. For the whole tensor, in any crystal, the transverse and mixed parts must vanish, which needs the limit $c\to\infty$. Like , the longitudinal part grows in proportion to $c$, so the limit is taken of $D^{\mathrm e}/c$:

$$\lim_{c\to\infty}\frac{D^{\mathrm e}_{\alpha\beta}(\bq,\omega)}{c}=-\frac{q_\alpha q_\beta}{\omega^2}\,\frac{W^{\mathrm e}(\bq,\omega)}{e^2} . \tag{X2.5}$$

This is with the bare Coulomb line replaced by the screened one. The factor $1/e^2$ appears because this primer puts the charges into $v$ and $W$, while the photon propagator acts on currents that already contain them.4

Bubbles: the density response becomes the photon self-energy

A density response says how much charge a scalar potential induces; a photon self-energy says how much current a vector potential induces. The photon self-energy $\Pi$ of Section X.1 gives the current induced by the total field, $j^{\mathrm{ind}}_\alpha=\sum_\beta\Pi_{\alpha\beta}A_\beta$, . It is the irreducible response of the current, the counterpart of the irreducible polarizability $\chi^{\mathrm{irr}}$ for the charge. By the induced charge is fixed by the longitudinal part of the induced current, which ties the two together. For the electrons:

  1. A scalar potential $A_0$ of wavevector $\bq$ produces the field $\bE^{\mathrm L}=-\ii\bq A_0$. The longitudinal vector potential $\bA^{\mathrm L}=-(c/\omega)\,\bq A_0$ produces the same field, $\bE^{\mathrm L}=\frac{\ii\omega}{c}\bA^{\mathrm L}$.

    The two descriptions differ by a change of gauge, and only the field acts on the charges. Part A describes the field by $A_0$, Part B by $\bA^{\mathrm L}$.

  2. The electrons answer with the induced current $\bj^{\mathrm{ind}}=\Pi^{\mathrm e}\bA^{\mathrm L}=-\frac{c}{\omega}\,\Pi^{\mathrm e}\bq\,A_0$.

    The definition of $\Pi$, applied to the potential of step 1 and to the electronic part $\Pi^{\mathrm e}$ of $\Pi=\Pi^{\mathrm e}+\Pi^{\mathrm{ion}}$.

  3. Continuity turns it into an induced charge: $\rho^{\mathrm{ind}}=\bq\cdot\bj^{\mathrm{ind}}/\omega=-\frac{c}{\omega^2}\,(\bq\cdot\Pi^{\mathrm e}\bq)\,A_0$.

    The charge depends only on the longitudinal projection $\bq\cdot\Pi^{\mathrm e}\bq$ of the tensor $\Pi^{\mathrm e}$.

  4. In Part A the same induced charge is $\rho^{\mathrm{ind}}=e^2\chi^{\mathrm{irr}}A_0$ for the electrons.

    An electron in the potential $A_0$ has the potential energy $-eA_0$. The electron density changes by $\chi^{\mathrm{irr}}$ times this energy (Section A.5), and the charge density by $-e$ times the density change.

Comparing steps 3 and 4 identifies the charge–charge correlator $\Pi^{\mathrm e}_{\rho\rho}$ of the electrons, the induced charge per unit potential, with the polarizability of Part A in charge units:

$$\begin{gathered}\Pi^{\mathrm e}_{\rho\rho}(\bq,\omega)=e^2\chi^{\mathrm{irr}}(\bq,\omega)\\=-\frac{c}{\omega^2}\sum_{\alpha\beta}q_\alpha\,\Pi^{\mathrm e}_{\alpha\beta}(\bq,\omega)\,q_\beta .\end{gathered} \tag{X2.6}$$

Section X.1 derived the second equality in the same way. Applied to the dielectric function of the electrons, $\varepsilon^{\mathrm e}$$\,=1-v\chi^{\mathrm{irr}}$ of , the same identity gives

$$\begin{gathered}\varepsilon^{\mathrm e}(\bq,\omega)=1-v(\bq)\,\chi^{\mathrm{irr}}(\bq,\omega)\\=1+\frac{4\pi c}{\omega^2}\,\hat\bq\cdot\Pi^{\mathrm e}(\bq,\omega)\,\hat\bq .\end{gathered} \tag{X2.7}$$

The second form is the textbook relation $\varepsilon=1+4\pi\ii\sigma/\omega$ of a medium with conductivity $\sigma$: since $\bj=\sigma\bE=\sigma\frac{\ii\omega}{c}\bA$, the conductivity and the self-energy are related by $\Pi=\ii\omega\sigma/c$. The polarizability of Part A is thus the longitudinal projection of the self-energy of Part B. The transverse projection, the current induced by a transverse field, has no Part A counterpart. Part B also puts the ions into the same object, $\Pi=\Pi^{\mathrm e}+\Pi^{\mathrm{ion}}$, where $\Pi^{\mathrm{ion}}$ is the current response of the vibrating Born-charge dipoles, of Section B.5; Part A brings the lattice in through $W^{\mathrm{ph}}$ instead.5

Two conditions for the identity

First, $\Pi$ must contain the diamagnetic term: the contribution of the term $e^2A^2/2m_{\mathrm e}c^2$ that minimal coupling, $(\hat{\mathbf p}+\frac ec\bA)^2/2m_{\mathrm e}$ for an electron of mass $m_{\mathrm e}$ and momentum $\hat{\mathbf p}$, adds to the kinetic energy. Without it $\Pi$ does not vanish at $\omega=0$: a static longitudinal vector potential, which is a pure gauge and carries no electric field, would induce a current with $\bq\cdot\bj\neq0$ while $\omega\rho=0$. That current violates continuity, and fails; Section B.5 shows how the diamagnetic term restores it, . Second, irreducible must match irreducible: $\chi^{\mathrm{irr}}$ contains no Coulomb line that cuts it in two, and $\Pi$ no photon line. The reducible response $\chi$ of Part A contains Coulomb lines; it sits in the dressed charge–charge sector, $e^2D^{\mathrm e}_{\rho\rho}=W^{\mathrm e}=v+v\chi v$ for the electrons, not in $\Pi$.

Vertices: the charge vertex becomes the current vertex

An electron that scatters between the Bloch states $n\bk$ and $m\bk+\bq$ feels a scalar potential through the charge of the transition and a vector potential through its current. Let $\hat\rho_\bq=\int\dd^3r\,\ee^{-\ii\bq\cdot\br}\hat\rho(\br)$ and $\hat\bj_\bq$ be the Fourier components of the operators of the electronic charge density and current density. For the Bloch states $\psi_{n\bk}$ of Section A.2, the charge vertex of the transition is the matrix element

$$\rho^{\,n\bk,m\bk+\bq}=\langle\psi_{n\bk}|\hat\rho_\bq|\psi_{m\bk+\bq}\rangle=-e\,\langle\psi_{n\bk}|\ee^{-\ii\bq\cdot\br}|\psi_{m\bk+\bq}\rangle ,$$

and the current vertex $\bj^{\,n\bk,m\bk+\bq}$, with components $j_\alpha^{\,n\bk,m\bk+\bq}$, is the matrix element of $\hat\bj_\bq$ between the same states, of Section B.5. Continuity ties them together:

  1. Write both operators in the Bloch basis: $\hat\rho_\bq=\sum_{nm\bk}\rho^{\,n\bk,m\bk+\bq}\,c^\dagger_{n\bk}c_{m\bk+\bq}$, and $\hat\bj_\bq$ in the same way with $\bj^{\,n\bk,m\bk+\bq}$.

    A one-body operator in second quantization is a sum of $c^\dagger c$ weighted by its matrix elements. By Bloch's theorem the factor $\ee^{-\ii\bq\cdot\br}$ connects $\bk+\bq$ only to $\bk$ (up to a reciprocal lattice vector, dropped here as in Part B).

  2. Take the Hamiltonian of independent electrons in their bands, $H_0$$\,=\sum_{n\bk}E_{n\bk}\,c^\dagger_{n\bk}c_{n\bk}$, the electronic part of the free Hamiltonian of Part B. Each term of $\hat\rho_\bq$ obeys

    $$\begin{gathered}\big[H_0,c^\dagger_{n\bk}c_{m\bk+\bq}\big]\\=\big(E_{n\bk}-E_{m\bk+\bq}\big)\,c^\dagger_{n\bk}c_{m\bk+\bq} .\end{gathered}$$
    $c^\dagger_{n\bk}$ adds the energy $E_{n\bk}$ and $c_{m\bk+\bq}$ removes $E_{m\bk+\bq}$.

  3. The Heisenberg equation gives $\partial_t\hat\rho_\bq=\frac{\ii}{\hbar}[H_0,\hat\rho_\bq]$, and continuity gives $\partial_t\hat\rho_\bq=-\ii\bq\cdot\hat\bj_\bq$.

    The continuity equation holds for the operators as it does for their averages, and in Fourier space $\nabla\cdot$ becomes $\ii\bq\cdot$. Both statements use the same $H_0$, so the current must be that of the independent electrons whose motion $H_0$ generates; a local one-body potential guarantees it.

  4. Compare the coefficients of each $c^\dagger_{n\bk}c_{m\bk+\bq}$:

    $$\begin{gathered}\frac{\ii}{\hbar}\big(E_{n\bk}-E_{m\bk+\bq}\big)\,\rho^{\,n\bk,m\bk+\bq}\\=-\ii\bq\cdot\bj^{\,n\bk,m\bk+\bq},\end{gathered}$$

    that is

    $$\begin{gathered}\bq\cdot\bj^{\,n\bk,m\bk+\bq}\\=\frac{E_{m\bk+\bq}-E_{n\bk}}{\hbar}\,\rho^{\,n\bk,m\bk+\bq} .\end{gathered} \tag{X2.8}$$
    This is for a single transition, with $\omega$ replaced by the transition frequency.

The charge vertex is fixed by the longitudinal part of the current vertex: it is $\bq\cdot\bj^{\,n\bk,m\bk+\bq}$ divided by the transition frequency $(E_{m\bk+\bq}-E_{n\bk})/\hbar$. Here a factor of the charge is easily lost: $\hat\rho$ must be the charge density, not the number density, because $\hat\bj$ contains the charge. The EPP manuscript writes the relation without that factor; is the corrected form, which Section B.8 uses.6

Write $\bj_\nu(\bq)$ for the vector of its elements with $\bG=0$, $j_{\alpha\nu}(\bq)$. Its projection on $\bq$,

$$\bq\cdot\bj_\nu(\bq)=e\sum_\kappa\frac{\bq\cdot\mathbf Z^*_\kappa\cdot\be_{\kappa\nu}(\bq)}{\sqrt{M_\kappa}}\,\ee^{-\ii\bq\cdot\btau_\kappa} ,$$

To translate a Part A expression, replace each Coulomb line by a photon line, each polarizability bubble by a photon self-energy and each charge vertex by a current vertex, then add the transverse parts, which have no Part A counterpart. Going back: the electrostatic limit

The inverse translation is the electrostatic limit: $c\to\infty$ at fixed $\bq$ and $\omega$. Three things happen.

  1. The transverse line vanishes: $D^{0\mathrm T}_{\alpha\beta}\to4\pi(\delta_{\alpha\beta}-\hat q_\alpha\hat q_\beta)/cq^2\to0$.

    For $cq\gg\omega$ the denominator of $D^{0\mathrm T}$ is $-c^2q^2$, which grows faster than the prefactor $4\pi c$.

  2. The longitudinal line $D^{0\mathrm L}$ grows as $c$, but the energy it carries between two currents, $-\frac1c\,\bj_2^*\cdot D^{0\mathrm L}\cdot\bj_1$ of , contains one factor $1/c$ and keeps its value.

    A current couples to the field through $-\frac1c\bj\cdot\bA$, and the Coulomb energy of charges does not depend on $c$.

  3. The self-energy falls as $1/c$, so a transverse line next to a bubble, $D^{0\mathrm T}\Pi$, falls as $1/c^2$, while $D^{0\mathrm L}\Pi$ stays finite.

  • Part A starts from adiabatic phonons, computed with the electrons in their ground state for every frozen position of the nuclei, as density-functional perturbation theory does. Their force constants contain the static screening by the electrons and, in a polar crystal, the long-range dipole–dipole part $\Phi^{\mathrm{dd}}$ of , which splits the LO frequency from the TO frequency. The phonon self-energy of Section A.6 therefore has its static part removed, because these phonons already contain it, .
  • Part B starts from bare phonons, built from the short-range force constants $\Phi^{\mathrm{sr}}$ alone. A polar mode at long wavelength sits near the TO frequency. The two starting points must not be mixed: phonons from density-functional perturbation theory, dressed with the self-energy of Part B, would count the dipole–dipole forces twice. The starting point also sets the size of every electron–phonon vertex, because the zero-point amplitude $(\hbar/2M_\kappa\omega_{\bq\nu})^{1/2}$ contains the reference frequency. Units: Gaussian against SI

This primer writes every equation in Gaussian units, with $e$, $\hbar$, $m_{\mathrm e}$ and the speed of light $c$ kept explicit (Conventions). The review uses SI units throughout. The EPP manuscript uses SI units for the static polar coupling and Gaussian units from its field theory on, and its Born charges carry the charge, $eZ^*$, where this primer's are dimensionless. Three rules convert a Gaussian formula to SI:

  1. in every Coulomb energy, $e^2\to e^2/4\pi\varepsilon_0$, where $\varepsilon_0$ is the vacuum permittivity;
  2. in every source equation (Poisson, Gauss, Maxwell), $4\pi\to1/\varepsilon_0$;
  3. the vector potential is $\bA_{\mathrm{Gauss}}=c\,\bA_{\mathrm{SI}}$, so $\bE=-\frac1c\partial_t\bA$ becomes $\bE=-\partial_t\bA$ and the coupling $-\frac1c\bj\cdot\bA$ becomes $-\bj\cdot\bA$.

In numbers, the Gaussian $e^2=14.3996$ eV Å equals $e^2/4\pi\varepsilon_0$ in SI, so $e^2/\varepsilon_0=180.95$ eV Å; the Gaussian charge is $e=3.7947\ (\text{eV Å})^{1/2}$, and $\hbar c=1973.27$ eV Å. Its ionic plasma frequency $\omega^{\mathrm{ion}}_{\mathrm p}$ is that of a two-atom cell with Born charges $\pm Z^*$, reduced mass $M_{\mathrm r}$ and cell volume $\OV$, derived in Section B.3. With electronic screening the same limit gives 35.9 meV, which equals the LO frequency computed with the same Born charge (the measured one is 36.2 meV) but still belongs to a transverse mode; the LO–TO splitting of phonons with $q\gg\omega/c$ is the electrostatic result.   • Longitudinal photon. The propagator of the quantized longitudinal photon vanishes, while the longitudinal line $D^{0\mathrm L}$ is the Coulomb interaction . Both hold: the energy of the longitudinal field is moved into the Coulomb term of the Hamiltonian (Section B.4).
