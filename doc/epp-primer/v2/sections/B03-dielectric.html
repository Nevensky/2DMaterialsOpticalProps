The polar crystal as a dielectric

Shine infrared light on a polar crystal and its cations move one way while its anions move the other, so every cell becomes an oscillating dipole. This section drives the diatomic crystal of Section B.2 with a uniform field and derives, step by step, the dielectric function $\varepsilon(\omega)$ that sums up the response: a Lorentz oscillator set by the Born charge, on top of the constant screening of the electrons. Its zeros are the longitudinal optical phonons and its poles the transverse ones. The Lyddane–Sachs–Teller relation, the Reststrahlen band of near-perfect reflection and the phonon part of the screened interaction $W$ all follow from this one function.

What you need: the equation of motion of an ion from Section B.2, , in which every ion is a harmonic oscillator driven by the electric field through its Born charge, and the driven two-atom crystal that Section B.2 solves with it; the diatomic chain of Section A.1 and its optical mode at $\bq=0$ (); the Born effective charges, the polarization of a lattice wave, the LO–TO splitting and the Lyddane–Sachs–Teller relation of Section A.4; and the screened interaction $W$, with its split into an electronic and a phonon part, from Section A.5. Section A.5 also wrote the dielectric function of a polar insulator in a few lines. This section derives it step by step from the Lagrangian of Section B.2, as the EPP manuscript does, and adds what experiments see: why its zeros are the longitudinal and its poles the transverse optical modes, the role of the local field, the reflectance and the energy-loss spectrum. The electrodynamics of dielectrics is assumed; the background boxes “Driven oscillators and complex response” and “Fields in a dielectric, in Gaussian units” recall what is used. Everything is classical: $\hbar$ enters only at the end, when the lattice part of $W$ is written with phonons.1

The picture: a crystal that polarizes

In a polar crystal (Section A.4) the atoms of the basis carry Born charges of opposite sign: gallium and arsenic in GaAs, sodium and chlorine in NaCl. An electric field pushes the cations along the field and the anions against it. Each cell then carries a dipole, and the dipoles of all cells add up to a polarization $\bP$, the dipole per unit volume. Infrared light is the natural probe. Its wavelength at phonon frequencies, tens of micrometres (37 µm for the optical phonon of GaAs), is about $6.5\times10^4$ times the cubic lattice constant (5.65 Å for GaAs), so the field is uniform over many cells and pushes every cell the same way.

The polarization does not stay passive. Wherever $\bP$ changes in space, the displaced charges of neighbouring cells no longer cancel. The polarization charge they leave behind creates a field of its own, which acts back on the ions. The dielectric function $\varepsilon(\omega)$ of Section A.5 describes the whole response: it gives the polarization that a field oscillating at frequency $\omega$ produces, where the field is the total field, including the part that the polarization charges create. Before quantizing anything, the EPP manuscript checks that the Lagrangian of Section B.2 reproduces this classical optics of polar crystals. This section follows that check:

  1. reduce the ionic equations to two masses joined by one spring and driven by one field;
  2. solve them: the lattice responds like a single damped oscillator, a Lorentz oscillator;
  3. add the electrons, which gives $\varepsilon(\omega)$;
  4. read the longitudinal and transverse optical modes off the zeros and poles of $\varepsilon(\omega)$, derive the Lyddane–Sachs–Teller relation and settle the role of the local field;
  5. compute what experiments see: the reflectance, the energy loss, and the lattice part of the screened interaction $W$.

Two masses, one spring and one field

Section B.2 derived the motion of each ion from the Lagrangian of electrons, ions and the field, . Atom $\kappa$ of cell $\ell$ is a harmonic oscillator, held to its neighbours by the short-range part of the force constants and driven by the total electric field at its site through its Born charge:2

All cells in step

Consider a driving field, or a lattice wave, whose wavelength $2\pi/q$, with $q$ the length of its wavevector $\bq$, is much longer than the cell. Neighbouring cells then move almost identically. Over a region much smaller than the wavelength but still containing many cells, every cell moves in step and the field is uniform. From region to region the displacements and the field vary slowly, as a plane wave $\ee^{\ii\bq\cdot\br}$; that slow variation matters later, when longitudinal and transverse waves are told apart. Inside one region the crystal behaves as a single two-atom molecule per cell, repeated in lockstep. shows such a region driven by an oscillating field.

Section B.2 carried out this reduction for a crystal with two atoms per cell, $\kappa=1,2$, on sites of cubic symmetry, as in zincblende GaAs or rocksalt NaCl. Three facts make it short.3

  1. With every cell alike, only the lattice sums of the force constants enter.

    The displacement $u_{\ell'\kappa'\beta}$ of the partner atom is then the same in every cell $\ell'$, so it comes out of the sum over $\ell'$, which acts on the force constants alone. By translation invariance that sum gives the force constants at $\bq=0$, the lattice Fourier transform $\Phi_{\kappa\alpha,\kappa'\beta}(\bq)$ of with every phase factor equal to 1.

  2. On sites of cubic symmetry the three directions decouple, and the Born charge of each atom is a number, $Z^*_{\kappa,\alpha\beta}=Z^*_\kappa\,\delta_{\alpha\beta}$.

    The $\bq=0$ force-constant blocks and the Born charges are $3\times3$ tensors attached to a site, unchanged by the rotations that leave the site in place, and a tensor unchanged by the rotations of a cubic site is a multiple of the unit tensor. The equations can then be written for the vectors $\bu_\kappa$ and $\bE$. A uniaxial crystal such as hBN has one special axis, normal to its layers; its tensors are diagonal in axes along and across that axis, the principal axes, and the same reduction holds along each of them, with constants of its own.

  3. One stiffness is left, the short-range spring $K$ of Section B.2 between the two sublattices.

    A rigid translation of both sublattices stretches no spring, so each row of the lattice-summed force constants adds up to zero (the acoustic sum rule ), and their symmetry makes the two cross sums equal. Atom 1 then feels $-K(\bu_1-\bu_2)$ and atom 2 the opposite.

For the two atoms of every cell in the region this leaves

$$\begin{aligned}M_1\ddot\bu_1&=-K(\bu_1-\bu_2)+eZ^*_1\bE(t),\\ M_2\ddot\bu_2&=-K(\bu_2-\bu_1)+eZ^*_2\bE(t).\end{aligned} \tag{B3.1}$$
$K$
lattice-summed short-range spring between the two sublattices (eV/Å²), as in Section B.2; its resonance with the reduced mass $M_{\mathrm r}$ of Section A.1 is $\omega_0=(K/M_{\mathrm r})^{1/2}$
$\bu_1$, $\bu_2$
displacements of the two atoms, the same in every cell of a region (Å)
$Z^*_\kappa$
Born charge of atom $\kappa$ on a cubic site, the number that multiplies the unit tensor (dimensionless)
$\bE(t)$
electric field at the atoms, uniform over the region (V/Å)

This is the crystal reduced to two masses, one spring and one field. For the diatomic chain of Section A.1, with its springs $K_1$ and $K_2$, the same steps give $K=K_1+K_2$. At $\bq=0$ every cell moves alike, so the two springs attached to an atom change length by the same amount $|u_1-u_2|$, one stretched and the other compressed. Both forces on the atom point against $u_1-u_2$, so the springs act in parallel. The optical frequency of that chain at $\bq=0$, $\omega_+$ with $\omega_+^2=(K_1+K_2)/M_{\mathrm r}$, is the frequency of without the field.

One more fact fixes the charges. Moving the whole crystal creates no dipole, so the Born charges of a cell add up to zero, the sum rule of Section A.4. With two atoms, $Z^*_2=-Z^*_1$, and one number is left: the single Born charge $Z^*$ of Section A.4, $Z^*\equiv Z^*_1$, the charge of the cation. In GaAs, $Z^*=2.07$ for gallium.4

Which field acts on an ion

The field in is the total field at the atoms. It is not quite the field of Maxwell's equations for a medium. Those use the macroscopic field $\bE$ of Section A.4: the microscopic field averaged over a unit cell around each point, which keeps only variations on scales much longer than the cell. The field at an atom, the local field of Section B.2, differs from this average by the field of the nearby dipoles and of the electron cloud around the atom, which the average smears out. That difference is the local-field correction.

The main text keeps only the macroscopic field. So does the photon-exchange theory of Sections B.5–B.7, which keeps only the Fourier component of the field with zero reciprocal-lattice vector. The local-field correction is proportional to the polarization, and so to the displacements of the ions; it acts on them like an extra spring. The subsection Local field versus macroscopic field adds it, as the EPP manuscript does. It changes only the frequency that the short-range springs would give on their own, which cannot be measured, and the dielectric function written with the measured frequencies comes out the same. Density-functional perturbation theory makes the same split. At $\bq=0$ it computes the force constants at zero macroscopic field, local fields included, and adds the macroscopic field afterwards as the nonanalytic term of Section A.4; its Born charge, , is defined at zero macroscopic field too.

A Lorentz oscillator

A field pushes the two ions of a cell with equal and opposite forces, $eZ^*\bE$ and $-eZ^*\bE$. The centre of mass of the cell feels no net force, and only the bond between the ions is stretched. Two coordinates separate the two motions: the centre-of-mass displacement $\bu_{\mathrm c}=(M_1\bu_1+M_2\bu_2)/M_{\mathrm c}$, with the cell mass $M_{\mathrm c}=M_1+M_2$, and the relative displacement $\bu_{\mathrm{rel}}=\bu_1-\bu_2$ of the cation with respect to the anion (the vector form of the $u_{\mathrm{rel}}$ of Section B.2). Section B.2 separated them for a neutral cell; with the charges kept general, the steps are:

  1. Add the two equations of : $M_{\mathrm c}\ddot\bu_{\mathrm c}=e(Z^*_1+Z^*_2)\bE=0$.

    The spring forces on the two atoms are equal and opposite and cancel, and so do the field forces on a neutral cell. A uniform field cannot move the centre of mass, so the acoustic motion is not driven.

  2. Divide each equation by its mass and subtract the second from the first: $\ddot\bu_{\mathrm{rel}}=-K\big(\tfrac1{M_1}+\tfrac1{M_2}\big)\bu_{\mathrm{rel}}$ $+\,eZ^*\big(\tfrac1{M_1}+\tfrac1{M_2}\big)\bE$.

    The field terms add because $Z^*_2=-Z^*_1$: the field pushes the two atoms in opposite directions. The combination $1/M_1+1/M_2=1/M_{\mathrm r}$ is the inverse reduced mass of Section A.1, as in its optical mode.

  3. Add a friction term $-M_{\mathrm r}\gamma\dot\bu_{\mathrm{rel}}$, with a damping rate $\gamma$.

    An optical phonon decays, mainly into pairs of acoustic phonons through the anharmonic terms that Section A.1 dropped, and it scatters off defects. A friction term is the simplest model of that decay, and it keeps the response finite at resonance. In GaAs at room temperature $\hbar\gamma\approx0.3$ meV, a hundredth of the phonon energy.

The result is one damped oscillator for the relative coordinate, while the centre of mass stays undriven:

$$\begin{aligned}M_{\mathrm c}\,\ddot\bu_{\mathrm c}&=0,\\ M_{\mathrm r}\big(\ddot\bu_{\mathrm{rel}}+\gamma\,\dot\bu_{\mathrm{rel}}\big)&=-K\,\bu_{\mathrm{rel}}\\ &\quad+eZ^*\bE .\end{aligned} \tag{B3.2}$$
$\bu_{\mathrm c}$, $M_{\mathrm c}$
displacement of the centre of mass of a cell (Å); cell mass $M_1+M_2$ (amu)
$\bu_{\mathrm{rel}}$
relative displacement $\bu_1-\bu_2$ of the cation with respect to the anion (Å)
$M_{\mathrm r}$
reduced mass, $1/M_{\mathrm r}=1/M_1+1/M_2$ (amu): 36.1 amu for GaAs
$\gamma$
damping rate of the optical vibration (s⁻¹; $\hbar\gamma$ in meV); it is also the full width at half maximum of the resonance in angular frequency, twice the half width $\gamma_{\bq\nu}$ of Section A.6

Had the Born charges not added up to zero, the first equation would read $M_{\mathrm c}\ddot\bu_{\mathrm c}=e(Z^*_1+Z^*_2)\bE$. The field would then accelerate the whole crystal like a gas of mobile charges, and the response would grow as $1/\omega^2$ at low frequency, as in a metal. An insulator has no such response, and the sum rule of Section A.4 guarantees that the model has none. The derivation box below finds the same cancellation in the full $2\times2$ solution, the form in which the EPP manuscript writes it.

Driven oscillators and complex response skip if familiar

A mass $M$ on a spring of natural frequency $\omega_0$, with damping rate $\gamma$, obeys $M(\ddot x+\gamma\dot x+\omega_0^2x)=\mathcal F(t)$, where $x$ is its displacement and $\mathcal F$ the applied force. For a force at one frequency, $\mathcal F(t)=\mathrm{Re}\big[\mathcal F\,\ee^{-\ii\omega t}\big]$, the steady motion is $x(t)=\mathrm{Re}\big[x\,\ee^{-\ii\omega t}\big]$ with the complex amplitude

$$x=\frac{\mathcal F}{M\,(\omega_0^2-\omega^2-\ii\gamma\omega)} ,$$

because each time derivative multiplies $\ee^{-\ii\omega t}$ by $-\ii\omega$. The factor that multiplies $\mathcal F$ is a response function. Its modulus is the size of the motion per unit force and its phase is the lag behind the force. Well below $\omega_0$ the mass follows the force in phase; at $\omega_0$ the response is $\ii/(M\gamma\omega_0)$, a lag of 90°, with a height $1/(M\gamma\omega_0)$ that grows as the damping falls; well above $\omega_0$ the mass moves against the force, a lag of 180°.

The force does work at the time-averaged rate $\langle\mathcal F\dot x\rangle=\frac{\omega}2\,\mathrm{Im}\big[x/\mathcal F\big]\,|\mathcal F|^2$, so with time dependence $\ee^{-\ii\omega t}$ a positive imaginary part of the response function means absorption. As a function of complex $\omega$, the response has its poles at $\omega=-\ii\gamma/2\pm(\omega_0^2-\gamma^2/4)^{1/2}$, in the lower half plane. That placement expresses causality: the motion follows the force, never precedes it, and a response with this property is called retarded. When the damping is negligible, it is kept as a positive infinitesimal $\eta$, by writing $\omega+\ii\eta$ for $\omega$. In this section the oscillator is the relative coordinate: $M=M_{\mathrm r}$, $\omega_0^2=K/M_{\mathrm r}$ and $\mathcal F=eZ^*E$.

The driven response

Drive the crystal at one frequency. As in Section A.1, complex amplitudes are convenient: $\bE(t)=\mathrm{Re}\big[\bE\,\ee^{-\ii\omega t}\big]$ and $\bu_{\mathrm{rel}}(t)=\mathrm{Re}\big[\boldsymbol\xi_{\mathrm{rel}}\,\ee^{-\ii\omega t}\big]$, and from here on $\bE$, $\bP$ and $\mathbf D$ stand for complex amplitudes at the frequency $\omega$. The polarization and the ionic plasma frequency

Each cell now carries the dipole $eZ^*_1\bu_1+eZ^*_2\bu_2=eZ^*\bu_{\mathrm{rel}}$, which depends on the relative displacement only. Dividing by the cell volume $\OV$ gives the polarization of the lattice, the $\bq\to0$ form of . With ,

$$\begin{gathered}\begin{aligned}\bP^{\ion}&=\frac{eZ^*}{\OV}\,\boldsymbol\xi_{\mathrm{rel}}\\ &=\frac{\big(\omega_{\mathrm p}^{\ion}\big)^2}{4\pi}\,\frac{\bE}{\omega_{\mathrm{TO}}^2-\omega^2-\ii\gamma\omega},\end{aligned}\\ \big(\omega_{\mathrm p}^{\ion}\big)^2=\frac{4\pi e^2Z^{*2}}{\OV\,M_{\mathrm r}} .\end{gathered} \tag{B3.4}$$
$\bP^{\ion}$
ionic polarization: the dipole of the displaced ions per unit volume
$\omega_{\mathrm p}^{\ion}$
ionic plasma frequency, here for particles of charge $eZ^*$, mass $M_{\mathrm r}$ and density $1/\OV$
$\OV$
volume of the unit cell (Å³): $a^3/4=45.2$ Å³ for GaAs, where $a=5.653$ Å is the edge of the conventional cubic cell, which holds four unit cells

This is a Lorentz oscillator, the classical model of a resonance in a dielectric, due to Lorentz. The polarization follows the field in phase well below $\omega_{\mathrm{TO}}$, lags by 90° at $\omega_{\mathrm{TO}}$, and points against the field above it. Its strength is set by $\omega_{\mathrm p}^{\ion}$.

A plasma frequency is the frequency at which a gas of charged particles oscillates in its own field against a rigid background of opposite charge; its square is $4\pi$ times the density times the squared charge, divided by the mass. is that formula with one charge $eZ^*$ of mass $M_{\mathrm r}$ per cell. It is the ionic plasma frequency of Section A.5, which met it in jellium with mobile ions and, in this Born-charge form, in its polar insulator. Here it is the frequency at which the two sublattices would oscillate against each other in a longitudinal wave, held only by their own field, with no springs and no electrons.

In numbers, with $e^2=14.3996$ eV Å from the conventions section, so that $4\pi e^2=180.95$ eV Å,

$$\hbar\omega_{\mathrm p}^{\ion}=64.654\ \text{meV}\times\bigg(\frac{180.95\,Z^{*2}}{\OV[\text{Å}^3]\;M_{\mathrm r}[\text{amu}]}\bigg)^{1/2} .$$

For GaAs ($Z^*=2.07$, $\OV=45.17$ Å³, $M_{\mathrm r}=36.11$ amu) it is 44.6 meV, or 360 cm⁻¹; for NaCl ($Z^*=1.12$, $\OV=44.85$ Å³, $M_{\mathrm r}=13.95$ amu), 38.9 meV. Both are of the same size as the phonon energies: the field of the dipoles matters as much as the springs. Section A.5 quotes 46.9 meV for GaAs. That value is the one the measured LO and TO frequencies imply through below, and it corresponds to $Z^*=2.18$ rather than 2.07.5

Derivation: the driven two-mass problem with arbitrary charges

Keep $Z^*_1$ and $Z^*_2$ independent to see what neutrality does.6

  1. Insert $\bu_\kappa(t)=\mathrm{Re}\big[\boldsymbol\xi_\kappa\ee^{-\ii\omega t}\big]$ into , without damping:

    $$\begin{gathered}\begin{pmatrix}K-M_1\omega^2&-K\\-K&K-M_2\omega^2\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol\xi_1\\ \boldsymbol\xi_2\end{pmatrix}\\ =e\bE\begin{pmatrix}Z^*_1\\ Z^*_2\end{pmatrix} .\end{gathered}$$
    Two time derivatives give $-\omega^2$; the spring terms are moved to the left.

  2. The determinant of the matrix, with $\omega_{\mathrm{TO}}^2=K/M_{\mathrm r}$, is

    $$\begin{gathered}(K-M_1\omega^2)(K-M_2\omega^2)-K^2\\ =M_1M_2\,\omega^2(\omega^2-\omega_{\mathrm{TO}}^2) .\end{gathered}$$
    Its zeros are the free vibrations: $\omega=0$, the rigid acoustic translation with $\boldsymbol\xi_1=\boldsymbol\xi_2$, and $\omega_{\mathrm{TO}}$, the optical vibration with $M_1\boldsymbol\xi_1+M_2\boldsymbol\xi_2=0$.

  3. Invert the $2\times2$ matrix and form the dipole per cell, $e(Z^*_1\boldsymbol\xi_1+Z^*_2\boldsymbol\xi_2)$:

    $$\begin{aligned}&e\big(Z^*_1\boldsymbol\xi_1+Z^*_2\boldsymbol\xi_2\big)\\ &=e^2\bE\,\frac{K(Z^*_1+Z^*_2)^2}{M_1M_2\,\omega^2(\omega^2-\omega_{\mathrm{TO}}^2)}\\ &\quad-e^2\bE\,\frac{M_2Z^{*2}_1+M_1Z^{*2}_2}{M_1M_2\,(\omega^2-\omega_{\mathrm{TO}}^2)} .\end{aligned}$$
    The inverse gives $\boldsymbol\xi_1=e\bE\big[K(Z^*_1+Z^*_2)-M_2\omega^2Z^*_1\big]/\det$ and $\boldsymbol\xi_2=e\bE\big[K(Z^*_1+Z^*_2)-M_1\omega^2Z^*_2\big]/\det$. The factor $\omega^2$ of the determinant cancels in the second term only.

  4. For a neutral cell, $Z^*_2=-Z^*_1=-Z^*$, the first term vanishes and the second becomes $e^2Z^{*2}\bE/[M_{\mathrm r}(\omega_{\mathrm{TO}}^2-\omega^2)]$. Divided by $\OV$, this is without damping.

    $(M_1+M_2)/(M_1M_2)=1/M_{\mathrm r}$. The first term, with its $1/\omega^2$ at low frequency, is the response of a cell with net charge $e(Z^*_1+Z^*_2)$, which the field accelerates as a whole; neutrality removes it, and with it the acoustic pole.

Adding the electrons: the dielectric function

The valence electrons polarize too. They respond within femtoseconds, while the ions need about a tenth of a picosecond, and at phonon frequencies (tens of meV) far below the band gap (1.4 eV in GaAs) the electron cloud follows the field instantly: the electrons are adiabatic, in the sense of Section A.2. Their contribution is then the same at every phonon frequency, and it is measured by the electronic dielectric constant $\varepsilon_\infty$ of Section A.4, the response of the electrons with the ions clamped: 10.9 for GaAs, 2.34 for NaCl.

Fields in a dielectric, in Gaussian units skip if familiar

A polarization $\bP$ that varies in space leaves an uncompensated polarization charge of density $-\nabla\cdot\bP$, and a surface charge $\bP\cdot\hat{\mathbf n}$ where it ends at a surface with outward normal $\hat{\mathbf n}$. A polarization that changes in time carries the current $\partial\bP/\partial t$. Gauss's law for all charges, $\nabla\cdot\bE=4\pi(\rho^{\mathrm{free}}-\nabla\cdot\bP)$, then reads

$$\nabla\cdot\mathbf D=4\pi\rho^{\mathrm{free}},\qquad \mathbf D=\bE+4\pi\bP ,$$

where $\rho^{\mathrm{free}}$ is the density of charges that do not belong to the medium. The free charges alone are the sources of the electric displacement $\mathbf D$: they fix its divergence, and with it its longitudinal part, while a transverse $\mathbf D$ can exist without them. In a linear medium, $\mathbf D=\varepsilon(\omega)\bE$ at each frequency.

Two slabs show the geometry. In a slab with uniform $\bP$ normal to its faces and no free charge, the faces carry the surface charges $\pm|\bP|$, which make a field $\bE=-4\pi\bP$ inside, so that $\mathbf D=0$: a parallel-plate capacitor made of polarization charge. With $\bP$ parallel to the faces, no surface charge appears and $\bE=0$.

The field does work on the polarization current at the time-averaged rate $\frac12\mathrm{Re}\big[(-\ii\omega\bP)\cdot\bE^*\big]=\frac{\omega}{8\pi}\,\mathrm{Im}\,\varepsilon\,|\bE|^2$ per unit volume, using $\bP=(\varepsilon-1)\bE/4\pi$. The notes give the SI forms.

  1. Write the total polarization as an ionic and an electronic part, $\bP=\bP^{\ion}+\bP^{\rme}$, with $\bP^{\rme}=(\varepsilon_\infty-1)\bE/4\pi$.

    To linear order the dipole of a cell is a linear function of the two quantities that change: the ion displacements and the macroscopic field. The Born charge, defined at zero macroscopic field, is the first coefficient. The clamped-ion response of the electrons is the second. Each is measured with the other variable held fixed, so the two parts add without counting anything twice. This linear form is due to Born and Huang.

  2. Define the dielectric function through the electric displacement $\mathbf D$:

    $$\begin{aligned}\mathbf D&=\bE+4\pi\big(\bP^{\ion}+\bP^{\rme}\big)\\ &=\varepsilon(\omega)\,\bE .\end{aligned} \tag{B3.5}$$
    The divergence of $\mathbf D$ is set by the free charges alone, the charges that do not belong to the medium (background box). In a linear medium $\mathbf D$ is proportional to $\bE$ at each frequency, and the factor is $\varepsilon(\omega)$.

  3. Insert $\bP^{\ion}$ from and $\bP^{\rme}$, and read off the factor that multiplies $\bE$.

    The 1 of the vacuum term combines with the $\varepsilon_\infty-1$ of the electrons, and the ionic term brings the Lorentz oscillator. The result is the first line of below.
$\bP^{\rme}$
electronic polarization: the dipole per unit volume of the electron cloud, distorted by the field with the ions clamped
$\mathbf D$
electric displacement, whose divergence is set by the free charges alone, those that do not belong to the medium (bold; the italic $D$ of later sections is the photon propagator)

The dielectric function of the polar crystal is therefore

$$\begin{aligned}\varepsilon(\omega)&=\varepsilon_\infty+\frac{\big(\omega_{\mathrm p}^{\ion}\big)^2}{\omega_{\mathrm{TO}}^2-\omega^2-\ii\gamma\omega}\\ &=\varepsilon_\infty+\frac{(\varepsilon_{\mathrm s}-\varepsilon_\infty)\,\omega_{\mathrm{TO}}^2}{\omega_{\mathrm{TO}}^2-\omega^2-\ii\gamma\omega} .\end{aligned} \tag{B3.6}$$
$\varepsilon(\omega)$
dielectric function of the crystal at wavelengths much longer than the cell, electrons and ions together (dimensionless)
$\varepsilon_\infty$, $\varepsilon_{\mathrm s}$
electronic (clamped-ion) and total static dielectric constants

The second line uses the static limit. At $\omega=0$ the damping drops out, and

$$\varepsilon_{\mathrm s}=\varepsilon(0)=\varepsilon_\infty+\bigg(\frac{\omega_{\mathrm p}^{\ion}}{\omega_{\mathrm{TO}}}\bigg)^{\!2} . \tag{B3.7}$$

A static field gives the ions all the time they need, so they screen in full. Solving this for $(\omega_{\mathrm p}^{\ion})^2=(\varepsilon_{\mathrm s}-\varepsilon_\infty)\,\omega_{\mathrm{TO}}^2$ and inserting it in the first line of gives the second line.

has three regimes. Well below $\omega_{\mathrm{TO}}$ the ions follow the field and $\varepsilon\to\varepsilon_{\mathrm s}$, the static constant. Well above it they cannot follow, only the electrons screen, and $\varepsilon\to\varepsilon_\infty$; this is why $\varepsilon_\infty$ is called the high-frequency constant even though it is measured below the band gap. Near $\omega_{\mathrm{TO}}$ the ions resonate. The ionic contribution to the static constant, $(\omega_{\mathrm p}^{\ion}/\omega_{\mathrm{TO}})^2$, is 1.79 in GaAs, a sixth of the electronic 10.9, and 3.67 in NaCl, more than the electronic 2.34: covalent GaAs screens mostly with its electrons, ionic NaCl mostly with its ions. With the Born charges above, gives $\varepsilon_{\mathrm s}=12.7$ for GaAs and 6.0 for NaCl, against the measured 12.9 and 5.9.7

In the language of Section A.5, adding the two polarizations is the statement that the irreducible polarizabilities of two independent parts of a system add, $\chi^{\mathrm{irr}}$$\,=\chi^{\mathrm{irr,e}}+\chi^{\mathrm{irr,ion}}$, the electronic part written as in Section A.5 and the ionic part likewise. Then the test-charge dielectric function $\varepsilon=1-v\chi^{\mathrm{irr}}$ of Section A.5, with $v$ the bare Coulomb interaction, is the sum of the electronic and the ionic dielectric functions, minus 1. The EPP manuscript adds the electrons this way, calls the step ad hoc, and later obtains $\varepsilon_\infty$ from the photon self-energy of the electrons (Sections B.5 and B.9). A constant $\varepsilon_\infty$ assumes adiabatic electrons. Conduction electrons or holes in a doped semiconductor respond on the time scale of the phonons and add a term of their own (Section B.9).

Zeros are LO, poles are TO

So far the field was imposed from outside. In a free vibration of the crystal there is no outside field: any field is created by the polarization itself. Whether one is created depends on the direction in which the ions move relative to the wavevector, and a picture with slabs shows why ().

Cut the crystal into slabs perpendicular to $\bq$, each thin compared with the wavelength, so that $\bP$ is uniform inside each. In a longitudinal wave (Section A.1) the ions move along $\bq$, so $\bP$ is normal to the slab faces. Where two slabs with different $\bP$ meet, the displaced charges do not cancel: a sheet of polarization charge sits on the face, and these sheets make a field inside each slab that points against $\bP$. That field pushes every ion back toward its rest position, an extra restoring force, so the longitudinal vibration is stiffer. In a transverse wave the ions move perpendicular to $\bq$, $\bP$ lies in the slab faces, the faces stay neutral, and no field arises: the springs act alone. Section A.4 drew the Born dipoles of the same two waves ().

Maxwell's equations make the picture exact. A free vibration involves no free charges, and on the scale of a phonon wavelength the field is electrostatic: it has no curl. (This assumes that light crosses a wavelength much faster than one period, $cq\gg\omega$, with $c$ the speed of light; the caveat at the end of this subsection lifts the assumption.) For a wave $\propto\ee^{\ii\bq\cdot\br}$ the gradient becomes $\ii\bq$, and the two conditions read

$$\begin{gathered}\nabla\cdot\mathbf D=0\ \text{ gives }\ \varepsilon(\omega)\,\bq\cdot\bE=0,\\ \nabla\times\bE=0\ \text{ gives }\ \bq\times\bE=0 .\end{gathered} \tag{B3.8}$$

The second condition says that the field of a free vibration, if it is not zero, points along $\bq$. Which case holds is decided by the motion of the ions.

  1. Longitudinal wave, ions moving along $\bq$. Then $\bP^{\ion}$ points along $\bq$, and the field cannot vanish: with $\bE=0$, $\mathbf D$ would be $4\pi\bP^{\ion}$, whose divergence $4\pi\ii\bq\cdot\bP^{\ion}$ is not zero. So $\bq\cdot\bE\neq0$, the first condition requires $\varepsilon(\omega)=0$, and then $\mathbf D=\varepsilon\bE=0$.

    Without free charges, a longitudinal field can exist only at a frequency where the response of the medium cancels the vacuum term in $\mathbf D=\bE+4\pi\bP$.

  2. In this longitudinal wave, with $\mathbf D=0$ and $\bP^{\rme}=(\varepsilon_\infty-1)\bE/4\pi$, solve for the field:

    $$\bE=-\frac{4\pi\,\bP^{\ion}}{\varepsilon_\infty} . \tag{B3.9}$$
    $0=\bE+4\pi\bP^{\ion}+(\varepsilon_\infty-1)\bE$ gives $\varepsilon_\infty\bE=-4\pi\bP^{\ion}$. Without electrons, the field of the polarization charges would be $-4\pi\bP^{\ion}$, the field of the capacitor of the background box; the electrons screen it by $\varepsilon_\infty$.

  3. Insert this field into the relative equation (with $\gamma=0$), using $\bP^{\ion}=eZ^*\bu_{\mathrm{rel}}/\OV$:

    $$M_{\mathrm r}\ddot\bu_{\mathrm{rel}}=-\Big(K+\frac{4\pi e^2Z^{*2}}{\OV\,\varepsilon_\infty}\Big)\bu_{\mathrm{rel}} .$$
    The field is proportional to $-\bu_{\mathrm{rel}}$, so it acts as a second spring, in parallel with $K$.

  4. Divide by $M_{\mathrm r}$. The frequency of the longitudinal optical vibration is

    $$\omega_{\mathrm{LO}}^2=\omega_{\mathrm{TO}}^2+\frac{\big(\omega_{\mathrm p}^{\ion}\big)^2}{\varepsilon_\infty} . \tag{B3.10}$$
    It is the zero of at $\gamma=0$: $\varepsilon_\infty(\omega_{\mathrm{TO}}^2-\omega^2)+(\omega_{\mathrm p}^{\ion})^2=0$, as step 1 requires.

  5. Transverse wave, ions moving perpendicular to $\bq$. Then $\bP^{\ion}\perp\bq$, and $\nabla\cdot\mathbf D=0$ reads $\varepsilon_\infty\,\bq\cdot\bE=0$: the field has no component along $\bq$. The second condition allows no other component, so $\bE=0$, and the relative equation reduces to $M_{\mathrm r}\ddot\bu_{\mathrm{rel}}=-K\bu_{\mathrm{rel}}$, with frequency $\omega_{\mathrm{TO}}$.

    The ions vibrate with $\bP^{\ion}\neq0$ while $\bE=0$, so only the springs act. By , a finite polarization with zero field requires $1/(\omega_{\mathrm{TO}}^2-\omega^2)$ to be infinite: the pole of $\varepsilon(\omega)$. The displacement $\mathbf D=4\pi\bP^{\ion}$ is then transverse, and a transverse $\mathbf D$ needs no free charge.

So the zeros of $\varepsilon(\omega)$ are the LO phonons and its poles are the TO phonons.8 With both, the dielectric function takes its factorized form:

$$\varepsilon(\omega)=\varepsilon_\infty\,\frac{\omega_{\mathrm{LO}}^2-\omega^2-\ii\gamma\omega}{\omega_{\mathrm{TO}}^2-\omega^2-\ii\gamma\omega} . \tag{B3.11}$$

Over the common denominator, has the numerator $\varepsilon_\infty(\omega_{\mathrm{TO}}^2-\omega^2-\ii\gamma\omega)+(\omega_{\mathrm p}^{\ion})^2$, which is $\varepsilon_\infty(\omega_{\mathrm{LO}}^2-\omega^2-\ii\gamma\omega)$ by .

In GaAs the field adds the stiffness $4\pi e^2Z^{*2}/(\OV\varepsilon_\infty)=1.57$ eV/Å² to $K=9.58$ eV/Å², 16 %. then gives $\hbar\omega_{\mathrm{LO}}=35.9$ meV, against the measured 36.2 meV; a Born charge of 2.18 would reproduce the measurement exactly. In NaCl, with its small $\varepsilon_\infty$, the field adds 2.16 eV/Å² to $K=1.38$ eV/Å², more than the springs themselves, and gives 32.6 meV against 32.7 meV measured. This is the LO–TO splitting of Section A.4, : there it came from the nonanalytic term of the force constants, here from the macroscopic field of a dielectric. A vibration with $Z^*=0$, as in silicon or diamond, has $\omega_{\mathrm p}^{\ion}=0$ and no splitting.

The order of the argument shows what each frequency means. The TO frequency belongs to the springs. The LO frequency is the TO frequency raised by the vibration's own macroscopic field, so only vibrations that carry a dipole are shifted.

What the electrostatic picture leaves out: phonon polaritons

The classification used $\nabla\times\bE=0$, which holds for $cq\gg\omega$. With the full Faraday law, $\nabla\times\bE=-\frac1c\partial_t\bB$ with $\bB$ the magnetic field, a transverse field no longer has to vanish: it is a light wave, which inside the crystal obeys $c^2q^2=\omega^2\varepsilon(\omega)$. Where this dispersion of light meets the TO frequency, the TO phonon and the photon mix into the phonon polariton of Section A.4. That happens only for $q\lesssim\sqrt{\varepsilon_{\mathrm s}}\,\omega_{\mathrm{TO}}/c$, which is $6\times10^{-5}$ Å⁻¹ in GaAs, against a zone-boundary wavevector of about 1.1 Å⁻¹. For almost every wavevector of the zone the electrostatic picture, which is the picture of Part A, holds. The LO mode is not affected: a longitudinal field has no curl and does not radiate. The Lagrangian of Section B.2 already contains the transverse field. Section B.4 quantizes it, Section B.6 finds the polaritons, and Section B.7 shows that the limits $q\to0$ and $c\to\infty$ do not commute (the light cone of Section X.1).

The Lyddane–Sachs–Teller relation

Static screening and the two optical frequencies are tied together. Set $\omega=0$ in the factorized form :

$$\frac{\varepsilon_{\mathrm s}}{\varepsilon_\infty}=\frac{\omega_{\mathrm{LO}}^2}{\omega_{\mathrm{TO}}^2} . \tag{B3.12}$$

This is the Lyddane–Sachs–Teller (LST) relation, published by Lyddane, Sachs and Teller in 1941 and met in Section A.4. The same result follows from and , the route Section A.4 took: inserting $(\omega_{\mathrm p}^{\ion})^2=\varepsilon_\infty(\omega_{\mathrm{LO}}^2-\omega_{\mathrm{TO}}^2)$ into the static constant gives $\varepsilon_{\mathrm s}=\varepsilon_\infty\omega_{\mathrm{LO}}^2/\omega_{\mathrm{TO}}^2$. The relation ties a static quantity to two vibrational frequencies. For GaAs, $10.9\times(36.2/33.3)^2=12.88$, against 12.9 measured; for NaCl, $2.34\times(32.7/20.3)^2=6.07$, against 5.9.9

The lattice adds $\varepsilon_{\mathrm s}-\varepsilon_\infty=\varepsilon_\infty(\omega_{\mathrm{LO}}^2-\omega_{\mathrm{TO}}^2)/\omega_{\mathrm{TO}}^2$ to the static screening, which grows with the splitting. A TO frequency that drops toward zero makes $\varepsilon_{\mathrm s}$ diverge. This happens near a ferroelectric transition, below which the crystal carries a spontaneous polarization: in strontium titanate the TO frequency drops on cooling (the mode softens), and $\varepsilon_{\mathrm s}$ exceeds $10^4$ at liquid-helium temperature.

What does not depend on the model

Two results survive any refinement of the model.

  • The LST relation itself. It needs only that $\varepsilon(\omega)$ has one pole and one zero and tends to $\varepsilon_\infty$ above them. The field conditions then make the pole the TO frequency and the zero the LO frequency, whatever the springs. With several polar modes along a principal axis, $\varepsilon(\omega)$ is a ratio of two polynomials in $\omega^2$: the numerator vanishes at the LO frequencies and the denominator at the TO frequencies. Setting $\omega=0$ then gives $\varepsilon_{\mathrm s}/\varepsilon_\infty$ as the product of the ratios $\omega_{\mathrm{LO}}^2/\omega_{\mathrm{TO}}^2$ of all the polar modes (Cochran and Cowley).
  • The splitting of the squares. By , $\omega_{\mathrm{LO}}^2-\omega_{\mathrm{TO}}^2=(\omega_{\mathrm p}^{\ion})^2/\varepsilon_\infty$ $=4\pi e^2Z^{*2}/(\OV M_{\mathrm r}\varepsilon_\infty)$ does not contain the spring $K$. It is fixed by the Born charge, the reduced mass, the cell volume and the electronic screening, and for a two-atom cubic crystal it equals the nonanalytic term of the force constants in Section A.4.

What does depend on the model is the frequency the ions would have if the dipole field were switched off. No experiment can switch it off, so that frequency cannot be measured. The next subsection shows how it depends on the treatment of the local field.

Local field versus macroscopic field

The EPP manuscript adds the local field that the main text has set aside so far (Which field acts on an ion). It keeps the short-range spring $K$ of Section B.2, whose resonance $\omega_0=(K/M_{\mathrm r})^{1/2}$ it calls the bare phonon, and lets the ions feel the field of the neighbouring dipoles as well as the macroscopic field.

Lorentz's construction does it (). Draw a sphere around one ion, large compared with the cell and small compared with the wavelength. Outside the sphere, treat the crystal as a continuum with the polarization $\bP^{\ion}$. The field of this continuum at the centre is the macroscopic field $\bE$ minus the contribution of the material inside the sphere, which has been taken out; a uniformly polarized sphere makes the field $-4\pi\bP^{\ion}/3$ inside itself, so removing it adds $+4\pi\bP^{\ion}/3$, the field of the polarization charge on the sphere's surface. Inside the sphere, keep the point dipoles of the lattice: on sites of cubic symmetry their fields at the centre cancel.

The local field at the ion is therefore the Lorentz field

$$\bE^{\mathrm{loc}}=\bE+\frac{4\pi}{3}\,\bP^{\ion} . \tag{B3.13}$$
$\bE^{\mathrm{loc}}$
field acting on one ion of a lattice of point dipoles on cubic sites; it differs from the macroscopic field by the field of the nearby dipoles

  1. Replace $\bE$ by $\bE^{\mathrm{loc}}$ in the relative equation, with $\bP^{\ion}=eZ^*\bu_{\mathrm{rel}}/\OV$:

    $$M_{\mathrm r}\ddot\bu_{\mathrm{rel}}=-\Big(K-\frac{4\pi e^2Z^{*2}}{3\OV}\Big)\bu_{\mathrm{rel}}+eZ^*\bE .$$
    The Lorentz term is proportional to $\bu_{\mathrm{rel}}$, like a spring force, but it points along the displacement: a spring of negative stiffness. The surrounding dipoles, all parallel, reinforce the displacement of each ion.

  2. Compare with : the local field only replaces the spring $K$ by $K-M_{\mathrm r}(\omega_{\mathrm p}^{\ion})^2/3$. Everything derived from on holds unchanged, with

    $$\begin{gathered}\omega_{\mathrm{TO}}^2=\omega_0^2-\zeta\,\big(\omega_{\mathrm p}^{\ion}\big)^2,\\ \omega_{\mathrm{LO}}^2=\omega_{\mathrm{TO}}^2+\frac{\big(\omega_{\mathrm p}^{\ion}\big)^2}{\varepsilon_\infty} ,\end{gathered} \tag{B3.14}$$

$\zeta$

So the choice of local-field model changes only $\omega_0$, the frequency of the short-range springs, which cannot be measured. The dielectric function written with the measured $\omega_{\mathrm{TO}}$, the LST relation and the splitting are the same for every $\zeta$. To reproduce the measured $\hbar\omega_{\mathrm{TO}}=33.3$ meV of GaAs, $\hbar\omega_0$ must be 33.3 meV for $\zeta=0$, 35.5 meV in the Clausius–Mossotti model and 42.1 meV for $\zeta=1/3$. In the form the EPP manuscript prints, with $\zeta=1/3$ reads $\omega_{\mathrm{LO}}^2=\omega_0^2+(1/\varepsilon_\infty-1/3)(\omega_{\mathrm p}^{\ion})^2$.10

Derivation: the Clausius–Mossotti model with Szigeti charges

Treat the electrons, too, as point dipoles that feel the local field. Each cell then holds rigid point ions with charges $\pm eZ_{\mathrm S}$ and an electronic polarizability $\alpha_{\mathrm e}$, all on sites of cubic symmetry.11

  1. The dipole per cell is $\OV\bP=eZ_{\mathrm S}\bu_{\mathrm{rel}}+\alpha_{\mathrm e}\bE^{\mathrm{loc}}$ with $\bE^{\mathrm{loc}}=\bE+4\pi\bP/3$, now with the total $\bP$.

    Ions and electrons both sit in the Lorentz field of all the dipoles.

  2. For clamped ions the model must give $\varepsilon_\infty$, which fixes $4\pi\alpha_{\mathrm e}/(3\OV)=(\varepsilon_\infty-1)/(\varepsilon_\infty+2)$, the Clausius–Mossotti relation. Solving step 1 for $\bP$ then gives $\bP=\frac{\varepsilon_\infty+2}{3\OV}\big(eZ_{\mathrm S}\bu_{\mathrm{rel}}+\alpha_{\mathrm e}\bE\big)$.

    Set $\bu_{\mathrm{rel}}=0$ and require $\bP=(\varepsilon_\infty-1)\bE/4\pi$; the factor $1-4\pi\alpha_{\mathrm e}/(3\OV)=3/(\varepsilon_\infty+2)$ comes from the $\bP$ inside $\bE^{\mathrm{loc}}$.

  3. Insert $\bP$ into $\bE^{\mathrm{loc}}$: $\bE^{\mathrm{loc}}=\frac{\varepsilon_\infty+2}{3}\,\bE+\frac{4\pi(\varepsilon_\infty+2)}{9\OV}\,eZ_{\mathrm S}\bu_{\mathrm{rel}}$.

    $1+\frac{4\pi\alpha_{\mathrm e}}{3\OV}\,\frac{\varepsilon_\infty+2}{3}=\frac{\varepsilon_\infty+2}{3}$.

  4. The force on the ions is $eZ_{\mathrm S}\bE^{\mathrm{loc}}$. Its coefficient of $\bE$ is the Born charge, $Z^*=(\varepsilon_\infty+2)\,Z_{\mathrm S}/3$, the Szigeti relation. Its term in $\bu_{\mathrm{rel}}$ is a negative spring, $\frac{4\pi e^2Z_{\mathrm S}^2(\varepsilon_\infty+2)}{9\OV}=\frac{M_{\mathrm r}(\omega_{\mathrm p}^{\ion})^2}{\varepsilon_\infty+2}$, with $\omega_{\mathrm p}^{\ion}$ built from $Z^*$.

    The Born charge is the force per unit macroscopic field. Writing $Z_{\mathrm S}=3Z^*/(\varepsilon_\infty+2)$ turns the spring into the form shown.

  5. So $\zeta=1/(\varepsilon_\infty+2)$. Written with $Z^*$ instead of $Z_{\mathrm S}$, the polarization of step 2 is $\bP=eZ^*\bu_{\mathrm{rel}}/\OV+(\varepsilon_\infty-1)\bE/4\pi$, the linear form of the main text, so $\varepsilon(\omega)$, the LST relation and the splitting are unchanged.

    The model is consistent by construction: the electrons that screen the macroscopic field are the same that dress the ionic charge. For GaAs it gives $Z_{\mathrm S}=0.48$: the rest of the Born charge 2.07 comes from the electrons, polarized by the local field of the displaced ions.

What the Lorentz-field model leaves out

  • Cubic sites only. The fields of the nearby dipoles cancel at the centre only for sites of cubic symmetry. Otherwise a tensor whose trace is $4\pi$ replaces the factor $4\pi/3$; in uniaxial hBN the isotropic factor does not apply.
  • Born charges already contain local fields. A Born charge is computed with all electrons responding, local fields included. Putting it into the vacuum Lorentz field and adding $\varepsilon_\infty$ afterwards counts part of the screening twice. A symptom: for $\varepsilon_\infty\gt3$ the bracket $1/\varepsilon_\infty-1/3$ is negative and $\omega_{\mathrm{LO}}$ falls below $\omega_0$ (35.9 against 42.1 meV in GaAs), as if the macroscopic field, which stiffens the longitudinal vibration, softened it. The Clausius–Mossotti model keeps $\omega_{\mathrm{LO}}$ above $\omega_0$ for every $\varepsilon_\infty$.
  • Soft modes. If $\zeta(\omega_{\mathrm p}^{\ion})^2\gt\omega_0^2$, then $\omega_{\mathrm{TO}}^2\lt0$: the attraction between neighbouring dipoles beats the short-range springs, and the lattice polarizes spontaneously. This is Cochran's picture of a ferroelectric. As $\omega_{\mathrm{TO}}\to0$, the LST relation sends $\varepsilon_{\mathrm s}\to\infty$.

The Reststrahlen band

Between $\omega_{\mathrm{TO}}$ and $\omega_{\mathrm{LO}}$ the dielectric function is negative (for $\gamma\to0$): the ionic polarization, past its resonance, points against the field and outweighs the vacuum and the electrons. A light wave in such a medium has an imaginary refractive index $\sqrt{\varepsilon}$. It does not propagate, but decays within a fraction of a wavelength, and its energy is reflected: a polished crystal is a near-perfect mirror in this band. Rubens and Nichols used this in 1897 to isolate far-infrared light; after several reflections off a crystal, only light in the band survives, the Reststrahlen (“residual rays”) that give the band of Section A.5 its name. For NaCl the band runs from 20.3 to 32.7 meV, wavelengths of 61 to 38 µm; for GaAs from 33.3 to 36.2 meV, or 37.2 to 34.2 µm. computes the spectra from .

Absorption, energy loss and which probe sees which mode

The two kinds of mode show up in two kinds of experiment. A field oscillating inside the crystal does work on the bound charges, at the time-averaged rate per unit volume (background box above)

$$\begin{aligned}&\frac{\omega}{8\pi}\,\mathrm{Im}\,\varepsilon(\omega)\,|\bE|^2\\ &\quad=\frac{\omega}{8\pi}\,\mathrm{Im}\Big[\!-\frac1{\varepsilon(\omega)}\Big]\,|\mathbf D|^2 .\end{aligned} \tag{B3.16}$$

The two forms are equal because $\mathbf D=\varepsilon\bE$ and $\mathrm{Im}(-1/\varepsilon)=\mathrm{Im}\,\varepsilon/|\varepsilon|^2$.

Which form is useful depends on what the probe fixes. A transverse light wave crossing a thin film sets the field $\bE$ inside it, so the absorption follows $\mathrm{Im}\,\varepsilon$, which peaks at the pole, $\omega_{\mathrm{TO}}$: infrared absorption sees the TO phonon. A fast electron passing through the crystal brings its own charge, and Gauss's law $\nabla\cdot\mathbf D=4\pi\rho^{\mathrm{free}}$, with $\rho^{\mathrm{free}}$ the density of charges that do not belong to the crystal, fixes the longitudinal $\mathbf D$ that this charge creates. The energy it loses then follows the loss function $-\mathrm{Im}[1/\varepsilon(\omega)]$, which peaks at the zero, $\omega_{\mathrm{LO}}$: electron energy-loss spectroscopy (EELS) sees the LO phonon. The screened interaction of the next subsection carries the same factor $1/\varepsilon$.

Physical picture: optics sees TO, Coulomb sees LO

A transverse light wave probes the poles of $\varepsilon$: infrared absorption peaks at $\omega_{\mathrm{TO}}$, and the crystal reflects between $\omega_{\mathrm{TO}}$ and $\omega_{\mathrm{LO}}$. Everything that acts through charge, through the Coulomb field, probes the zeros: an electron crossing a film, an electron scattered by the lattice (the Fröhlich coupling of Section A.4), the screening of the attraction between the electron and the hole of an exciton (a bound electron–hole pair). All go through $1/\varepsilon$, whose pole sits at $\omega_{\mathrm{LO}}$. This is why carriers in polar semiconductors couple to LO phonons, while infrared spectra show the TO frequency.

The lattice inside the screened interaction

Place a test charge in a polar crystal. Within femtoseconds the electrons rearrange and reduce its field by a factor $\varepsilon_\infty$. If the charge stays put longer than a phonon period, about 0.1 ps, the ions move too and the field drops further, to $1/\varepsilon_{\mathrm s}$ of its bare value. For a charge that oscillates at frequency $\omega$ the reduction is $1/\varepsilon(\omega)$.   • At $\omega=0$, $W=v/\varepsilon_{\mathrm s}$: the lattice adds to the static screening, and $W^{\ph}(\bq,0)=-v(1/\varepsilon_\infty-1/\varepsilon_{\mathrm s})$ is attractive. An electron that moves slowly compared with the lattice drags a lattice distortion along with it, a polaron (Sections A.4 and A.8).
  • Between $\omega_{\mathrm{TO}}$ and $\omega_{\mathrm{LO}}$ the total $W$ is negative: the Reststrahlen band as charges see it.
  • Far above $\omega_{\mathrm{LO}}$ the ions cannot follow and $W\to v/\varepsilon_\infty$, the $W$ of a GW calculation with clamped nuclei.

In GaAs, $1/\varepsilon_\infty-1/\varepsilon_{\mathrm s}=0.014$, so the lattice weakens the static interaction by 15 %, and $|g^{\mathrm{lr}}|\,q=0.032$ eV Å⁻¹. In NaCl the same factor is 0.26, more than half of $1/\varepsilon_\infty$. For excitons this matters directly: the attraction between an electron and a hole is screened at the frequencies of their internal motion. When the binding energy is small compared with $\hbar\omega_{\mathrm{LO}}$ (4 meV against 36 meV in GaAs), the ions follow and $\varepsilon_{\mathrm s}$ applies; when it is large, only $\varepsilon_\infty$ does; in between the screening depends on frequency, as Haken showed.

The review splits $W$ into $W^{\rme}$ and $W^{\ph}$ in general, following Hedin and Lundqvist; for a polar insulator at long wavelength the split is . The review also describes the route of Botti and Marques, who build $W$ directly from a total dielectric function of the Lyddane–Sachs–Teller type, which is $v/\varepsilon(\omega)$ here.13 In the EPP formalism the same object returns as the charge–charge (Coulomb) part of the photon propagator, with $\varepsilon_\infty$ produced by the photon self-energy of the electrons instead of being inserted by hand (Sections B.6 and B.9).

Check yourself: three short questions

  1. A polar crystal has $\varepsilon_\infty=4.0$, $\hbar\omega_{\mathrm{TO}}=40$ meV and $\hbar\omega_{\mathrm{LO}}=50$ meV. What is $\varepsilon_{\mathrm s}$, and over which photon energies does a polished sample reflect nearly all normally incident light?

    Answer. By the LST relation , $\varepsilon_{\mathrm s}=4.0\times(50/40)^2=6.25$. The sample reflects in the Reststrahlen band between 40 and 50 meV, where $\varepsilon\lt0$ and gives $R\approx1$.

  2. Why does the infrared absorption of a thin film peak at $\omega_{\mathrm{TO}}$, while an electron energy-loss spectrum peaks at $\omega_{\mathrm{LO}}$?

    Answer. By , the absorbed power is $\mathrm{Im}\,\varepsilon\,|\bE|^2$ or $\mathrm{Im}(-1/\varepsilon)\,|\mathbf D|^2$. Light fixes the transverse field $\bE$ in the film, so it measures $\mathrm{Im}\,\varepsilon$, which peaks at the pole of $\varepsilon$, the TO frequency. A passing charge fixes the longitudinal $\mathbf D$ through Gauss's law, so it measures $\mathrm{Im}(-1/\varepsilon)$, which peaks at the zero of $\varepsilon$, the LO frequency.

  3. A model gives Born charges with $Z^*_1+Z^*_2=0.1$. What goes wrong in $\varepsilon(\omega)$ at low frequency?

    Answer. The centre of mass is then driven, $M_{\mathrm c}\ddot\bu_{\mathrm c}=0.1\,e\bE$, as for a gas of mobile charges. Its polarization adds a term proportional to $-1/\omega^2$ to $\varepsilon$, so $\varepsilon\to-\infty$ as $\omega\to0$ instead of tending to $\varepsilon_{\mathrm s}$: the crystal would conduct. The Born-charge sum rule forbids this in an insulator.
