One Lagrangian for electrons, ions and the field

The EPP manuscript puts the valence electrons, the harmonic ions and the full electromagnetic field into one classical Lagrangian. In the temporal gauge the field is a single vector potential, which carries both the Coulomb interaction and light. Electrons couple to it through their current and ions through the current of their Born dipoles; there is no electron–phonon term, because the field carries the interaction. The Euler–Lagrange equations give the Lorentz force for the electrons and, for the ions, a lattice driven by the field.

What you need: Section B.1 (the bare and polar vertices, the basis phase, and the rule against double counting); the labels, displacements and force constants of Section A.1; the Born charges, the polarization and the long-range force constants of Section A.4; the potentials, the longitudinal and transverse parts of a field, and the light cone of Section X.1. From electrodynamics, Maxwell's equations, which this primer writes in Gaussian units with the elementary charge $e$, the speed of light $c$ and the electron mass $m_{\mathrm e}$ kept explicit; $e\gt0$, and the electron carries the charge $-e$. Two background boxes recall the Lagrangian form of mechanics and gauge freedom. Everything in this section is classical; Section B.4 quantizes it.1

The picture: three systems and one field

In Part A every interaction between particles is the instantaneous Coulomb interaction: a displaced ion changes the potential everywhere at once. The working Hamiltonian of Section A.2, , goes further and folds that interaction into numbers computed beforehand: band energies, phonon frequencies, and an electron–phonon coupling $g$ that already contains the screened Coulomb field of the displaced ions. Light enters Part A only as an external field (Section A.9).

The EPP manuscript starts one level lower. It treats three dynamical systems on an equal footing:

  1. the valence electrons, which move in the potential of the ions frozen at their equilibrium sites;
  2. the ions, which vibrate about those sites on short-range springs;
  3. the electromagnetic field, with all its polarizations.

Apart from the static potential of the frozen ions, which the electrons feel, electrons and ions never act on each other directly. Each acts on the field, and the field acts on each. A moving electron drives the field through its current. A vibrating ion drives it through the current of the electric dipole that its displacement creates. What Part A calls the Coulomb interaction is the part of the field that follows the charges at every instant; the rest is light, which travels at the speed $c$. An electron–phonon interaction becomes a two-step process: electron to field, field to ion. , further on, draws this map of who acts on whom, term by term.

The natural way to write such a system down is a Lagrangian $L$: one function of all coordinates and velocities, the field included, from which one rule, the Euler–Lagrange equation, gives the equations of motion of every part. The background box below recalls how this works. The plan of the section:

  1. describe the field by one vector function, the vector potential in the temporal gauge;
  2. write the Lagrangian term by term;
  3. explain why the ions couple through their Born dipoles, and why there is no electron–phonon term;
  4. derive the equations of motion: the Lorentz force for the electrons, a driven lattice for the ions.
Lagrangians, for particles and for fields skip if familiar

A system with coordinates $x_1,x_2,\dots$ is described by a Lagrangian $L(x,\dot x,t)$, a function of the coordinates, of their velocities $\dot x_i=\dd x_i/\dd t$ and possibly of the time. For particles in a potential it is the kinetic energy minus the potential energy. Among all paths between two fixed end points, the actual motion makes the action $S=\int L\,\dd t$ stationary. Varying the path by $\delta x_i(t)$, integrating by parts and using that the end points are fixed gives one equation per coordinate, the Euler–Lagrange equation:

$$\frac{\dd}{\dd t}\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot x_i}=\frac{\partial L}{\partial x_i} .$$

For $L=\frac12m\dot x^2-V(x)$ it is Newton's law, $m\ddot x=-\dd V/\dd x$. Four facts are used in this section.

  • The canonical momentum of $x_i$ is $\partial L/\partial\dot x_i$. For a particle in a potential it is $m\dot x$. When $L$ contains a term linear in the velocity, as for a charge in a vector potential, the canonical momentum differs from $m\dot x$. If $L$ does not depend on $x_i$, the Euler–Lagrange equation says that its canonical momentum is conserved.
  • Adding a total time derivative $\frac{\dd}{\dd t}f(x,t)$ to $L$ does not change the equations of motion: it changes the action only by the values of $f$ at the end points, which are fixed.
  • A field such as the vector potential $\bA(\br,t)$ has one coordinate per point $\br$ and per component $\alpha$. Its Lagrangian is an integral, $L=\int\dd^3r\,\mathcal L$, of a Lagrangian density $\mathcal L$ that depends on $A_\alpha$, on $\partial_tA_\alpha$ and on the space derivatives $\partial_\beta A_\alpha$. The Euler–Lagrange equation becomes $\partial_t\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial(\partial_tA_\alpha)}+\sum_\beta\partial_\beta\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial(\partial_\beta A_\alpha)}=\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial A_\alpha}$. Section B.4 applies it to the field of this section.
  • The Hamiltonian follows from $L$ by a Legendre transform, the sum of each canonical momentum times its velocity minus $L$, written in terms of the momenta. For a charge $-e$ in a vector potential it has the minimal-coupling form $(\mathbf p+\frac ec\bA)^2/2m$, where $\mathbf p$ is the canonical momentum (Section B.4).

Potentials and the temporal gauge

Maxwell's equations are written for the electric field $\bE$ and the magnetic field $\bB$. Two of them hold whatever the charges do: $\nabla\cdot\bB=0$ (there are no magnetic charges) and Faraday's law, $\nabla\times\bE=-\frac1c\partial_t\bB$. Here $\partial_t$ is the time derivative at a fixed point $\br$. Both equations are satisfied automatically if the fields are derived from a scalar potential $A_0(\br,t)$ and a vector potential $\bA(\br,t)$:

$$\begin{gathered}\bE=-\nabla A_0-\frac1c\,\partial_t\bA,\\ \bB=\nabla\times\bA .\end{gathered} \tag{B2.1}$$

electric and magnetic fields (Gaussian units, in which $\bE$ and $\bB$ have the same units; $e\bE$ is a force, quoted in eV/Å)
$A_0$, $\bA$
scalar and vector potentials; $A_\alpha$ are the Cartesian components of $\bA$; $eA_0$ and $e\bA$ are energies (eV)
$c$

The divergence of a curl vanishes, so $\nabla\cdot\bB=0$; and $\nabla\times\bE=-\frac1c\partial_t(\nabla\times\bA)=-\frac1c\partial_t\bB$, because the curl of a gradient vanishes.

The potentials are not unique. Adding the gradient of any smooth function $\vartheta(\br,t)$, called a gauge function, to $\bA$, while subtracting $\frac1c\partial_t\vartheta$ from $A_0$, leaves $\bE$ and $\bB$ unchanged. The background box below recalls this gauge freedom. A rule that picks one set of potentials among all those that give the same fields is called a gauge. The EPP manuscript works in the temporal gauge (also called the Weyl gauge), in which the scalar potential vanishes:2

$$\begin{gathered}A_0=0:\\ \bE=-\frac1c\,\partial_t\bA,\qquad \bB=\nabla\times\bA .\end{gathered} \tag{B2.2}$$

Both fields now come from one vector function: the electric field is $-1/c$ times the rate of change of $\bA$ at a fixed point, and the magnetic field is its curl.

Gauge freedom skip if familiar

In Gaussian units Maxwell's equations read

$$\begin{gathered}\nabla\cdot\bE=4\pi\rho,\qquad \nabla\cdot\bB=0,\\ \nabla\times\bE=-\frac1c\,\partial_t\bB,\qquad \nabla\times\bB=\frac{4\pi}c\,\bj+\frac1c\,\partial_t\bE ,\end{gathered}$$

with $\rho$ the charge density and $\bj$ the current density. The two equations without sources are solved by the potentials of . For any smooth function $\vartheta(\br,t)$, the gauge transformation

$$\bA\to\bA+\nabla\vartheta,\qquad A_0\to A_0-\frac1c\,\partial_t\vartheta$$

changes neither field. $\bB$ gains $\nabla\times\nabla\vartheta=0$, and $\bE$ gains $\frac1c\nabla\partial_t\vartheta-\frac1c\partial_t\nabla\vartheta=0$. Every prediction of electrodynamics depends on the fields only, so all these potentials describe the same physics. Common choices are the Coulomb gauge, $\nabla\cdot\bA=0$, in which $A_0$ is the instantaneous Coulomb potential of the charges; the Lorenz gauge, $\nabla\cdot\bA+\frac1c\partial_tA_0=0$, which treats space and time alike; and the temporal gauge, $A_0=0$.

A moving point charge enters the Lagrangian through two terms: minus its potential energy, the charge times $A_0$, and the charge over $c$ times $\mathbf v\cdot\bA$. For an electron, of charge $-e$, at $\br(t)$ with velocity $\mathbf v$, this Lagrangian is $eA_0(\br,t)-\frac ec\,\mathbf v\cdot\bA(\br,t)$. Under the gauge transformation it changes by $-\frac ec\big[\partial_t\vartheta+\mathbf v\cdot\nabla\vartheta\big]=-\frac ec\,\frac{\dd}{\dd t}\vartheta\big(\br(t),t\big)$, a total time derivative, which does not change the equations of motion. The same holds for any other charge: its motion is the same in every gauge.

Why the temporal gauge is allowed

Any field can be described with $A_0=0$. In steps:

  1. Start from any potentials $(A_0,\bA)$ that give the fields through .

    They exist for every field that obeys Maxwell's equations, because the two equations without sources are exactly the conditions for .

  2. Choose the gauge function

    $$\vartheta(\br,t)=c\int_0^t A_0(\br,t')\,\dd t'$$

    and transform: the new scalar potential is $A_0-\frac1c\partial_t\vartheta=0$, and the new vector potential is $\bA+\nabla\vartheta$.

    By construction $\partial_t\vartheta=cA_0$, so the time derivative cancels the scalar potential at every point and every time.

  3. The fields $\bE$ and $\bB$ are unchanged, and so is the motion of every charge.

    A gauge transformation never changes the fields, and it changes the Lagrangian of each charge only by a total time derivative (background box).

  4. A little freedom remains: a function $\vartheta(\br)$ that does not depend on time keeps $A_0=0$.

    Its time derivative vanishes. This leftover freedom is tied to Gauss's law, which the temporal gauge turns into a condition on the initial state (below and Section B.4).

Why it is convenient

  • One field carries everything. The Coulomb field of the charges and the radiation field both live in $\bA$, as its longitudinal and transverse parts (next subsection). That is what later allows one photon propagator for both, the sum of a longitudinal and a transverse part (Section B.5).
  • Every coupling has one form. With no scalar potential there is no "charge times $A_0$" term. Every coupling is a current times $\bA$, so electrons and ions enter the theory in the same way, as two sources of one field.
  • The Coulomb interaction is derived, not postulated. Part A takes the instantaneous interaction as given. Here it comes out of the energy of the longitudinal field (Section B.4). The Coulomb gauge describes the same physics with a different bookkeeping: there $\bA$ is purely transverse, and $A_0$ is fixed at each instant by Poisson's equation.

Longitudinal and transverse field

Write the vector potential as a sum of plane waves, $\bA(\br,t)=\sum_\bq\bA_\bq(t)\,\ee^{\ii\bq\cdot\br}$, with one complex amplitude $\bA_\bq$ for each wavevector allowed by the Born–von Kármán supercell of Section A.1 (all of them, not only those in the first Brillouin zone, because the field also varies within a cell). Each amplitude splits into a part along $\hat\bq=\bq/q$ and a part perpendicular to it:

$$\begin{gathered}\bA_\bq=\bA^{\mathrm L}_\bq+\bA^{\mathrm T}_\bq,\\ \bA^{\mathrm L}_\bq=\hat\bq\,(\hat\bq\cdot\bA_\bq),\qquad \hat\bq\cdot\bA^{\mathrm T}_\bq=0 .\end{gathered} \tag{B2.3}$$

The longitudinal part points along the wavevector, the transverse part across it, as for the lattice waves of Section A.1 and the polarization waves of Section X.1. Summing each part over $\bq$ gives fields $\bA^{\mathrm L}$ and $\bA^{\mathrm T}$, and likewise $\bE^{\mathrm L}=-\frac1c\partial_t\bA^{\mathrm L}$ and $\bE^{\mathrm T}=-\frac1c\partial_t\bA^{\mathrm T}$. On a plane wave $\nabla$ acts as multiplication by $\ii\bq$, which gives the two parts different roles:

  • The longitudinal part has no magnetic field. Its curl is $\ii\bq\times\hat\bq\,(\hat\bq\cdot\bA_\bq)=0$. Gauss's law, $\nabla\cdot\bE=4\pi\rho$ with $\rho$ the charge density of all charges, involves only $\bE^{\mathrm L}$, because $\ii\bq\cdot\bE^{\mathrm T}_\bq=0$. With $\rho_\bq$ and $\bE_\bq$ the Fourier amplitudes of the charge density and of the electric field, defined like $\bA_\bq$, it reads $\ii q\,\hat\bq\cdot\bE_\bq=4\pi\rho_\bq$, so
    $$\bE^{\mathrm L}_\bq=-\frac{4\pi\ii}{q}\,\rho_\bq\,\hat\bq . \tag{B2.4}$$
    This is the Coulomb field of the charges present at that same instant, with no delay: for a point charge it is the familiar $1/r^2$ field. In the temporal gauge, $\bA^{\mathrm L}$ changes in time exactly as fast as needed to produce it.
  • The transverse part carries the magnetic field and light. $\bE^{\mathrm T}$ and $\bB$ obey wave equations whose waves travel at the speed $c$.

The temporal gauge has a price. In a Lagrangian that keeps $A_0$, Gauss's law is the equation of motion of $A_0$. With $A_0$ gone, no variable has Gauss's law as its equation of motion. Varying $\bA$ gives only the Ampère–Maxwell law, $\nabla\times\bB=\frac{4\pi}c\bj+\frac1c\partial_t\bE$, with $\bj$ the current density of all charges (Section B.4 derives it). Gauss's law survives as a condition on the initial state. Take the divergence of the Ampère–Maxwell law: the left side vanishes, because the divergence of a curl is zero, which leaves $0=\frac{4\pi}c\nabla\cdot\bj+\frac1c\partial_t\nabla\cdot\bE$. Charge conservation, $\partial_t\rho+\nabla\cdot\bj=0$, turns this into

$$\partial_t\big(\nabla\cdot\bE-4\pi\rho\big)=0 . \tag{B2.5}$$

Gauss's law, once true, stays true; it is imposed on the initial state, and the dynamics keeps it. The densities $\rho$ and $\bj$ are made explicit in – below. Section B.4 imposes the condition and turns the energy of $\bE^{\mathrm L}$ into the Coulomb energy of the charges, .

Term 1: electrons in the frozen crystal

The electrons are point particles of mass $m_{\mathrm e}$ and charge $-e$. Classical particles suffice to set up the Lagrangian; Section B.4 quantizes them, and they become the band electrons of Part A. The potential energy they feel from the ions is

$$V^{\mathrm{en}}_0(\br)=\sum_{\ell\kappa}V_\kappa\big(\br-\btau_{\ell\kappa}\big), \tag{B2.7}$$

the electron–nucleus potential energy of Section A.2 with every ion at its equilibrium site. $V_\kappa$ is the potential energy of an electron near ion $\kappa$, here a pseudopotential that includes the nucleus and its core electrons. The potential is frozen: it does not move when the ions move. This choice has a consequence. Expanding the electron–nucleus potential in the displacements is exactly how Section A.2 obtains the electron–phonon coupling. Freezing it removes that coupling from the model. Its long-range, polar part returns through the field (terms 5 and 6); its short-range part does not, as the caveat further below records.

Terms 2 and 3: harmonic ions on short-range springs

Terms 2 and 3 are the kinetic and harmonic energies of the lattice of Section A.1, , with one change. The force constants are only the short-range part $\Phi^{\mathrm{sr}}$: the full force constants with their long-range dipole–dipole part $\Phi^{\mathrm{dd}}$ removed (Section A.4, ). The reason is the range rule of Section B.1, which keeps the same force from being counted twice. In a polar crystal a displaced ion carries a dipole whose field reaches far beyond its cell, and the force of that field on the other ions is $\Phi^{\mathrm{dd}}$, a tail that falls off as the inverse cube of the distance. In this Lagrangian the field is a dynamical variable that the dipoles drive (term 6), so the field regenerates that force. Keeping $\Phi^{\mathrm{dd}}$ in term 3 as well would count it twice.4 In GaAs the dipole–dipole force is what lifts the longitudinal optical frequency at the zone centre above the transverse one, from 33.3 to 36.2 meV (Section A.4). Quantized, terms 2 and 3 give the bare phonons of Section B.1: the phonons of the short-range springs alone, before the field dresses them.

Term 4: the free field

Term 4 is the Lagrangian of the electromagnetic field without charges: the electric energy density $E^2/8\pi$ minus the magnetic energy density $B^2/8\pi$. In the temporal gauge both fields are derivatives of $\bA$, by :

$$\begin{gathered}\frac1{8\pi}\int\dd^3r\,\big(E^2-B^2\big)\\ =\int\frac{\dd^3r}{8\pi}\Big[\frac{(\partial_t\bA)^2}{c^2}-(\nabla\times\bA)^2\Big] .\end{gathered} \tag{B2.8}$$

Read as mechanics, with the value of $\bA$ at each point as a coordinate, the electric energy is the kinetic energy, since it contains the velocity $\partial_t\bA$, and the magnetic energy is the potential energy. For a transverse wave the curl is $\ii\bq\times\bA^{\mathrm T}_\bq$, so the potential energy grows as $q^2|\bA^{\mathrm T}_\bq|^2$: a spring. The velocity term carries the factor $1/8\pi c^2$ and the spring term $q^2/8\pi$, and their ratio sets the frequency, $\omega^2=c^2q^2$. Each transverse wave is therefore an oscillator of frequency $cq$, and quantized it is a photon of energy $\hbar cq$. A longitudinal wave has no curl and so no spring: it does not oscillate. Its electric field is fixed by Gauss's law, , and its energy becomes the Coulomb energy in Section B.4.

Terms 5 and 6: charges, currents and one coupling

Term 5 couples each electron to the field at its own position. It has the standard form of the coupling of a moving point charge to a vector potential, the charge over $c$ times $\mathbf v\cdot\bA$ (gauge background box), here with the charge $-e$; the equations of motion below confirm it. Term 6 couples each ion to the field at its own equilibrium site, $\btau_{\ell\kappa}$, through the rate of change of the dipole $e\mathbf Z^*_\kappa\cdot\bu_{\ell\kappa}$ that its displacement creates; the next subsection explains why.

Both terms take one form when the charges and currents are written as densities. The electrons have the charge and current densities

$$\begin{gathered}\rho^{\mathrm e}(\br,t)=-e\sum_s\delta\big(\br-\br_s(t)\big),\\ \bj^{\mathrm e}(\br,t)=-e\sum_s\mathbf v_s\,\delta\big(\br-\br_s(t)\big) .\end{gathered} \tag{B2.9}$$

The ions enter through the polarization density of their Born dipoles, each placed at its own atom (the reason for this placement is given below):5

$$\begin{gathered}\bP=e\sum_{\ell\kappa}\mathbf Z^*_\kappa\cdot\bu_{\ell\kappa}\,\delta(\br-\btau_{\ell\kappa}),\\ \bj^{\mathrm{ion}}=\partial_t\bP,\qquad \rho^{\mathrm{ion}}=-\nabla\cdot\bP .\end{gathered} \tag{B2.10}$$

Why the ions couple through their Born dipoles

An ion at rest at its site does not appear in the field terms at all. Its static charge acts on the electrons through $V^{\mathrm{en}}_0$ in term 1, and between ions at rest it only adds a constant to the energy. What the field sees of an ion is how the charge distribution around it changes when it moves. To first order in the displacement, and seen from distances larger than an atom, that change is a point dipole at the atom, $e\mathbf Z^*_\kappa\cdot\bu_{\ell\kappa}$: by the definition of the Born charge in Section A.4, , $eZ^*_{\kappa,\alpha\beta}$ is the dipole along $\alpha$ per unit displacement along $\beta$. The Born charge counts the nucleus and also the electrons that follow it, which is why it differs from the bare ionic charge; in GaAs it is about $2.07$ for Ga and $-2.07$ for As.6 A changing dipole is a current, and term 6 couples that current to $\bA$ exactly as term 5 couples the electron current. Four consequences follow.

The current coupling is the dipole energy

The energy of an electric dipole in a field is minus the dipole times the field. Term 6 does not look like that, but it is equivalent to it. For one atom, with both fields taken at its site, $\bA\equiv\bA(\btau_{\ell\kappa},t)$ and $\bE\equiv\bE(\btau_{\ell\kappa},t)$:

  1. Differentiate the product of the dipole and the field at the atom's fixed site:

    $$\begin{aligned}&\frac{\dd}{\dd t}\Big[\big(\mathbf Z^*_\kappa\cdot\bu_{\ell\kappa}\big)\cdot\bA\Big]\\ &=\big(\mathbf Z^*_\kappa\cdot\dot\bu_{\ell\kappa}\big)\cdot\bA+\big(\mathbf Z^*_\kappa\cdot\bu_{\ell\kappa}\big)\cdot\partial_t\bA .\end{aligned}$$
    The site $\btau_{\ell\kappa}$ does not move, so the only time dependence of $\bA(\btau_{\ell\kappa},t)$ is explicit: its total time derivative is $\partial_t\bA$ at the site.

  2. Multiply by $e/c$, solve for the first term on the right, and replace $\partial_t\bA$ by $-c\bE$:

    $$\begin{aligned}&\frac ec\big(\mathbf Z^*_\kappa\cdot\dot\bu_{\ell\kappa}\big)\cdot\bA\\ &=e\big(\mathbf Z^*_\kappa\cdot\bu_{\ell\kappa}\big)\cdot\bE\\ &\quad+\frac{\dd}{\dd t}\Big[\frac ec\big(\mathbf Z^*_\kappa\cdot\bu_{\ell\kappa}\big)\cdot\bA\Big] .\end{aligned} \tag{B2.12}$$
    This is the temporal-gauge relation between $\bE$ and $\bA$.

  3. Drop the total time derivative. Term 6 is then equivalent to $+\sum_{\ell\kappa}e(\mathbf Z^*_\kappa\cdot\bu_{\ell\kappa})\cdot\bE(\btau_{\ell\kappa},t)$ in $L$, that is, to the potential energy

    $$-\sum_{\ell\kappa}e\big(\mathbf Z^*_\kappa\cdot\bu_{\ell\kappa}\big)\cdot\bE(\btau_{\ell\kappa},t) .$$
    A total time derivative does not change the equations of motion (background box above). What remains is the energy of the Born dipoles in the field at the atom positions.

Coupling a current to $\bA$ and coupling a dipole to $\bE$ are two ways of writing one interaction.7 The same equivalence relates the two forms of the optical matrix elements of electrons, the "velocity" form written with the electron's momentum and $\bA$, and the "length" form written with its position and $\bE$. This section keeps the current form, because it treats electrons and ions alike.

Rigid motion carries no dipole

The Born charges of a cell add up to zero, $\sum_\kappa\mathbf Z^*_\kappa=0$, : moving a whole cell rigidly moves a neutral object and creates no dipole. A rigid translation of the crystal, the acoustic mode at $\bq=0$, therefore does not couple to the field. Only motions that move the atoms of a cell against each other, the optical modes, polarize the crystal at long wavelength.

Each dipole sits at its own atom

In the dipole of atom $\ell\kappa$ sits at the atom, $\btau_{\ell\kappa}=\bR_\ell+\btau_\kappa$, not at the corner $\bR_\ell$ of its cell, and term 6 takes the field there. At long wavelength this placement decides whether the conclusion just drawn, that rigid motion carries no dipole, still holds in a wave. In a long acoustic wave every atom moves with the wave at its own position, $\bu_{\ell\kappa}=\bd\,\ee^{\ii\bq\cdot\btau_{\ell\kappa}}$ to lowest order in $q$. The physical displacement is the real part, and $\bd$ is the common amplitude: locally the wave is a rigid translation. The Fourier component of the polarization at the wavevector of the wave is then

$$\begin{aligned}&\int\dd^3r\,\ee^{-\ii\bq\cdot\br}\,\bP(\br)\\ &=e\sum_{\ell\kappa}\mathbf Z^*_\kappa\cdot\bd\;\ee^{\ii\bq\cdot\btau_{\ell\kappa}}\,\ee^{-\ii\bq\cdot\btau_{\ell\kappa}}\\ &=eN_\ell\sum_\kappa\mathbf Z^*_\kappa\cdot\bd=0 .\end{aligned} \tag{B2.13}$$

Here $N_\ell$ is the number of cells. The phase of the wave at each atom cancels the phase of the delta function at the same atom, and the sum rule removes what is left: the rigid part of the motion leaves no polarization at the wavevector of the wave, whatever that wavevector is. Only the relative displacements of the atoms within a cell, which are of first order in $q$ for an acoustic wave, can polarize the crystal. Had every dipole been put at its cell corner, the delta function would carry $\ee^{-\ii\bq\cdot\bR_\ell}$ instead, and the result would be $eN_\ell\sum_\kappa\mathbf Z^*_\kappa\cdot\bd\,\ee^{\ii\bq\cdot\btau_\kappa}\simeq\ii eN_\ell\sum_\kappa(\bq\cdot\btau_\kappa)\,\mathbf Z^*_\kappa\cdot\bd$: a spurious polarization of first order in $q$ that changes when an atom is assigned to a neighbouring cell. This is the basis-phase rule of Section B.1: written with phonon eigenvectors, the dipole at the atom puts the factor $e_{\kappa\beta,\nu}(\bq)$$\,\ee^{-\ii(\bq+\bG)\cdot\btau_\kappa}$ into every vertex, , with $\bG$ a reciprocal lattice vector, and only this product is independent of the choice of cell, .8

The Born charge is the right charge

$\mathbf Z^*_\kappa$ is defined at zero macroscopic electric field, the condition met in a transverse optical wave away from the light cone, which is why it is also called the transverse effective charge. It contains the rearrangement of the electrons near the displaced ion, but not their screening of the long-range field of its dipole. That screening must come from somewhere, and in this Lagrangian it comes from the electrons themselves: through term 5 they respond to the field of the dipoles. The polar vertex of Section B.1 needed the electronic dielectric constant $\varepsilon_\infty$ put in by hand. Here the same screening is computed, from the electronic photon self-energy of Section B.5, together with its frequency dependence (Sections B.8 and B.9). A charge that already contained the macroscopic screening would count it twice.

There is no electron–phonon term

No term of contains both an electron coordinate and an ionic displacement. There is no $g$. draws the structure: every line that leaves the electrons or the ions ends on the field.

Apart from the frozen potential of term 1, which does not follow the ions, electrons and ions interact only by sharing $\bA$, through two channels, one for each part of the field.9

  • Longitudinal: the Coulomb channel. A longitudinal polarization wave carries bound charge, $\rho^{\mathrm{ion}}=-\nabla\cdot\bP\neq0$. The electrons' charge and this bound charge are sources of the same longitudinal field, , and the energy of that field contains their cross term: the instantaneous Coulomb energy of the electrons in the field of the ionic dipoles (Section B.4). That is the polar coupling of Section B.1, , without the electronic screening, $\boldsymbol\varepsilon_\infty$ replaced by 1. The electrons, through their own coupling to the same field, then supply the screening (Section B.8).
  • Transverse: the light channel. An electron current emits a transverse photon through term 5, and a polarization current absorbs it through term 6. This channel carries the delay that Section X.1 calls the finite speed of light: the photon takes time to travel, at the speed $c$, unlike the dynamical screening of Part A, which is a delay of the electrons. It has no counterpart in Part A, and it matters only near the light cone of Section X.1, at wavevectors comparable to those of light of the same frequency in the crystal: $q\lesssim\sqrt{\varepsilon_\infty}\,\omega_{\mathrm{TO}}/c$, with $\omega_{\mathrm{TO}}$ the transverse optical frequency and $\sqrt{\varepsilon_\infty}$ the refractive index that the electrons alone, with the ions clamped, give the crystal at that frequency. In GaAs ($\varepsilon_\infty=10.9$, $\hbar\omega_{\mathrm{TO}}=33.3$ meV) that is $5.6\times10^{-5}$ Å⁻¹, against a Brillouin zone of size $2\pi/a=1.11$ Å⁻¹ ($a=5.65$ Å).

Two ionic couplings (term 6) joined through the field make the dipole–dipole interaction between ions: the force removed from term 3, which the field now regenerates, and with it the splitting between longitudinal and transverse optical phonons (Section B.7, Section B.9). What the Lagrangian leaves out

  • Short-range coupling. $V^{\mathrm{en}}_0$ is frozen at the equilibrium sites, so the short-range (deformation-potential) part of the coupling, $g^{\mathrm{sr}}$ of Section A.4, is absent. Only the polar, long-range channel is rebuilt from the field. A complete theory adds $g^{\mathrm{sr}}$ explicitly, computed with density-functional perturbation theory; Section B.1 describes the bookkeeping that avoids double counting.
  • Double counting. Term 3 must contain only the short-range force constants. The same care applies to the electron–electron interaction, whose mean-field part is already inside band energies computed with density-functional theory (Section B.4).
  • Point dipoles at fixed sites. Each atom carries a point dipole, linear in its displacement, at its equilibrium site. Quadrupoles and higher multipoles are dropped. Because the field is taken at the site rather than at the moving ion, the ions feel no magnetic force. As written, the Lagrangian also contains the field of each point dipole at its own site, which diverges; it must be left out, as in any lattice sum of dipoles.
  • Model electrons and ions. The electrons are nonrelativistic and spinless (a factor 2 for spin is written where it enters), with a local pseudopotential. The ions are harmonic, so the anharmonic decay that gives real phonons a finite lifetime is absent; the damping used at the end of this section is added by hand.

Electrons: the Lorentz force

An electron feels the field only where it is. In the temporal gauge that field is described by $\bA$ alone, so both forces of electrodynamics must come out of $\bA$: the electric force from how $\bA$ changes in time at the electron, the magnetic force from how $\bA$ varies in space around the moving electron. shows a free electron in a vector potential that produces a uniform oscillating electric field and a static magnetic field $B$, which bends the path into circles at the cyclotron frequency $\omega_{\mathrm c}=eB/m_{\mathrm e}c$ ($\hbar\omega_{\mathrm c}=0.116$ meV per tesla). The arrows compare the kinetic momentum $m_{\mathrm e}\mathbf v_s$ with the canonical momentum of the Lagrangian, which differs from it by $-\frac ec\bA$.

The derivation takes six steps.11

  1. Collect the terms of that contain $\br_s$ or $\mathbf v_s$:

    $$L_s=\frac12m_{\mathrm e}\mathbf v_s^2-V^{\mathrm{en}}_0(\br_s)-\frac ec\,\mathbf v_s\cdot\bA(\br_s,t) .$$
    The other terms belong to other electrons, to the ions or to the field alone. Here the field enters only through its value at the electron; in term 4 it is a coordinate of its own.

  2. The canonical momentum of the electron is

    $$\begin{aligned}\mathbf p_s&=\frac{\partial L}{\partial\mathbf v_s}\\ &=m_{\mathrm e}\mathbf v_s-\frac ec\,\bA(\br_s,t) .\end{aligned} \tag{B2.15}$$
    Differentiate $L_s$ with respect to each component of $\mathbf v_s$ at fixed $\br_s$. The coupling is linear in $\mathbf v_s$ and contributes $-\frac ec\bA$.

  3. Differentiate $\mathbf p_s$ along the motion:

    $$\frac{\dd\mathbf p_s}{\dd t}=m_{\mathrm e}\dot{\mathbf v}_s-\frac ec\Big[\partial_t\bA+(\mathbf v_s\cdot\nabla)\bA\Big]_{\br=\br_s} .$$
    $\bA(\br_s(t),t)$ changes for two reasons: the field changes in time at a fixed point, and the electron moves to where the field is different. The chain rule gives both; the second is the convective derivative $(\mathbf v_s\cdot\nabla)\bA$.

  4. The other side of is the gradient with respect to the position:

    $$\frac{\partial L}{\partial\br_s}=-\nabla V^{\mathrm{en}}_0(\br_s)-\frac ec\,\nabla\big(\mathbf v_s\cdot\bA\big)\Big|_{\br=\br_s} .$$
    Position and velocity are independent variables of $L$, so $\mathbf v_s$ is held fixed and the gradient acts on $\bA$ only.

  5. Equate the two sides and solve for the acceleration:

    $$\begin{aligned}m_{\mathrm e}\dot{\mathbf v}_s={}&-\nabla V^{\mathrm{en}}_0+\frac ec\,\partial_t\bA\\ &-\frac ec\Big[\nabla(\mathbf v_s\cdot\bA)-(\mathbf v_s\cdot\nabla)\bA\Big] .\end{aligned}$$
    This is for the three components of $\br_s$, with the term $\frac ec(\mathbf v_s\cdot\nabla)\bA$ moved to the right.

  6. The bracket is $\mathbf v_s\times(\nabla\times\bA)$. Then turns $\frac ec\partial_t\bA$ into $-e\bE$ and $\nabla\times\bA$ into $\bB$.

    For a vector $\mathbf v$ that does not depend on position, component $\alpha$ of $\mathbf v\times(\nabla\times\bA)$ is $\sum_\beta v_\beta\,\partial_\alpha A_\beta-\sum_\beta v_\beta\,\partial_\beta A_\alpha$, which is component $\alpha$ of the bracket.

The result is the Lorentz equation for a charge $-e$:

$$\begin{aligned}m_{\mathrm e}\dot{\mathbf v}_s={}&-\nabla V^{\mathrm{en}}_0(\br_s)\\ &-e\,\bE(\br_s,t)\\ &-\frac ec\,\mathbf v_s\times\bB(\br_s,t) .\end{aligned} \tag{B2.16}$$
$\bE(\br_s,t)$, $\bB(\br_s,t)$
total electric and magnetic fields at the electron's position
$\dot{\mathbf v}_s$
acceleration of electron $s$

Term 5 thus gives an electron the correct force, so it is the right coupling. The field in is the total field at the electron. It contains the Coulomb field of every other electron and of the ionic bound charges, through $\bE^{\mathrm L}$, and radiation, through $\bE^{\mathrm T}$; in the temporal gauge both come from $\partial_t\bA$. In a light wave $|\bB|=|\bE|$ (Gaussian units), so the magnetic force is a fraction $v_s/c$ of the electric one: $3\times10^{-3}$ for an electron moving at $10^6$ m/s.

The canonical momentum is not $m_{\mathrm e}\mathbf v_s$. Nor is it a dipole: Sections A.4 and B.1 write dipoles with the letter $\mathbf p$, but in this section a dipole appears only as $e\mathbf Z^*_\kappa\cdot\bu_{\ell\kappa}$, and $\mathbf p_s$ is a momentum. Where $\bA$ does not depend on position, as for a uniform field, $L_s$ with $V^{\mathrm{en}}_0=0$ does not depend on $\br_s$, and $\mathbf p_s$ is conserved. The kinetic momentum then follows the vector potential, $m_{\mathrm e}\mathbf v_s(t)=\mathbf p_s+\frac ec\bA(t)$, and a field pulse changes it by $\frac ec\Delta\bA=-e\int\bE\,\dd t$. With a magnetic field, the component of $\mathbf p_s$ along any direction on which $\bA$ does not depend is still conserved; shows both cases. Section B.4 writes the Hamiltonian with $\mathbf p_s$, and $m_{\mathrm e}\mathbf v_s=\mathbf p_s+\frac ec\bA$ turns the kinetic energy into the minimal-coupling form $(\mathbf p_s+\frac ec\bA)^2/2m_{\mathrm e}$, .

Ions: a lattice driven by the field

For the ions the same rule gives a different kind of equation, because the field couples to the dipole of an ion, not to its position.12

  1. Collect the terms that contain $u_{\ell\kappa\alpha}$ or $\dot u_{\ell\kappa\alpha}$: terms 2, 3 and 6. Written in components, term 6 is

    $$\frac ec\sum_{\ell\kappa}\sum_{\alpha\beta}A_\beta(\btau_{\ell\kappa},t)\,Z^*_{\kappa,\beta\alpha}\,\dot u_{\ell\kappa\alpha} .$$
    Component $\beta$ of $\mathbf Z^*_\kappa\cdot\dot\bu_{\ell\kappa}$ is $\sum_\alpha Z^*_{\kappa,\beta\alpha}\dot u_{\ell\kappa\alpha}$, and it multiplies $A_\beta$. The velocity $\dot u_{\ell\kappa\alpha}$ therefore meets the Born charge through its second index.

  2. The derivative with respect to the displacement is

    $$\frac{\partial L}{\partial u_{\ell\kappa\alpha}}=-\sum_{\ell'\kappa'\beta}\Phi^{\mathrm{sr}}_{\ell\kappa\alpha,\ell'\kappa'\beta}\,u_{\ell'\kappa'\beta} .$$
    Only term 3 depends on the displacements themselves. $u_{\ell\kappa\alpha}$ appears in both slots of the double sum, and $\Phi^{\mathrm{sr}}$ is symmetric like all force constants, so the two contributions are equal and cancel the $\frac12$, exactly as for the forces that Section A.1 derives from .

  3. The canonical momentum of the ion is

    $$\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot u_{\ell\kappa\alpha}}=M_\kappa\dot u_{\ell\kappa\alpha}+\frac ec\sum_\beta Z^*_{\kappa,\beta\alpha}\,A_\beta(\btau_{\ell\kappa},t) .$$
    Term 2 gives the usual mass times velocity. Term 6 is linear in $\dot u_{\ell\kappa\alpha}$, with the coefficient found in step 1.

  4. Its time derivative is

    $$M_\kappa\ddot u_{\ell\kappa\alpha}+\frac ec\sum_\beta Z^*_{\kappa,\beta\alpha}\,\partial_tA_\beta(\btau_{\ell\kappa},t) .$$
    The field is taken at the fixed site $\btau_{\ell\kappa}$, which does not move, so there is no convective term. This is the difference from step 3 for the electron, and it is why the ions feel no magnetic force.

  5. Equate steps 2 and 4, as requires, and replace $\partial_t\bA$ by $-c\bE$, .

    With $\partial_t\bA=-c\bE$, the field term of step 4 becomes $-e\sum_\beta Z^*_{\kappa,\beta\alpha}E_\beta$; moved to the right-hand side, it is the driving force.

Each ion obeys

$$\begin{aligned}&M_\kappa\ddot u_{\ell\kappa\alpha}\\ &=-\sum_{\ell'\kappa'\beta}\Phi^{\mathrm{sr}}_{\ell\kappa\alpha,\ell'\kappa'\beta}\,u_{\ell'\kappa'\beta}\\ &\quad+e\sum_\beta Z^*_{\kappa,\beta\alpha}\,E_\beta(\btau_{\ell\kappa},t) .\end{aligned} \tag{B2.17}$$
$E_\beta(\btau_{\ell\kappa},t)$
component $\beta$ of the total electric field at the equilibrium site of atom $\ell\kappa$
$Z^*_{\kappa,\beta\alpha}$
Born charge with its indices in the order field $\beta$, force $\alpha$: the transpose of the order in the dipole

Each ion is a harmonic oscillator, joined to its neighbours by the short-range springs and driven by the field through its Born charge. The field couples to the dipole $e\mathbf Z^*_\kappa\cdot\bu_{\ell\kappa}$, not to the displacement itself: an atom with $\mathbf Z^*_\kappa=0$ feels no force from the field, however far it moves.

The force that the field exerts along $\alpha$, $F_{\ell\kappa\alpha}$$\,=e\sum_\beta Z^*_{\kappa,\beta\alpha}E_\beta$, contains the transposed Born charge. The dipole along $\alpha$ per unit displacement along $\beta$ is $eZ^*_{\kappa,\alpha\beta}$; the force along $\alpha$ per unit field along $\beta$ is $eZ^*_{\kappa,\beta\alpha}$. These are the two common definitions of the Born charge. That one tensor serves for both, read in two directions, is required by energy conservation. The work of the force in a small displacement $\delta\bu$ is $\sum_\alpha F_{\ell\kappa\alpha}\,\delta u_{\ell\kappa\alpha}=\bE\cdot\big(e\mathbf Z^*_\kappa\cdot\delta\bu\big)$, the decrease of the dipole energy $-e(\mathbf Z^*_\kappa\cdot\bu)\cdot\bE$ found from . For the cubic crystals used below $\mathbf Z^*_\kappa$ is a multiple of the unit tensor, and the order of its indices does not matter.

The field in is the total field at the site. It contains the fields of all the other Born dipoles, so the ions act on each other through the field: this dipole–dipole force is exactly what was removed from term 3. It also contains the field of the electrons, which is how they screen the ions. The neglected magnetic force is a fraction $v/c$ of the electric one; an ion moving with its zero-point amplitude in GaAs (0.04 Å at 33 meV, a speed of about 200 m/s) has $v/c\approx7\times10^{-7}$.

Physical picture: a displaced ion is a charged particle

Compare the canonical momenta: $m_{\mathrm e}\mathbf v_s-\frac ec\bA$ for an electron, and $M_\kappa\dot u_{\ell\kappa\alpha}+\frac ec\sum_\beta Z^*_{\kappa,\beta\alpha}A_\beta$ for an ion. The ion behaves like a particle of charge $e\mathbf Z^*_\kappa$, a tensor rather than a number, tied to its site by springs. This is why Section B.4 finds the same two kinds of coupling for ions as for electrons: one linear in $\bA$ and one quadratic in $\bA$.

A driven diatomic crystal

The simplest test of is a crystal with two atoms per cell, such as GaAs, driven by a uniform field. Take a cubic crystal. Its Born charges are multiples of the unit tensor, and the sum rule then leaves a single number $Z^*$, as in Section A.4: $Z^*_{1,\alpha\beta}=-Z^*_{2,\alpha\beta}=Z^*\delta_{\alpha\beta}$. Let the field point along $x$, $\bE(t)=E(t)\,\hat{\mathbf x}$ with $\hat{\mathbf x}$ the unit vector along $x$, and let its wavelength be much longer than a cell, so that every cell moves alike.13

  1. Set $u_{\ell\kappa x}=u_\kappa(t)$ in every cell and sum the short-range force constants over the partner cells:

    $$\begin{gathered}\sum_{\ell'}\Phi^{\mathrm{sr}}_{\ell1x,\ell'1x}=\sum_{\ell'}\Phi^{\mathrm{sr}}_{\ell2x,\ell'2x}=K,\\ \sum_{\ell'}\Phi^{\mathrm{sr}}_{\ell1x,\ell'2x}=\sum_{\ell'}\Phi^{\mathrm{sr}}_{\ell2x,\ell'1x}=-K .\end{gathered}$$
    With every cell alike, only these lattice sums enter: they are the force constants at $\bq=0$ of . The acoustic sum rule, , makes each row add up to zero; $\Phi^{\mathrm{sr}}$ obeys it because the full force constants do and so does their dipole–dipole part, , whose sum over the partner atom contains $\sum_{\kappa'}\mathbf Z^*_{\kappa'}=0$. Symmetry and translation invariance make the two cross sums equal, and in a cubic crystal motion along $x$ does not couple to motion along $y$ or $z$.

  2. for the two atoms reads

    $$\begin{aligned}M_1\ddot u_1&=-K(u_1-u_2)+eZ^*E,\\ M_2\ddot u_2&=-K(u_2-u_1)-eZ^*E .\end{aligned}$$
    The springs pull on the separation of the two atoms only, and the field pushes them in opposite directions with equal force.

  3. Adding the two equations gives $M_1\ddot u_1+M_2\ddot u_2=0$: the centre of mass of the cell does not move. Dividing each by its mass and subtracting gives, for the relative displacement $u_{\mathrm{rel}}=u_1-u_2$,

    $$M_{\mathrm r}\ddot u_{\mathrm{rel}}=-Ku_{\mathrm{rel}}+eZ^*E . \tag{B2.18}$$
    $\frac1{M_1}+\frac1{M_2}=\frac1{M_{\mathrm r}}$ defines the reduced mass $M_{\mathrm r}$ of Section A.1. The field exerts no net force on a neutral cell, so it drives the optical motion only.

  4. Add a damping force $-M_{\mathrm r}\gamma\dot u_{\mathrm{rel}}$ and drive at frequency $\omega$, $E(t)=\mathrm{Re}\big[E_0\ee^{-\ii\omega t}\big]$. The steady state is $u_{\mathrm{rel}}(t)=\mathrm{Re}\big[\xi\,\ee^{-\ii\omega t}\big]$, with the complex amplitude $\xi$

    $$\begin{gathered}\xi=\frac{eZ^*E_0/M_{\mathrm r}}{\omega_0^2-\omega^2-\ii\gamma\omega},\\ \omega_0^2=K/M_{\mathrm r} .\end{gathered} \tag{B2.19}$$

For $Z^*E_0\gt0$ the numerator of is positive, so the displacement $u_{\mathrm{rel}}(t)=|\xi|\cos(\omega t-\varphi)$ lags behind the field by the phase $\varphi=\arg\xi$, the argument of $\omega_0^2-\omega^2+\ii\gamma\omega$. Below resonance it follows the field in phase ($\varphi\simeq0$), at resonance it lags by $\varphi=90°$, and above resonance it moves against the field ($\varphi\to180°$). The polarization of the crystal points along $x$, $P_x=eZ^*u_{\mathrm{rel}}/\OV$ (the cell average of ), and its rate of change is the polarization current.
