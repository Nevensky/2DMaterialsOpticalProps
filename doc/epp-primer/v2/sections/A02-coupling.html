The electron–phonon Hamiltonian and its matrix element

An electron in a crystal moves in the periodic potential of the nuclei. When the nuclei vibrate, that potential changes, and the change scatters the electron while a phonon is absorbed or emitted. This section describes the electrons from scratch, derives the coupling and its matrix element $g$ step by step from the phonons of Section A.1, and reads the standard electron–phonon Hamiltonian term by term. A short history closes it: almost every step toward computing $g$ taught something about how electrons screen a moving ion.

What you need: Section A.1, above all the displacement operator , the normal modes and the Born–von Kármán supercell; Bloch's theorem and band structures; first-order perturbation theory; the Fourier transform of the Coulomb potential. Two background boxes teach what may be less familiar: density-functional theory, and the second quantization of electrons. Equations are in Gaussian units, and key equations with charges carry an SI footnote.1

The picture: an electron in a vibrating lattice

In the Born–Oppenheimer picture of Section A.1, the electrons stay in their ground state for the current positions of the nuclei. With the nuclei at rest at their equilibrium sites, an electron moves in a perfectly periodic potential. It occupies a Bloch state with a definite wavevector $\bk$ and is never scattered. Now displace one nucleus by a small vector $\bu$. The potential energy $V(\br)$ that the electron feels from that nucleus at a point $\br$ slides along with it and becomes $V(\br-\bu)$. To first order in $\bu$ this is $V(\br)-\bu\cdot\nabla V(\br)$, with $\nabla V$ the gradient of $V$, so the potential changes by $-\bu\cdot\nabla V$. That change is not periodic, and it can move the electron from one Bloch state to another.

In a crystal the nuclei do not move one at a time: they move in the waves of Section A.1. A wave of wavevector $\bq$ creates a potential change that is itself a wave of wavevector $\bq$, and this wave moves the electron from $\bk$ to $\bk+\bq$. In quantum mechanics the displacement is an operator that creates and destroys phonons, so the same event absorbs a phonon of wavevector $\bq$ or emits one of wavevector $-\bq$. The strength of the event is the electron–phonon matrix element $g$. Two numbers give its size: the zero-point amplitude of a nucleus, about 0.03 Å (Section A.1), and the gradient of the potential near a nucleus, a few eV/Å. Their product is of order 0.1 eV.

The section follows this picture:

  1. describe the electrons of the perfect crystal: Bloch states, bands, occupations, and the language of second quantization;
  2. expand the potential felt by an electron to first order in the displacements, and show that a wave $\bq$ moves an electron from $\bk$ to $\bk+\bq$;
  3. insert the displacement operator of Section A.1, which gives the coupling Hamiltonian and the matrix element $g$;
  4. read the complete Hamiltonian term by term, and list what it leaves out;
  5. follow the history of attempts to compute $g$, up to Bardeen's screened vertex and the first-principles methods of Section A.3, which compute $g$ from the atomic numbers and masses, with no parameter fitted to experiment.

Electrons in a perfect crystal

Electrons interact with the nuclei and with each other. Density-functional theory replaces this many-electron problem by independent electrons that move in one common effective potential, chosen so that the density of the independent electrons equals the true ground-state density (background box below). Each of these Kohn–Sham electrons obeys a one-electron Schrödinger equation:

$$\begin{gathered}\Big[-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m_{\mathrm e}}\nabla^2+V^{\mathrm{KS}}(\br)\Big]\psi_{n\bk}(\br)\\ =E_{n\bk}\,\psi_{n\bk}(\br),\\ V^{\mathrm{KS}}=V^{\mathrm{en}}+V^{\mathrm H}+V^{\mathrm{xc}} .\end{gathered} \tag{A2.1}$$
$m_{\mathrm e}$
electron mass
$V^{\mathrm{KS}}(\br)$
Kohn–Sham potential: the potential energy of an electron at $\br$ (eV), the sum of the three terms below
$V^{\mathrm{en}}$
electron–nucleus potential energy, the attraction of all nuclei,
$V^{\mathrm H}$
Hartree potential energy, the classical electrostatic repulsion by the average density of all electrons,
$V^{\mathrm{xc}}$
exchange–correlation potential energy, the quantum remainder: the effects of the Pauli principle (exchange) and of the correlated motion of the electrons
$\psi_{n\bk}$, $E_{n\bk}$
one-electron state and its energy (eV), labelled by a band index $n$ and a wavevector $\bk$, both explained below

Every potential in this primer is a potential energy of an electron, in eV, not an electrostatic potential. The nuclei enter through

$$\begin{gathered}V^{\mathrm{en}}(\br)=\sum_{\ell\kappa}V_\kappa(\br-\btau_{\ell\kappa}),\\ V_\kappa(\br)=-\frac{Z_\kappa e^2}{|\br|}\ \ \text{(bare nucleus)} .\end{gathered} \tag{A2.2}$$
$e$
elementary charge, $e\gt0$, so that the electron carries $-e$; in Gaussian units $e^2=14.3996$ eV Å
$V_\kappa(\br)$
potential energy of an electron at the position $\br$ relative to the ion $\kappa$ (eV): the nucleus of atom $\kappa$ or, in most calculations, the nucleus together with its tightly bound core electrons. For a bare nucleus it is the Coulomb attraction shown, with $Z_\kappa$ the atomic number. For a nucleus with its core it is a pseudopotential: it has the same tail $-Z_\kappa e^2/|\br|$, with $Z_\kappa$ now the number of valence electrons, and it is finite at $\br=0$
$Z_\kappa$
charge of ion $\kappa$ in units of $e$
$\btau_{\ell\kappa}$
equilibrium position of atom $\kappa$ of cell $\ell$, ; the sum runs over every cell of the crystal, the Born–von Kármán supercell and its periodic images

Ion $\ell\kappa$ sits at $\btau_{\ell\kappa}$, the position of atom $\kappa$ in cell $\ell$, so $V_\kappa(\br-\btau_{\ell\kappa})$ is the potential of that one ion and the sum adds all of them.2

Bloch states on the supercell

With the nuclei at their equilibrium sites, $V^{\mathrm{KS}}$ is periodic: $V^{\mathrm{KS}}(\br+\bR_\ell)=V^{\mathrm{KS}}(\br)$ for every lattice vector $\bR_\ell$. The argument of Section A.1 that turned translation invariance into waves applies here as well. A shift of the crystal by one cell commutes with the Hamiltonian, so the eigenstates can be chosen to pick up a pure phase under that shift, $\psi(\br+\bR_\ell)=\ee^{\ii\bk\cdot\bR_\ell}\psi(\br)$. This is Bloch's theorem. The Born–von Kármán condition, that $\psi$ repeats after $N_j$ cells along the primitive vector $\ba_j$, puts $\bk$ on the same grid of $N_\ell$ wavevectors as the phonon wavevector $\bq$, and $\bk$ and $\bk+\bG$ describe the same state, for every reciprocal lattice vector $\bG$. The primer normalizes the states on the supercell:

$$\begin{gathered}\psi_{n\bk}(\br)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_\ell}}\,\ee^{\ii\bk\cdot\br}\,u_{n\bk}(\br),\\ u_{n\bk}(\br+\bR_\ell)=u_{n\bk}(\br),\\ \int_{\mathrm{SC}}\!|\psi_{n\bk}|^2\dd^3r=\int_{\mathrm{cell}}\!|u_{n\bk}|^2\dd^3r=1 .\end{gathered} \tag{A2.3}$$
$\bk$
electron wavevector (Å⁻¹), one of the $N_\bk=N_\ell$ points of the Born–von Kármán grid in the first Brillouin zone; $\hbar\bk$ is the electron's crystal momentum
$n$
band index, which numbers the states at each $\bk$ by increasing energy
$u_{n\bk}(\br)$
periodic part of the Bloch state, normalized on one unit cell (Å$^{-3/2}$)
$\int_{\mathrm{SC}}$, $\int_{\mathrm{cell}}$
integrals over the supercell and over one unit cell

The two normalizations agree because $|\psi_{n\bk}|^2=|u_{n\bk}|^2/N_\ell$ is periodic: the supercell integral is $N_\ell$ equal cell integrals, each divided by $N_\ell$. Plotted against $\bk$, each $E_{n\bk}$ traces a band. Two conventions complete the labels. First, a state carries the same label at $\bk$ and at $\bk+\bG$, $\psi_{n\bk+\bG}=\psi_{n\bk}$, which by means $u_{n\bk+\bG}(\br)=\ee^{-\ii\bG\cdot\br}u_{n\bk}(\br)$. Second, the phase of each $\psi_{n\bk}$ is free, like the phase of a phonon eigenvector in Section A.1, and nothing measurable depends on it. Each state holds two electrons of opposite spin. Spin plays no role in the coupling, so the labels carry none, and a factor 2 is written where the two spin channels add.3

Occupations and the electron density

In thermal equilibrium at temperature $T$, the state $n\bk$ is occupied, for each spin, with the Fermi–Dirac probability

$$f_{n\bk}=\frac{1}{\ee^{(E_{n\bk}-\mu)/\kB T}+1} . \tag{A2.4}$$
$f_{n\bk}$
occupation of the state $n\bk$ for one spin, between 0 and 1
$\mu$
chemical potential (eV), fixed by the total number of electrons, $2\sum_{n\bk}f_{n\bk}$

At $T=0$ the states below $\mu$ are full and those above it are empty. In a metal a band crosses $\mu$. The energy of the highest occupied state at $T=0$ is then the Fermi energy $E_{\mathrm F}$, and the wavevectors at which a band crosses it form the Fermi surface. In an insulator $\mu$ lies in the gap between the highest occupied band (the valence band) and the lowest empty one (the conduction band). The occupied states make up the electron density, and the density makes the Hartree potential energy:

$$\begin{gathered}n(\br)=2\sum_{n\bk}f_{n\bk}\,|\psi_{n\bk}(\br)|^2,\\ V^{\mathrm H}(\br)=e^2\!\int\!\dd^3r'\,\frac{n(\br')}{|\br-\br'|} .\end{gathered} \tag{A2.5}$$
$n(\br)$
electron number density (Å⁻³); the factor 2 counts both spins. It always carries its argument, which tells it apart from the band index $n$ and from the phonon occupation $n_{\bq\nu}$

The exchange–correlation potential is $V^{\mathrm{xc}}(\br)=\delta E^{\mathrm{xc}}/\delta n(\br)$, the change of the exchange–correlation energy $E^{\mathrm{xc}}[n]$ per unit change of the density at the point $\br$. $E^{\mathrm{xc}}$ is a functional of the density, a number that depends on the whole function $n(\br)$; its exact form is unknown, and density-functional theory approximates it (background box below). Since $V^{\mathrm H}$ and $V^{\mathrm{xc}}$ depend on the density, and the density on the states, equations to are solved together, iterating until the potential that comes out is the potential that went in: a self-consistent solution.4

Density-functional theory in brief skip if familiar

Hohenberg and Kohn proved that the ground-state density $n(\br)$ of interacting electrons determines the potential of the nuclei that produced it, and with it every ground-state property. The ground-state energy is therefore a functional of the density, minimal at the true density. Kohn and Sham wrote this functional as four parts: the kinetic energy of independent electrons with the same density, their energy in the field of the nuclei, the classical Coulomb energy of the density with itself, and a remainder, the exchange–correlation energy $E^{\mathrm{xc}}[n]$. Minimizing over the orbitals of independent electrons gives the one-electron equations , in which the classical Coulomb energy produces $V^{\mathrm H}$ and the remainder produces $V^{\mathrm{xc}}=\delta E^{\mathrm{xc}}/\delta n$: a change $\delta n(\br)$ of the density changes $E^{\mathrm{xc}}$ by $\int V^{\mathrm{xc}}(\br)\,\delta n(\br)\,\dd^3r$.

Only $E^{\mathrm{xc}}$ is unknown. The local-density approximation takes, at each point, the exchange–correlation energy per electron of a uniform electron gas of the local density, and more refined functionals add gradients of the density. The same calculation gives the total energy for any frozen arrangement of the nuclei, which is the Born–Oppenheimer energy surface $U$ of Section A.1, and its second derivatives are the force constants. The one-electron energies $E_{n\bk}$ are not exact excitation energies: band gaps come out too small, about 0.5 eV in silicon against 1.17 eV measured (literature values, illustrative). Many-body corrections (Sections A.5 and A.7) repair this.

Second quantization of electrons skip if familiar

A state of many identical fermions can be described by listing how many electrons occupy each one-electron state $a$ of a complete set (here $a=n\bk$): 0 or 1, by the Pauli principle. The creation operator $c^\dagger_a$ adds an electron in state $a$, and the annihilation operator $c_a$ removes one. They obey the anticommutation rules

$$\begin{gathered}\{c_a,c^\dagger_b\}=c_ac^\dagger_b+c^\dagger_bc_a=\delta_{ab},\\ \{c_a,c_b\}=\{c^\dagger_a,c^\dagger_b\}=0 .\end{gathered}$$

The second rule with $a=b$ gives $(c^\dagger_a)^2=0$: no state can be filled twice. The operator $\hat n_a=c^\dagger_ac_a$ counts the electrons in state $a$, with eigenvalues 0 and 1. Interchanging two creation operators changes the sign of the state, which builds in the antisymmetry of fermion wavefunctions, so Slater determinants never have to be written out.

A one-body operator acts on one electron at a time, $\hat O=\sum_so(\br_s)$ summed over all electrons $s$ at their positions $\br_s$, like a potential energy. In second quantization it reads

$$\begin{gathered}\hat O=\sum_{ab}\langle a|o|b\rangle\,c^\dagger_ac_b,\\ \langle a|o|b\rangle=\int\dd^3r\,\psi^*_a(\br)\,o(\br)\,\psi_b(\br) .\end{gathered}$$

Each term removes an electron from $b$ and puts it into $a$ with the amplitude $\langle a|o|b\rangle$. For a single electron this is ordinary quantum mechanics. Acting on $c^\dagger_b|0\rangle$, the state with one electron in $b$ (where $|0\rangle$ holds no electrons), the operator gives $\sum_a\langle a|o|b\rangle\,c^\dagger_a|0\rangle$; this is $o\,\psi_b=\sum_a\psi_a\langle a|o|b\rangle$ written in the basis of states. If the basis diagonalizes $o$, only the terms with $a=b$ survive, and $\hat O=\sum_ao_a\hat n_a$: each occupied state contributes its eigenvalue $o_a$. For independent electrons in thermal equilibrium, $\langle c^\dagger_ac_b\rangle=\delta_{ab}f_a$. Electron operators commute with the phonon operators $\hat a$, $\hat a^\dagger$ of Section A.1, which obey commutation rules instead of anticommutation rules.

The Kohn–Sham Hamiltonian is a one-body operator, diagonal in the Bloch states, so the independent electrons have the Hamiltonian

$$\hat H^{\mathrm e}=\sum_{n\bk}E_{n\bk}\,c^\dagger_{n\bk}c_{n\bk} . \tag{A2.6}$$
$c^\dagger_{n\bk}$, $c_{n\bk}$
creation and annihilation operators of an electron in the Bloch state $\psi_{n\bk}$ (background box). They carry no hats, because no ordinary number shares their name; the phonon operators keep the hats of Section A.1
$\hat H^{\mathrm e}$
Hamiltonian of the independent electrons: the sum of the energies of all occupied states

Treating the electrons as independent particles with sharp energies is an approximation. Section A.5 shows how the interactions dress them into quasiparticles, electron-like excitations with a shifted energy and a finite lifetime, and the Kohn–Sham energies $E_{n\bk}$ can be replaced by quasiparticle energies.5

From moving nuclei to a coupling

A frozen displacement changes the potential

Displace the atoms by small amounts $u_{\ell\kappa\alpha}$ and hold them there: a frozen displacement pattern, made of ordinary numbers for now. Two effects change the potential felt by an electron. The ions carry their potentials $V_\kappa$ with them, which changes $V^{\mathrm{en}}$. And the electrons rearrange around the new positions, which changes the density and with it $V^{\mathrm H}$ and $V^{\mathrm{xc}}$. To first order in the displacements,

$$\begin{gathered}V^{\mathrm{KS}}(\br;\{\btau+\bu\})=V^{\mathrm{KS}}(\br;\{\btau\})\\ +\sum_{\ell\kappa\alpha}\frac{\partial V^{\mathrm{KS}}(\br)}{\partial u_{\ell\kappa\alpha}}\,u_{\ell\kappa\alpha} .\end{gathered} \tag{A2.7}$$
$\{\btau\}$, $\{\btau+\bu\}$
all atoms at their equilibrium sites; all atoms displaced
$\partial V^{\mathrm{KS}}(\br)/\partial u_{\ell\kappa\alpha}$
change of the potential energy at $\br$ per unit displacement of atom $\ell\kappa$ along $\alpha$, with all other atoms at rest and the electrons in their new ground state, evaluated at equilibrium (eV/Å)

The part due to the ions can be written down at once. Moving ion $\ell\kappa$ by $\bu_{\ell\kappa}$ turns its potential into $V_\kappa(\br-\btau_{\ell\kappa}-\bu_{\ell\kappa})\simeq V_\kappa(\br-\btau_{\ell\kappa})-\bu_{\ell\kappa}\cdot\nabla V_\kappa(\br-\btau_{\ell\kappa})$, so

$$\frac{\partial V^{\mathrm{en}}(\br)}{\partial u_{\ell\kappa\alpha}}=-\partial_\alpha V_\kappa(\br-\btau_{\ell\kappa}) , \tag{A2.8}$$

with $\partial_\alpha=\partial/\partial r_\alpha$: minus the gradient of the ion's potential, evaluated where the electron is. Keeping only this part, as if the other electrons did not respond, is the rigid-ion approximation. The response of the electrons adds $\partial V^{\mathrm H}/\partial u_{\ell\kappa\alpha}$ and $\partial V^{\mathrm{xc}}/\partial u_{\ell\kappa\alpha}$, which oppose the change: the electrons screen it, that is, they rearrange so as to weaken it. Section A.3 computes the full self-consistent change. This section keeps the general $\partial V^{\mathrm{KS}}/\partial u_{\ell\kappa\alpha}$ and uses the rigid-ion form only in examples.

Two numbers justify stopping at first order. A zero-point amplitude of 0.03 Å is about 2 % of a bond of 1.5 Å. The gradient of an ion's potential where the electrons are is a few eV/Å, so the potential changes by about 0.1 eV, small against band energies of several eV. The second-order term returns below, as the Debye–Waller coupling.6

One property of the derivative is needed below. Pushing atom $\kappa$ of cell $\ell$ and looking at the point $\br$ is the same as pushing atom $\kappa$ of cell 0 and looking at $\br-\bR_\ell$, because a translation by $\bR_\ell$ maps the perfect crystal onto itself:

$$\frac{\partial V^{\mathrm{KS}}(\br)}{\partial u_{\ell\kappa\alpha}}=\frac{\partial V^{\mathrm{KS}}(\br-\bR_\ell)}{\partial u_{0\kappa\alpha}} . \tag{A2.9}$$

For the rigid-ion part this is visible in , since $\btau_{\ell\kappa}=\bR_\ell+\btau_\kappa$. It is the counterpart for potentials of the translation invariance of the force constants in Section A.1.

A wave of displacements is a wave of potential

Now displace the atoms in a wave of wavevector $\bq$, as in Section A.1: atom $\kappa$ of cell $\ell$ moves by $u_{\ell\kappa\alpha}=\xi_{\kappa\alpha}\,\ee^{\ii\bq\cdot\bR_\ell}$, with one complex amplitude $\xi_{\kappa\alpha}$ (Å) for each atom of the cell and each direction $\alpha$, and the phase of the cell. The first-order term of is linear in the displacements, with real coefficients. So, as in Section A.1, the complex wave can be used directly: the physical displacement is its real part, half the sum of the wave and its complex conjugate (the partner wave at $-\bq$), and the potential change it causes is the real part of the change computed for the complex wave. Inserting the wave and doing the sum over cells first gives

$$\begin{gathered}\Delta V(\br)=\sum_{\kappa\alpha}\xi_{\kappa\alpha}\,\partial_{\kappa\alpha,\bq}V(\br),\\ \partial_{\kappa\alpha,\bq}V(\br)=\sum_\ell\ee^{\ii\bq\cdot\bR_\ell}\,\frac{\partial V(\br)}{\partial u_{\ell\kappa\alpha}} .\end{gathered} \tag{A2.10}$$
$\Delta V(\br)$
change of the potential energy caused by the frozen wave (eV)
$\partial_{\kappa\alpha,\bq}V(\br)$
potential change per unit amplitude when every atom $\kappa$ moves along $\alpha$ with the phase $\ee^{\ii\bq\cdot\bR_\ell}$ of its cell (eV/Å). The phase sits on the cell of the displaced atom. $V$ stands for $V^{\mathrm{KS}}$ or for its rigid-ion part $V^{\mathrm{en}}$

The potential change of a wave is itself a wave. Shifting the point of observation by a lattice vector $\bR_{\ell'}$ multiplies it by the phase of that cell:

$$\begin{gathered}\partial_{\kappa\alpha,\bq}V(\br+\bR_{\ell'})\\ =\ee^{\ii\bq\cdot\bR_{\ell'}}\,\partial_{\kappa\alpha,\bq}V(\br) .\end{gathered} \tag{A2.11}$$

  1. By , each term of the sum, evaluated at $\br+\bR_{\ell'}$, is a term at $\br$ with the pushed atom moved back by $\bR_{\ell'}$: $\partial V(\br+\bR_{\ell'})/\partial u_{\ell\kappa\alpha}=\partial V(\br)/\partial u_{\ell''\kappa\alpha}$, where $\bR_{\ell''}=\bR_\ell-\bR_{\ell'}$.

    Moving the observer forward by $\bR_{\ell'}$ is the same as moving the pushed atom back by $\bR_{\ell'}$.

  2. Write the phase of cell $\ell$ as $\ee^{\ii\bq\cdot\bR_\ell}=\ee^{\ii\bq\cdot\bR_{\ell'}}\,\ee^{\ii\bq\cdot\bR_{\ell''}}$, and sum over $\ell''$ instead of $\ell$.

    As $\ell$ runs over all cells, so does $\ell''$; the relabelling changes no term.

  3. The factor $\ee^{\ii\bq\cdot\bR_{\ell'}}$ is common to all terms and comes out of the sum. What remains is $\partial_{\kappa\alpha,\bq}V(\br)$, which proves .

    This is the relabelling that removed the cell index from Newton's equations in Section A.1.

Equivalently, $\ee^{-\ii\bq\cdot\br}\,\partial_{\kappa\alpha,\bq}V(\br)$ is a periodic function, as multiplying both sides of by $\ee^{-\ii\bq\cdot(\br+\bR_{\ell'})}$ shows: the potential change has the form of a Bloch function with wavevector $\bq$. It carries crystal momentum $\hbar\bq$, which is why it can move an electron from $\bk$ to $\bk+\bq$, as shown next. It is also why Section A.3 can compute it in a single unit cell for each $\bq$, without a supercell.

In a chain with one atom per cell that moves only along the chain, the labels $\kappa$ and $\alpha$ drop out, and reads $\Delta V(x)=\xi\,\partial_qV(x)$, with $x$ the position along the chain. shows such a frozen wave, the potential change it creates, and what happens when that change is sandwiched between two Bloch states.

Why $\bk$ goes to $\bk+\bq$

The electron feels the potential change through its matrix elements between two Bloch states: in first-order perturbation theory, the amplitude of a transition is the matrix element of the perturbation between the initial and the final state. With band indices $n$ and $m$ and wavevectors $\bk$ and $\bk'$, the matrix element is $\langle\psi_{m\bk'}|\partial_{\kappa\alpha,\bq}V|\psi_{n\bk}\rangle=\int_{\mathrm{SC}}\psi^*_{m\bk'}\,\partial_{\kappa\alpha,\bq}V\,\psi_{n\bk}\,\dd^3r$. Lattice periodicity makes almost all of them vanish:

  1. Split the supercell into its $N_\ell$ cells, writing each point as $\br+\bR_{\ell'}$ with $\br$ in cell 0:

    $$\int_{\mathrm{SC}}I(\br)\,\dd^3r=\sum_{\ell'}\int_{\mathrm{cell}}I(\br+\bR_{\ell'})\,\dd^3r .$$
    The cells tile the supercell without overlap. Here $I(\br)$ stands for any integrand, and each term of the sum, the integral over cell $\ell'$, is a cell integral $I_{\ell'}$.

  2. Translate each factor of the integrand by $\bR_{\ell'}$. Bloch's theorem gives $\psi_{n\bk}(\br+\bR_{\ell'})=\ee^{\ii\bk\cdot\bR_{\ell'}}\psi_{n\bk}(\br)$ and $\psi^*_{m\bk'}(\br+\bR_{\ell'})=\ee^{-\ii\bk'\cdot\bR_{\ell'}}\psi^*_{m\bk'}(\br)$, and gives the phase $\ee^{\ii\bq\cdot\bR_{\ell'}}$ for the potential change.

    All three factors are Bloch-type functions, so a translation multiplies each by a phase, and the phases multiply.

  3. The integrand in cell $\ell'$ is therefore the integrand in cell 0 times $\ee^{\ii(\bk+\bq-\bk')\cdot\bR_{\ell'}}$:

    $$\begin{gathered}\langle\psi_{m\bk'}|\partial_{\kappa\alpha,\bq}V|\psi_{n\bk}\rangle=\Big(\sum_{\ell'}\ee^{\ii(\bk+\bq-\bk')\cdot\bR_{\ell'}}\Big)\\ \times\int_{\mathrm{cell}}\psi^*_{m\bk'}\,\partial_{\kappa\alpha,\bq}V\,\psi_{n\bk}\,\dd^3r .\end{gathered}$$
    Every cell contributes the same integral, turned by its own phase. These are the cell integrals $I_\ell$ of , drawn there as hands on a dial.

  4. The lattice sum equals $N_\ell$ if $\bk'=\bk+\bq+\bG$ for some reciprocal lattice vector $\bG$, and zero otherwise.

    This is the lattice sum of Section A.1: for wavevectors on the grid, the phases are unit arrows spread evenly around the circle unless their wavevector is a reciprocal lattice vector (). Since $\bk+\bq+\bG$ labels the same state as $\bk+\bq$, only the final state $m\,\bk+\bq$ survives.

  5. Insert the Bloch form into the surviving term. The plane waves $\ee^{-\ii(\bk+\bq)\cdot\br}$ and $\ee^{\ii\bk\cdot\br}$ combine into $\ee^{-\ii\bq\cdot\br}$, and the factor $N_\ell$ from the lattice sum cancels the $1/N_\ell$ from the two normalizations:

    $$\begin{gathered}\langle\psi_{m\bk'}|\partial_{\kappa\alpha,\bq}V|\psi_{n\bk}\rangle\\ =\delta_{\bk',\bk+\bq}\int_{\mathrm{cell}}u^*_{m\bk+\bq}\\ \times\big[\ee^{-\ii\bq\cdot\br}\partial_{\kappa\alpha,\bq}V\big]\,u_{n\bk}\,\dd^3r .\end{gathered} \tag{A2.12}$$
    The Kronecker delta $\delta_{\bk',\bk+\bq}$ is read modulo reciprocal lattice vectors: it equals 1 when $\bk'$ and $\bk+\bq$ label the same state, and 0 otherwise. All three functions in the remaining integral are periodic.

Two conclusions follow. First, crystal momentum is conserved: a potential change with wavevector $\bq$ moves an electron from $\bk$ to $\bk+\bq$ and nowhere else. When $\bk+\bq$ lies outside the first Brillouin zone, the final state is labelled $\bk+\bq-\bG$ inside it, and the process is called umklapp (German for "flipping over"); a process with $\bG=0$ is called normal. Second, the matrix element is an integral of periodic functions over one unit cell, so it does not depend on the size of the supercell.7

Quantized displacements: the coupling Hamiltonian

In the crystal the displacements are not frozen numbers but quantum operators, given by . Replacing each $u_{\ell\kappa\alpha}$ in the first-order term of by its operator $\hat u_{\ell\kappa\alpha}$ turns the potential change into an operator $\Delta\hat V(\br)$, which acts on the electron through $\br$ and on the phonons through the phonon field $\hat\phi_{\bq\nu}$. Hats mark the phonon operators $\hat u$, $\hat\phi$, $\hat a$ as in Section A.1, because frozen displacements also appear in this section.

  1. Insert the displacement operator:

    $$\begin{gathered}\Delta\hat V(\br)=\sum_{\ell\kappa\alpha}\frac{\partial V(\br)}{\partial u_{\ell\kappa\alpha}}\sum_{\bq\nu}\Big(\frac{\hbar}{2N_\ell M_\kappa\omega_{\bq\nu}}\Big)^{1/2}\\ \times e_{\kappa\alpha,\nu}(\bq)\,\ee^{\ii\bq\cdot\bR_\ell}\,\hat\phi_{\bq\nu} .\end{gathered}$$
    The first-order term is linear in each displacement, so each displacement can be replaced by its operator. Each term of destroys an electron in $n\bk$ and creates one in $m\,\bk+\bq$, while the phonon field does one of two things: its part $\hat a_{\bq\nu}$ absorbs a phonon of wavevector $\bq$, and its part $\hat a^\dagger_{-\bq\nu}$ emits a phonon of wavevector $-\bq$. Either way the electron gains crystal momentum $\hbar\bq$ and the lattice loses it. In the diagrams of and of later sections, each term is drawn as a vertex, a point where an incoming electron line, an outgoing electron line and a phonon line meet, with the amplitude $g/\sqrt{N_\ell}$. The Hamiltonian does not require the energies to balance. It contains every vertex, and energy conservation selects the real transitions among them when rates are computed (Sections A.7 and A.9).9

Where the factor $N_\ell^{-1/2}$ comes from, and why $g$ has none. Two facts combine. The prefactor $N_\ell^{-1/2}$ is the share of one mode's zero-point motion carried by each atom, inherited from . The matrix element $g$ is a cell integral, by : an intensive property of the crystal, the same for a supercell of any size, computed in one unit cell for each $\bk$ and $\bq$. Physical results combine the two consistently. A scattering rate contains $|g|^2/N_\ell$, because Fermi's golden rule squares the amplitude $g/\sqrt{N_\ell}$ of each vertex. Summed over the $N_\ell$ phonon wavevectors, this is an average over the Brillouin zone, $\frac1{N_\ell}\sum_\bq\to\frac{\OV}{(2\pi)^3}\int_{\mathrm{BZ}}\dd^3q$ with $\OV$ the volume of the unit cell, and it stays finite as $N_\ell\to\infty$. This primer keeps $N_\ell^{-1/2}$ outside $g$, as the review does.10

How the sources write $g$

The review writes the matrix element between periodic parts, $g_{mn\nu}(\bk,\bq)=\langle u_{m\bk+\bq}|\Delta_{\bq\nu}v^{\rm KS}|u_{n\bk}\rangle_{\rm uc}$, where $\Delta_{\bq\nu}v^{\rm KS}=\ee^{-\ii\bq\cdot\br}\Delta_{\bq\nu}V^{\rm KS}$ is the periodic part of our $\Delta_{\bq\nu}V^{\mathrm{KS}}$, and its zero-point factor is $l_{\bq\nu}(M_0/M_\kappa)^{1/2}$, with a reference mass $M_0$ that cancels. The EPP manuscript derives the rigid-ion version, puts $N^{-1/2}$ inside the matrix element and couples $c^+_{n\bk}c_{m\bk-\bq}$, so its $g^0_{nm\alpha}(\bk,\bq)$ is $N^{-1/2}g_{nm\nu}(\bk-\bq,\bq)$ in our notation, with the bare ionic potential in place of $V^{\mathrm{KS}}$.

What $g$ is made of

Inserting the definition of $\Delta_{\bq\nu}V$ from into separates three factors:

$$\begin{gathered}g_{mn\nu}(\bk,\bq)=\sum_{\kappa\alpha}\\ \underbrace{\Big(\frac{\hbar}{2M_\kappa\omega_{\bq\nu}}\Big)^{1/2}e_{\kappa\alpha,\nu}(\bq)}_{\text{displacement (Å)}}\\ \times\underbrace{\langle\psi_{m\bk+\bq}|\partial_{\kappa\alpha,\bq}V^{\mathrm{KS}}|\psi_{n\bk}\rangle}_{\text{potential gradient (eV/Å)}} .\end{gathered} \tag{A2.17}$$
  • The zero-point amplitude $(\hbar/2M_\kappa\omega_{\bq\nu})^{1/2}$ is the size of the quantum fluctuation of atom $\kappa$ in one mode: about 0.03 Å for an optical phonon of silicon, or for the boron atom in an optical phonon of hBN (Section A.1).
  • The eigenvector component $e_{\kappa\alpha,\nu}(\bq)$ says how much of that motion atom $\kappa$ makes along $\alpha$. It projects the potential gradient onto the pattern of the mode.
  • The matrix element of the potential gradient says how strongly the potential at the electron's position changes when the atoms $\kappa$ move along $\alpha$, weighted by the overlap of the initial and final states (eV/Å).

So $g$ is a zero-point amplitude times a potential gradient projected on the mode, and its unit is Å × eV/Å = eV. With an amplitude of 0.03 Å and gradients of 1 to 10 eV/Å, $|g|$ lies between about 30 and 300 meV. First-principles values confirm the estimate. For the highest optical phonon of diamond, with the electron starting at the top of the valence band, $|g|$ lies between about 190 and 450 meV as $\bk+\bq$ runs along the high-symmetry lines of the Brillouin zone. In polar crystals the coupling to longitudinal optical phonons grows without bound as $\bq\to0$ (Section A.4), while the short-range remainder, in anatase TiO$_2$ for example, stays below about 150 meV.11 The model chain of gives values from zero, where symmetry forbids the transition, up to about 170 meV.

Three properties of $g$

Hermiticity. The matrix elements of opposite processes are complex conjugates:

$$g_{nm\nu}(\bk+\bq,-\bq)=g^*_{mn\nu}(\bk,\bq) . \tag{A2.18}$$

The left side moves the electron back from $m\,\bk+\bq$ to $n\bk$ with the phonon field of $-\bq$. Because $\hat\phi^\dagger_{\bq\nu}=\hat\phi_{-\bq\nu}$, the terms of at $(\bk,\bq,m,n)$ and at $(\bk+\bq,-\bq,n,m)$ are then Hermitian conjugates of each other, and $\hat H^{\mathrm{e\text{-}ph}}$ is Hermitian, as an energy must be.

Derivation: the Hermiticity relation

  1. The derivative $\partial V(\br)/\partial u_{\ell\kappa\alpha}$ is real, so by $\partial_{\kappa\alpha,-\bq}V=(\partial_{\kappa\alpha,\bq}V)^*$.

    A real potential changes by a real amount when a real coordinate changes; only the phase $\ee^{\ii\bq\cdot\bR_\ell}$ is complex, and it is conjugated by $\bq\to-\bq$.

  2. Section A.1 chose the eigenvectors so that $e_{\kappa\alpha,\nu}(-\bq)=e^*_{\kappa\alpha,\nu}(\bq)$, with $\omega_{-\bq\nu}=\omega_{\bq\nu}$. Hence $\Delta_{-\bq\nu}V=(\Delta_{\bq\nu}V)^*$.

    Every factor of $\Delta_{\bq\nu}V$ in is either real or conjugated by $\bq\to-\bq$.

  3. Then $g_{nm\nu}(\bk+\bq,-\bq)=\int_{\mathrm{SC}}\psi^*_{n\bk}\,(\Delta_{\bq\nu}V)^*\,\psi_{m\bk+\bq}\,\dd^3r=\Big(\int_{\mathrm{SC}}\psi^*_{m\bk+\bq}\,\Delta_{\bq\nu}V\,\psi_{n\bk}\,\dd^3r\Big)^*=g^*_{mn\nu}(\bk,\bq)$.

    The final state of the left side is $n\,(\bk+\bq)-\bq=n\bk$; conjugating an integral conjugates each factor and swaps the roles of the two states.

The three translations carry no coupling. At $\bq=0$ the three acoustic modes move every atom by the same vector. The potential then moves rigidly with the crystal, and the electrons move along with it: nothing is scattered. These modes have zero frequency and an infinite zero-point amplitude; as in Section A.1 they are left out of every sum, and $g$ is set to zero for them, a consequence of the acoustic sum rule . At small $\bq$ an acoustic wave looks locally like a translation, and in non-polar crystals its coupling vanishes as $\bq\to0$, as Bardeen's result below shows for a metal.12

Phases. $g$ depends on the arbitrary phases of $\psi_{n\bk}$, of $\psi_{m\bk+\bq}$ and of the eigenvector of the mode. Its modulus does not, as long as the states and the mode are not degenerate. When several states or modes share an energy, only the sum of $|g|^2$ over the degenerate set is physical, as Section A.1 noted for the eigenvectors.

contains no electron–electron interaction, no ion–ion interaction, no vector potential and no photons. Yet electrons and nuclei interact only through electromagnetic forces. The Hamiltonian is therefore an effective model in which the Coulomb interaction has been absorbed into screened parameters, and the screening is static: computed as if the electrons responded instantly to the nuclei. The review names the assumptions this requires. The electrons must be well-defined quasiparticles. The phonons must be harmonic and adiabatic: computed, as in Section A.1, with the electrons in their ground state for each frozen arrangement of the nuclei, as if the electrons followed the nuclei instantly. Finally, the Hamiltonian itself gives no prescription for its parameters $E_{n\bk}$, $\omega_{\bq\nu}$, $g$ and $g^{\mathrm{DW}}$. In the review's words, the history of electron–phonon physics is the history of how to compute them.15

One consequence needs care later. The phonon frequencies already contain the static screening of the nuclei by the electrons, through the force constants. When $g$ is used to compute how the electrons change the phonons, that screening must not be counted a second time; Section A.6 shows how the phonon self-energy avoids this. Section A.5 derives the whole structure from a many-body theory that contains only the Coulomb interaction, and Part B puts the full electromagnetic field back.

A short history of $g$

For more than fifty years nobody could compute $g$ from first principles. Bloch wrote down the first matrix element in 1929, in the work that founded the quantum theory of electrical resistance; his "elastic waves of the lattice" were renamed phonons by Frenkel in 1932. The decades that followed replaced the potential gradient in Bloch's formula by better and better guesses. Free electrons: Bloch, Nordheim, Mott and Jones

The early theories were built for simple metals such as sodium, with four simplifications:

  1. one atom per cell, placed at the cell corner, $\btau_\kappa=0$;
  2. free electrons, with energies $\hbar^2|\bk+\bG_n|^2/2m_{\mathrm e}$ measured from the bottom of the band and periodic parts $u_{n\bk}=\ee^{\ii\bG_n\cdot\br}/\sqrt{\OV}$: plane waves in which the reciprocal lattice vector $\bG_n$ plays the role of the band index;

Derivation: the rigid-ion potential change in Fourier space

  1. Write the ion's potential as a Fourier integral, $V_\kappa(\br)=\int\frac{\dd^3q'}{(2\pi)^3}V_\kappa(\bq')\,\ee^{\ii\bq'\cdot\br}$, and differentiate as in :

    $$\begin{gathered}\frac{\partial V^{\mathrm{en}}(\br)}{\partial u_{\ell\kappa\alpha}}=-\int\frac{\dd^3q'}{(2\pi)^3}\,\ii q'_\alpha\,V_\kappa(\bq')\\ \times\ee^{\ii\bq'\cdot(\br-\bR_\ell-\btau_\kappa)} .\end{gathered}$$
    The derivative $\partial_\alpha$ of $\ee^{\ii\bq'\cdot\br}$ brings down $\ii q'_\alpha$, and $\btau_{\ell\kappa}=\bR_\ell+\btau_\kappa$.

  2. Multiply by $\ee^{\ii\bq\cdot\bR_\ell}$ and sum over all cells. The only dependence on $\ell$ is $\ee^{\ii(\bq-\bq')\cdot\bR_\ell}$, and

    $$\sum_\ell\ee^{\ii(\bq-\bq')\cdot\bR_\ell}=\frac{(2\pi)^3}{\OV}\sum_{\bG}\delta^3(\bq'-\bq-\bG) .$$
    The displacement wave repeats with the supercell, so the cells of the periodic images carry the same phases and the sum runs over the infinite lattice. That sum vanishes unless $\bq-\bq'$ is a reciprocal lattice vector, as in Section A.1; as a function of the continuous $\bq'$ it is a set of three-dimensional Dirac deltas $\delta^3$. Each has the weight $(2\pi)^3/\OV$, the volume of the Brillouin zone: integrated over one zone, every term with $\bR_\ell\neq0$ averages to zero, and the term $\ell=0$ gives the zone volume.

Without umklapp, $\bG_m=\bG_n$: the plane wave of the electron changes its wavevector by $\bq$ alone. The potential then enters only through $V_\kappa(\bq)/\OV$, an energy. Bloch replaced it by a constant $V_0$, the average over a cell of an effective potential:

$$\begin{gathered}g_{mn\nu}(\bk,\bq)=-\ii\Big(\frac{\hbar}{2M_\kappa\omega_{\bq\nu}}\Big)^{1/2}\\ \times\bq\cdot\be_{\kappa\nu}(\bq)\,V_0 .\end{gathered} \tag{A2.23}$$

He estimated $V_0=\hbar^2/(16m_{\mathrm e}a_0^2)\approx1.70$ eV by treating the crystal as a deformable continuum, with $a_0=\hbar^2/m_{\mathrm e}e^2=0.529$ Å the Bohr radius. The factor $\bq\cdot\be_{\kappa\nu}$, the so-called polarization factor, means that without umklapp only longitudinal waves scatter electrons: for a transverse wave $\bq\cdot\be_{\kappa\nu}=0$. Nordheim (1931) inserted the bare ion, $V_0\to V_\kappa(\bq)/\OV=-4\pi Z_\kappa e^2/(\OV q^2)$: the rigid-ion approximation. It diverges at long wavelengths. For acoustic phonons, $\omega=v_{\mathrm s}q$, the zero-point amplitude grows as $q^{-1/2}$, so $|g|\propto q\cdot q^{-1/2}\cdot q^{-2}=q^{-3/2}$: unrealistically strong coupling, about 1.7 eV in aluminium at $q=0.88$ Å⁻¹ ( plots it).

Mott and Jones (1936) cut the ion's potential off at the boundary of its own cell, as if the other electrons cancelled it outside, the first rough form of screening. For a Wigner–Seitz sphere, the sphere of the same volume as the cell, with radius $R_{\mathrm{WS}}$ given by $\frac{4\pi}3R_{\mathrm{WS}}^3=\OV$, the cut potential is $V_\kappa(\bq)\,[1-\cos(qR_{\mathrm{WS}})]$. It stays finite as $q\to0$: the potential per cell, the cut potential divided by $\OV$, tends to $-\frac32Z_\kappa e^2/R_{\mathrm{WS}}$. For aluminium, sodium and potassium this is about five to six times deeper than the screened value found next.17

Bardeen: the first screened vertex

The missing piece was the response of the other electrons. When an ion moves, the electrons gather where it has moved to and thin out where it has left, and the charge they move cancels most of the ion's long-range potential. Bardeen (1937) computed this screening for a metal self-consistently, in the linearized Hartree theory: the electrons respond, to first order, to the total potential, the ion's plus the Hartree potential that their own rearrangement creates. The review calls this strategy similar in spirit to modern density-functional perturbation theory, the linear-response method of Section A.3.

The simplest model metal is jellium: free electrons in a uniform positive background (the "jelly"), which stands for the ions smeared out into a constant charge of $Z_\kappa e$ per cell. To see how the electrons screen one ion, that ion is put back as a point charge $Z_\kappa e$, a test charge in the electron gas. The electrons have the mean density $n_0=Z_\kappa/\OV$, $Z_\kappa$ electrons per cell, so the whole is neutral. Two equivalent parameters describe them. The Fermi wavevector $k_{\mathrm F}$ is the radius of the sphere of occupied plane waves, $n_0=k_{\mathrm F}^3/3\pi^2$ (two spins times the $\frac{4\pi}3k_{\mathrm F}^3/(2\pi)^3$ plane waves per unit volume inside the sphere), so $E_{\mathrm F}=\hbar^2k_{\mathrm F}^2/2m_{\mathrm e}$. The density parameter $r_{\mathrm s}$ is the radius, in units of $a_0$, of a sphere that holds one electron, $\frac{4\pi}3(r_{\mathrm s}a_0)^3=1/n_0$: 2.07 for aluminium, 3.93 for sodium and 4.86 for potassium (literature values, illustrative).

Thomas–Fermi screening, the long-wavelength form of this response, follows in five steps:

  1. Let the ion's potential energy $V_\kappa$ act on the electron gas, and call the total potential energy felt by the electrons, the ion's plus the induced part, $V^{\mathrm{scr}}_\kappa=V_\kappa+V^{\mathrm{ind}}$.

    The electrons respond to the total potential, not to the bare one; this is what makes the calculation self-consistent.

  2. Where $V^{\mathrm{scr}}_\kappa$ is negative, the bottom of the band is locally lowered, and electrons flow in until the occupied states again reach the common chemical potential $\mu$. If $V^{\mathrm{scr}}_\kappa$ varies slowly on the scale $1/k_{\mathrm F}$, the local density changes by $n^{\mathrm{ind}}(\br)=-(\partial n_0/\partial\mu)\,V^{\mathrm{scr}}_\kappa(\br)$.

    Lowering the potential by $|V^{\mathrm{scr}}_\kappa|$ at fixed $\mu$ fills the same states as raising $\mu$ by $|V^{\mathrm{scr}}_\kappa|$ in a uniform gas. The derivative $\partial n_0/\partial\mu$ is the density of states at the Fermi level per unit volume, both spins counted.

  3. The induced density creates a Hartree potential energy of its own, , which in Fourier space reads $V^{\mathrm{ind}}(\bq)=(4\pi e^2/q^2)\,n^{\mathrm{ind}}(\bq)$.

    The Fourier transform of $e^2/|\br|$ is $4\pi e^2/q^2$, and the convolution in becomes a product.

  4. Combine the three relations into $V^{\mathrm{scr}}_\kappa=V_\kappa-\frac{4\pi e^2}{q^2}\frac{\partial n_0}{\partial\mu}V^{\mathrm{scr}}_\kappa$, and solve:

    $$\begin{gathered}V^{\mathrm{scr}}_\kappa(\bq)=\frac{V_\kappa(\bq)}{\varepsilon^{\mathrm e}(q)},\\ \varepsilon^{\mathrm e}(q)=1+\frac{k_{\mathrm{TF}}^2}{q^2},\\ k_{\mathrm{TF}}^2=4\pi e^2\,\frac{\partial n_0}{\partial\mu} .\end{gathered} \tag{A2.24}$$
    The induced potential is proportional to the total one, so the total is the bare potential divided by a factor: the dielectric function $\varepsilon^{\mathrm e}(q)$ of the electrons (the superscript e marks the response of the electrons alone), and $k_{\mathrm{TF}}$ is the Thomas–Fermi wavevector.

  5. For free electrons, $n_0=k_{\mathrm F}^3/3\pi^2$ and $\mu=E_{\mathrm F}=\hbar^2k_{\mathrm F}^2/2m_{\mathrm e}$ give $\partial n_0/\partial\mu=m_{\mathrm e}k_{\mathrm F}/\pi^2\hbar^2$, hence $k_{\mathrm{TF}}^2=4k_{\mathrm F}/\pi a_0$.

    $\partial n_0/\partial\mu=(\dd n_0/\dd k_{\mathrm F})/(\dd E_{\mathrm F}/\dd k_{\mathrm F})$, and $a_0=\hbar^2/m_{\mathrm e}e^2$.

The screened ion, $V_\kappa(\bq)/\varepsilon^{\mathrm e}(q)=-4\pi Z_\kappa e^2/(q^2+k_{\mathrm{TF}}^2)$, is finite at $q=0$. In real space it is $-(Z_\kappa e^2/r)\,\ee^{-k_{\mathrm{TF}}r}$, the Coulomb potential cut off beyond the screening length $1/k_{\mathrm{TF}}$: 0.49 Å in aluminium, where $k_{\mathrm{TF}}=2.05$ Å⁻¹ and $k_{\mathrm F}=1.75$ Å⁻¹. Thomas–Fermi theory assumes slow variations. Lindhard's dielectric function gives the same linearized-Hartree response of free electrons at every wavevector. This response, in which each electron reacts to the average potential of the others while exchange and correlation are neglected, is called the random-phase approximation:18

$$\begin{gathered}\varepsilon^{\mathrm e}(q)=1+\frac{k_{\mathrm{TF}}^2}{q^2}\,F_{\mathrm L}\Big(\frac{q}{2k_{\mathrm F}}\Big),\\ F_{\mathrm L}(x)=\frac12+\frac{1-x^2}{4x}\ln\Big|\frac{1+x}{1-x}\Big| .\end{gathered} \tag{A2.25}$$
$F_{\mathrm L}(x)$
Lindhard function of the dimensionless wavevector $x=q/2k_{\mathrm F}$. $F_{\mathrm L}(0)=1$, which recovers Thomas–Fermi; $F_{\mathrm L}(1)=\frac12$; and $F_{\mathrm L}(x)\simeq1/3x^2$ for $x\gg1$, so potentials that vary on scales shorter than the electron wavelength are hardly screened

The slope of $F_{\mathrm L}$ is infinite, logarithmically, at $q=2k_{\mathrm F}$, the largest wavevector that joins two points of the Fermi sphere. This weak singularity returns as kinks in phonon dispersions, the Kohn anomalies of Section A.6. Section A.3 derives the Lindhard function from the response of independent electrons. Replacing $V_\kappa$ by the screened potential in without umklapp gives Bardeen's matrix element, the first screened electron–phonon vertex:

$$\begin{gathered}g_{mn\nu}(\bk,\bq)=-\ii\Big(\frac{\hbar}{2M_\kappa\omega_{\bq\nu}}\Big)^{1/2}\\ \times\bq\cdot\be_{\kappa\nu}(\bq)\,\frac{V_\kappa(\bq)}{\OV\,\varepsilon^{\mathrm e}(q)},\\ \frac{V_\kappa(\bq)}{\varepsilon^{\mathrm e}(q)}=-\frac{4\pi Z_\kappa e^2}{q^2\,\varepsilon^{\mathrm e}(q)} .\end{gathered} \tag{A2.26}$$

At long wavelengths $q^2\varepsilon^{\mathrm e}(q)\to k_{\mathrm{TF}}^2$, in the Lindhard form as in the Thomas–Fermi form since $F_{\mathrm L}(0)=1$. The $1/q^2$ of the ion cancels, and the screened potential per cell tends to a constant, its limit as $q\to0$:

$$\begin{gathered}\frac{V_\kappa(\bq)}{\OV\,\varepsilon^{\mathrm e}(q)}\bigg|_{q\to0}=-\frac{4\pi Z_\kappa e^2}{\OV k_{\mathrm{TF}}^2}\\ =-\frac{n_0}{\partial n_0/\partial\mu}=-\frac23E_{\mathrm F} .\end{gathered} \tag{A2.27}$$

The second equality uses $Z_\kappa/\OV=n_0$ and $k_{\mathrm{TF}}^2=4\pi e^2\,\partial n_0/\partial\mu$; the third uses the free-electron values, $n_0/(\partial n_0/\partial\mu)=(k_{\mathrm F}^3/3\pi^2)/(m_{\mathrm e}k_{\mathrm F}/\pi^2\hbar^2)=\hbar^2k_{\mathrm F}^2/3m_{\mathrm e}=\frac23E_{\mathrm F}$. The limit is $-7.80$ eV in aluminium ($E_{\mathrm F}=11.70$ eV), $-2.16$ eV in sodium and $-1.41$ eV in potassium, so Bloch's 1.70 eV has the right size for an alkali metal. For acoustic phonons the finite potential gives $|g|\propto q^{1/2}$ at small $q$, and the coupling is tens to hundreds of meV: about 270 meV in aluminium and 90 meV in sodium at $q=k_{\mathrm F}/2$, with the atomic masses (26.98 and 22.99 amu) and longitudinal sound velocities of 6400 and 3200 m/s (illustrative), against 1.7 and 1.0 eV for the bare ions.19 compares the screening models, from Bloch's constant to Lindhard's function, for these three metals.

Physical picture: why a metal removes the divergence

Over long distances the electrons of a metal have room to move, and they gather until they cancel the ion's potential: $\varepsilon^{\mathrm e}\to k_{\mathrm{TF}}^2/q^2$ cancels the $1/q^2$ of the ion exactly. What survives is local. A long wave compresses the crystal in some places and dilates it in others, and the electrons, which follow the ions, become denser or thinner there. The bottom of the band then shifts with the local density, so that the electrons stay in equilibrium with one common chemical potential. The limit measures this shift: it is minus the rise of the band bottom per unit relative change of volume, as the subsection on semiconductors below shows. An insulator cannot move charge over long distances. Its dielectric function stays finite as $q\to0$, so a long-range part of the coupling can survive: in polar crystals it is the Fröhlich coupling of Section A.4.

In a real crystal the same idea holds with two refinements. The response is that of the crystal's own electrons, which at each $\bq$ is a matrix in the reciprocal lattice vectors rather than one number. And the scattered electron also feels exchange and correlation. Section A.3 shows that density-functional perturbation theory computes exactly this: the self-consistent change of $V^{\mathrm{KS}}$ is the bare change of $V^{\mathrm{en}}$ screened by the electronic dielectric function, .

Bardeen and Pines: screening that lags

Nakajima (1954) and then Bardeen and Pines (1955) were the first to treat the electron–phonon problem with the methods of quantum field theory. They found that the potential felt by an electron depends on how fast the ion moves, so the screening has to be evaluated at the phonon frequency:

$$\frac{V_\kappa(\bq)}{\OV\,\varepsilon^{\mathrm e}(q)}\ \to\ \frac{V_\kappa(\bq)}{\OV\,\varepsilon^{\mathrm e}(q,\omega_{\bq\nu})} . \tag{A2.28}$$

Here $\varepsilon^{\mathrm e}(q,\omega)$ is the dielectric function for a potential that oscillates at the frequency $\omega$, the dynamic Lindhard function. This is the first retarded electron–phonon vertex: the response of the electrons lags behind the motion of the ions, so the screening depends on frequency. (In Part B the word also refers to the finite speed of light.) Bardeen and Pines also included the exchange interaction in the screening.

For acoustic phonons the static approximation is excellent. What decides is the ratio of the phonon frequency to the rate $qv_{\mathrm F}$ at which electrons at the Fermi surface cross a distance $1/q$, where $v_{\mathrm F}=\hbar k_{\mathrm F}/m_{\mathrm e}$ is the Fermi velocity: $\omega_{\bq\nu}/qv_{\mathrm F}=v_{\mathrm s}/v_{\mathrm F}$. In aluminium $v_{\mathrm s}\approx6400$ m/s and $v_{\mathrm F}\approx2.0\times10^6$ m/s, so the ratio is about $3\times10^{-3}$. The real part of the dynamic Lindhard function then differs from the static one only at second order in this ratio, while a small imaginary part appears, $\mathrm{Im}\,\varepsilon^{\mathrm e}\simeq\frac\pi2(k_{\mathrm{TF}}/q)^2\,(v_{\mathrm s}/v_{\mathrm F})$ for $q\lt2k_{\mathrm F}$. It describes Landau damping, the transfer of the phonon's energy to electron–hole pairs near the Fermi surface. It gives the vertex a phase of about $3\times10^{-3}$ at $q=k_{\mathrm F}$ in aluminium, and the phonon a finite linewidth (Section A.6). Dynamic screening matters only where $\omega\gtrsim qv_{\mathrm F}$, as for optical phonons of small $q$ in doped semiconductors and in graphene. First-principles calculations kept static screening for decades; the first to include the lag appeared in 2006 (Lazzeri and Mauri, for graphene).20

Semiconductors and polar crystals

In a semiconductor the carriers sit near the band extrema, so they scatter mainly off phonons of long wavelength. Bardeen and Shockley (1950) noticed that a long acoustic wave looks locally like a uniform strain of the crystal, and that a strain shifts the band edges. When the volume of a region changes by the fraction $\Delta\OV/\OV$, the band edge there moves by $E_{\mathrm{def}}\,\Delta\OV/\OV$, where

$$E_{\mathrm{def}}=\OV\,\frac{\partial E_{n\bk}}{\partial\OV} \tag{A2.29}$$

is the deformation potential of the band edge $n\bk$ (eV). Over distances much longer than a cell, the displacements of a long wave form a smooth displacement field $\bu(\br)$, and the relative change of volume of a small region is its divergence, $\Delta\OV/\OV=\nabla\cdot\bu$. For a wave $\bu\propto\ee^{\ii\bq\cdot\br}$ this is $\ii\bq\cdot\bu$, which vanishes for a transverse wave: the same polarization factor as in Bloch's formula . Between plane waves, that formula is the matrix element of the potential change $-V_0\nabla\cdot\bu$ for the displacement field $\bu=(\hbar/2M_\kappa\omega_{\bq\nu})^{1/2}\be_{\kappa\nu}(\bq)\,\ee^{\ii\bq\cdot\br}$ of one unit of the phonon field. A band edge that moves by $E_{\mathrm{def}}\nabla\cdot\bu$ therefore enters it as $V_0\to-E_{\mathrm{def}}$, and again $|g|\propto q^{1/2}$. Deformation potentials of a few eV to about ten eV (illustrative) were fitted to measured mobilities, so the method describes rather than predicts; band extrema with anisotropic energy surfaces need shear deformation potentials as well (Dumke, 1956).21

Jellium makes the link with Bardeen's result. Where a long wave dilates the crystal by the fraction $\Delta\OV/\OV$, the electrons thin out with the ions, and since $E_{\mathrm F}\propto n_0^{2/3}$ the Fermi energy, counted from the bottom of the band, drops there by $\frac23E_{\mathrm F}\,\Delta\OV/\OV$. The electrons stay in equilibrium only if the bottom of the band rises by the same amount, which keeps the chemical potential uniform. The band bottom of jellium therefore has the deformation potential $E_{\mathrm{def}}=\frac23E_{\mathrm F}$, and Bloch's formula needs $V_0=-E_{\mathrm{def}}=-\frac23E_{\mathrm F}$: exactly the long-wavelength limit of Bardeen's screened ion.

Ionic crystals add a new channel. Displacing oppositely charged ions creates electric dipoles, and in a longitudinal optical wave the dipoles add up to a macroscopic polarization and a long-range electric field. Fröhlich, Pelzer and Zienau (1950) showed that an electron couples to this field with $g\propto1/q$ at small $q$, with a strength set by the ionic part of the screening: the difference between the inverse high-frequency and the inverse static dielectric constant of the crystal. A metal would cancel the $1/q^2$ of such a potential, as in Bardeen's result; an insulator screens it only by a finite factor, so the singularity survives. Strong coupling dresses an electron with a cloud of lattice distortion that travels with it, a polaron. Section A.4 derives the Fröhlich vertex, , from the charges of the displaced ions.22

From pseudopotentials to first principles

Pseudopotentials made the rigid-ion approach quantitative beyond the alkali metals. Phillips and Kleinman (1959) showed that the orthogonality of the valence states to the core states acts like a repulsive potential near each core. Cohen and Heine (1961) showed that this repulsion largely cancels the attraction of the nucleus, so the valence electrons feel a weak, smooth pseudopotential; this explains why they behave as nearly free in simple metals. Sham (1961) showed that $g$ can be computed with pseudopotentials that move rigidly with their cores, an improved rigid-ion approximation. With phonon dispersions taken from neutron scattering, such calculations gave the resistivity of metals and their mass enhancement (the increase of the electrons' effective mass by the coupling, Section A.7), superconducting critical temperatures, carrier mobilities and the temperature dependence of band gaps.

Density-functional theory removed the rigid-ion assumption. In 1985 Dacorogna, Cohen and Lam computed for aluminium with the self-consistent change of $V^{\mathrm{KS}}$, which includes the rearrangement of the electrons. They used frozen phonons, supercells in which the atoms are displaced along a mode and the potential is computed again, and their only inputs were the atomic number and the mass of aluminium. A wave fits into a supercell only if it repeats with it, so long wavelengths need impractically large supercells. Density-functional perturbation theory (Baroni, Giannozzi and Testa, 1987; Gonze, Allan and Teter, 1992; Savrasov, 1992) removed that limit: it computes $\partial_{\kappa\alpha,\bq}V^{\mathrm{KS}}$ for any $\bq$ in one unit cell, which the Bloch property allows. The cost of a density-functional calculation grows as the cube of the number of electrons, so a supercell of $N_\ell$ cells costs about $N_\ell^3$ times one cell, and it gives the coupling at the $N_\ell$ wavevectors that fit in it; density-functional perturbation theory computes each wavevector at about the cost of one cell. The saving is therefore of order $N_\ell^2$, typically more than a factor of 1000. The first such calculations (1994–1998) reproduced the resistivity, thermal conductivity, mass enhancement and critical temperature of elemental metals such as Al, Cu, Nb and Pb. Wannier interpolation (Giustino, Cohen and Louie, 2007), which computes $g$ on a coarse grid and interpolates it through localized orbitals, then made dense grids of $\bk$ and $\bq$ affordable, and polar materials followed once the long-range part of $g$ was treated analytically (Verdi and Giustino; Sjakste and coworkers, 2015; Section A.4).23

Put free electrons back into , with the potential of rigid ions or with that potential screened by the linear response of the electrons, and the models of Bloch, Nordheim and Bardeen reappear: the whole history of $g$ is contained in one formula.

What stays static and electrostatic

Apart from the Bardeen–Pines vertex , every vertex in this section, density-functional perturbation theory included, uses static screening: the electrons are assumed to follow the ions instantly. And every screening field is the instantaneous Coulomb interaction, the longitudinal part of the electromagnetic field, with no transverse field and no current of moving ions. Sections A.5 and A.6 lift the first restriction; Part B lifts the second.

Kohn–Sham potential energy of an electron, $V^{\mathrm{KS}}=V^{\mathrm{en}}+V^{\mathrm H}+V^{\mathrm{xc}}$ (eV), : the Hartree part, from the average electron density, , and the exchange–correlation part $V^{\mathrm{xc}}=\delta E^{\mathrm{xc}}/\delta n$, the derivative of the exchange–correlation energy functional $E^{\mathrm{xc}}[n]$. $E_{n\bk}$, $n$, $m$
energy of the one-electron (Kohn–Sham or quasiparticle) state $n\bk$ (eV); band indices $n$, $m$, numbering the states at each $\bk$ by increasing energy. Giustino: $\varepsilon_{n\bk}$. EPP: $E^0_{n\bk}$ (Eq. 96).
$V^{\mathrm{en}}$, $V_\kappa(\br)$
electron–nucleus potential energy of the crystal, $\sum_{\ell\kappa}V_\kappa(\br-\btau_{\ell\kappa})$, ; potential energy of an electron near ion $\kappa$, bare ($-Z_\kappa e^2/|\br|$) or a pseudopotential (eV). Giustino: $V^{\rm en}$, $V_\kappa$ (Eqs. 25–26). EPP: $V_{\rm cr}$, $v_{\rm a}(\br)$ (Eq. 92).
$Z_\kappa$
charge of ion $\kappa$ in units of $e$: the atomic number for a bare nucleus, the number of valence electrons for a pseudopotential; in jellium, the number of electrons per cell. Giustino: $Z_\kappa$ (Eq. 26).
$\psi_{n\bk}$, $u_{n\bk}$
Bloch state normalized on the Born–von Kármán supercell, $\psi_{n\bk}=\ee^{\ii\bk\cdot\br}u_{n\bk}/\sqrt{N_\ell}$, ; its periodic part, normalized on one cell (Å$^{-3/2}$). Giustino: same (Eq. 29). EPP: $\phi^0_{n\bk}=\Omega^{-1/2}e^{i\bk\br}u^0_{n\bk}$ (Eq. 96), with $u^0_{n\bk}=\Omega_{\rm uc}^{1/2}u_{n\bk}$.
$\bk$, $N_\bk$
electron wavevector on the Born–von Kármán grid (Å⁻¹), $\hbar\bk$ the crystal momentum; number of electron wavevectors, $N_\bk=N_\ell$.
$f_{n\bk}$, $\mu$, $E_{\mathrm F}$
Fermi–Dirac occupation of state $n\bk$ for one spin, ; chemical potential (eV); Fermi energy, the energy of the highest occupied state of a metal at $T=0$ (eV). Giustino: $f_{n\bk}$ (Eq. 51), $\varepsilon_F$.
$n(\br)$
electron number density, $2\sum_{n\bk}f_{n\bk}|\psi_{n\bk}(\br)|^2$ (Å⁻³), ; always written with its argument.
$c_{n\bk}$, $c^\dagger_{n\bk}$
annihilation and creation operators of an electron in $\psi_{n\bk}$, with $\{c_a,c^\dagger_b\}=\delta_{ab}$. Giustino: $\hat c_{n\bk}$, $\hat c^\dagger_{n\bk}$. EPP: $c_{n\bk}$, $c^+_{n\bk}$.
$\hat H^{\mathrm e}$
Hamiltonian of the independent electrons, $\sum_{n\bk}E_{n\bk}c^\dagger_{n\bk}c_{n\bk}$, . Giustino: $\hat H_{\rm e}$ (Eq. 30).
$\partial V(\br)/\partial u_{\ell\kappa\alpha}$
change of a potential energy at $\br$ per unit displacement of atom $\ell\kappa$ along $\alpha$, the others at rest, evaluated at equilibrium (eV/Å); for the rigid-ion part, $-\partial_\alpha V_\kappa(\br-\btau_{\ell\kappa})$, . $\Delta V(\br)$, $\Delta\hat V(\br)$
change of the potential energy caused by a frozen wave of displacements, (eV); the same change as an operator on the phonons, $N_\ell^{-1/2}\sum_{\bq\nu}\Delta_{\bq\nu}V\,\hat\phi_{\bq\nu}$, . EPP: $\Delta V_{\rm cr}$ (Eqs. 99–108).
$\partial_{\kappa\alpha,\bq}V$, $\partial_qV$, $\Delta_{\bq\nu}V$
potential change per unit amplitude when all atoms $\kappa$ move along $\alpha$ in a wave $\bq$, with the phase of each displaced atom's cell (eV/Å), ; $\partial_qV$ is its form for a chain with one atom per cell; potential change per unit phonon field of mode $\bq\nu$ (eV). Both are Bloch-type functions of wavevector $\bq$, . Giustino: $e^{i\bq\cdot\br}\partial_{\kappa\alpha,\bq}v^{\rm KS}$ and $\Delta_{\bq\nu}V^{\rm KS}=e^{i\bq\cdot\br}\Delta_{\bq\nu}v^{\rm KS}$ (Eqs. 33–35).
$I(\br)$, $I_\ell$
the integrand of a matrix element between Bloch states, and its integral over cell $\ell$, the cell integral; for a potential change of wavevector $\bq$ between $n\bk$ and $m\bk'$, $I_\ell=\ee^{\ii(\bk+\bq-\bk')\cdot\bR_\ell}\,I_0$ (, steps before ).
$\hat H^{\mathrm{e\text{-}ph}}$
first-order electron–phonon Hamiltonian, $N_\ell^{-1/2}\sum g_{mn\nu}(\bk,\bq)\,c^\dagger_{m\bk+\bq}c_{n\bk}\,\hat\phi_{\bq\nu}$, . Giustino: $\hat H_{\rm ep}$ (Eq. 37). EPP: $\Delta V^0_{\rm cr}$ (Eq. 108), with $N^{-1/2}$ inside $g^0$.
$g_{mn\nu}(\bk,\bq)$
electron–phonon matrix element for $n\bk\to m\,\bk+\bq$ with absorption of $\bq\nu$ or emission of $-\bq\nu$, $\langle\psi_{m\bk+\bq}|\Delta_{\bq\nu}V^{\mathrm{KS}}|\psi_{n\bk}\rangle$, : a supercell integral of normalized states, equal to a cell integral, independent of $N_\ell$ (eV). Giustino: same, as $\langle u_{m\bk+\bq}|\Delta_{\bq\nu}v^{\rm KS}|u_{n\bk}\rangle_{\rm uc}$ (Eqs. 11, 38). EPP: $g^0_{nm\alpha}(\bk,\bq)=N^{-1/2}g_{nm\nu}(\bk-\bq,\bq)$ for the rigid-ion potential (Eq. 107).
$\hat H$, $\hat H^{\ph}$, $\hat H^{\mathrm{DW}}$
the whole electron–phonon Hamiltonian, ; its phonon part (called $\hat H$ in Section A.1); its second-order (Debye–Waller) coupling, with two phonon fields at one vertex. Giustino: $\hat H$ (Eq. 1), $\hat H_{\rm p}$ (Eq. 22), $\hat H^{(2)}_{\rm ep}$ (Eq. 39). EPP: $H$ (Eq. 109).
$V_\kappa(\bq)$
Fourier transform of the ion's potential over all space, $\int\dd^3r\,\ee^{-\ii\bq\cdot\br}V_\kappa(\br)$ (eV Å³); $-4\pi Z_\kappa e^2/q^2$ for a bare ion. Giustino: $V_\kappa(\bq)$ per cell, our $V_\kappa(\bq)/\OV$ (Eq. 43). EPP: $V_{\rm a}(\bq)=e^{-i\bq\cdot\mathbf r^0_{\rm a}}v_{\rm a}(\bq)$ (Eq. 101).
$\bG_n$
reciprocal lattice vector that labels a free-electron band, $u_{n\bk}=\ee^{\ii\bG_n\cdot\br}/\sqrt{\OV}$.
$q_{\mathrm D}$
Debye wavevector of the Debye model $\omega=v_{\mathrm s}q$, with one mode per cell: $\frac{4\pi}3q_{\mathrm D}^3=(2\pi)^3/\OV$ (Å⁻¹).
$V_0$

$R_{\mathrm{WS}}$
