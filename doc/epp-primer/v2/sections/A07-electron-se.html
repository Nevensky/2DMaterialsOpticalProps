The electron self-energy: Fan–Migdal, Debye–Waller and kinks

An electron moving through a crystal pushes the ions near it, and the displaced ions pull back on it. At the same time it feels a crystal potential blurred by the vibrations of the ions. This section turns these two pictures into the Fan–Migdal and Debye–Waller self-energies, derives the Fan–Migdal formula at finite temperature, and shows what the self-energy does to a band: it shifts the band, flattens it near the Fermi level, gives its states a finite lifetime and moves part of their weight into satellites.

The picture: an electron that drags the lattice

An electron added to a crystal exerts forces on the ions near it, and the ions move slightly. The displaced ions change the potential the electron feels, and the change follows the electron as it moves. The electron therefore travels with a cloud of lattice distortion: it is lower in energy and heavier than an electron in a rigid lattice. In a polar crystal this dressed electron is Fröhlich's polaron (Section A.4). In the language of phonons, the electron keeps emitting and reabsorbing virtual phonons, phonons that exist only in intermediate states that do not conserve energy. When energy and crystal momentum permit, the emission is real: the electron leaves a phonon behind and drops into another state, so its original state lives only for a finite time.

A second effect needs no push from the electron. The ions are never at rest: zero-point and thermal motion spread each of them over a cloud a few hundredths of an ångström across (Section A.1). The electron sees the average potential of these fuzzy ions, which is smoother than the potential of ions at rest. This static correction is the Debye–Waller term.

Both effects are collected in the electron's self-energy, the complex, energy-dependent correction to a band energy that Section A.5 introduced. For a band state $n\bk$ with band energy $E_{n\bk}$, computed with the nuclei fixed, the real part of the self-energy shifts the energy, the imaginary part sets the inverse lifetime, and its dependence on energy reshapes the band near the Fermi level. In real materials the shifts are tens to hundreds of meV: zero-point motion alone reduces the band gap of silicon by about 60 meV and the direct gap of diamond by more than 0.5 eV. The broadenings are meV to tens of meV, and near the Fermi level of a metal the band velocity drops by factors between about 1.1 (graphene) and 2.5 (lead).2

  1. the energy shift of one electron in second-order perturbation theory;
  2. the general self-energy on top of clamped nuclei, split into Fan–Migdal and Debye–Waller parts;
  3. the Fan–Migdal formula at finite temperature, read as emission and absorption;
  4. the spectral function, the quasiparticle weight, the mass enhancement and the kink seen in photoemission;
  5. the Eliashberg function and the coupling strength;
  6. beyond the Dyson equation: the cumulant and polaron satellites.

The simplest case: one electron in an empty band

Take one electron in a band of an insulator that is otherwise empty, at zero temperature, and suppose that all other bands lie far away in energy. The lattice is in its ground state, the phonon vacuum $|0\rangle$, in which no mode is excited. The electron–phonon Hamiltonian of Section A.2 () is

$$\begin{gathered}\hat H^{\mathrm{e\text{-}ph}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_\ell}}\sum_{\bk\bq mn\nu}g_{mn\nu}(\bk,\bq)\\ \times c^\dagger_{m\bk+\bq}c_{n\bk}\,\hat\phi_{\bq\nu} .\end{gathered}$$

It moves an electron from the band state $n\bk$ to $m\bk+\bq$ (the operators $c_{n\bk}$, $c^\dagger_{m\bk+\bq}$ remove and create electrons) with the matrix element $g_{mn\nu}(\bk,\bq)$, an energy of tens to hundreds of meV. At the same time the phonon field $\hat\phi_{\bq\nu}=\hat a_{\bq\nu}+\hat a^\dagger_{-\bq\nu}$ absorbs a phonon $\bq\nu$ or creates a phonon $-\bq\nu$. Either way the electron gains crystal momentum $\hbar\bq$. $N_\ell$ is the number of cells of the Born–von Kármán supercell. Treat $\hat H^{\mathrm{e\text{-}ph}}$ as a perturbation, step by step:

  1. The unperturbed state is $|n\bk;0\rangle$: the electron in $n\bk$ and no phonons. Its energy is $E_{n\bk}$, measured from the zero-point energy of the lattice.

    The zero-point energy $\sum_{\bq\nu}\hbar\omega_{\bq\nu}/2$ of Section A.1 is the same in every state considered here, so it cancels in every energy difference.

  2. The first-order shift vanishes, $\langle n\bk;0|\hat H^{\mathrm{e\text{-}ph}}|n\bk;0\rangle=0$.

    Every term of $\hat H^{\mathrm{e\text{-}ph}}$ contains one phonon field, which changes the number of phonons by one, and states with different numbers of phonons are orthogonal.

  3. Acting on $|n\bk;0\rangle$, the coupling reaches the states $|m\bk+\bq;1_{-\bq\nu}\rangle$: the electron in $m\bk+\bq$ and one phonon $-\bq\nu$, with energy $E_{m\bk+\bq}+\hbar\omega_{\bq\nu}$. The matrix element is

    $$\langle m\bk+\bq;1_{-\bq\nu}|\hat H^{\mathrm{e\text{-}ph}}|n\bk;0\rangle=\frac{g_{mn\nu}(\bk,\bq)}{\sqrt{N_\ell}} .$$
    On the vacuum only the creation part $\hat a^\dagger_{-\bq\nu}$ of $\hat\phi_{\bq\nu}$ survives, and $c^\dagger_{m\bk+\bq}c_{n\bk}$ moves the electron. The phonon $-\bq\nu$ has the frequency $\omega_{-\bq\nu}=\omega_{\bq\nu}$ (Section A.1). Crystal momentum is conserved: the electron gains $\hbar\bq$ and the phonon carries away $-\hbar\bq$.

  4. Second-order perturbation theory sums, over all intermediate states, the squared matrix element divided by the energy of the initial state minus that of the intermediate state:

    $$\Delta E_{n\bk}=\frac{1}{N_\ell}\sum_{\bq m\nu}\frac{|g_{mn\nu}(\bk,\bq)|^2}{E_{n\bk}-E_{m\bk+\bq}-\hbar\omega_{\bq\nu}} . \tag{A7.1}$$
    This is the textbook second-order formula for a perturbation without a first-order term, applied to the intermediate states of step 3.
$\Delta E_{n\bk}$
second-order shift of the band energy of state $n\bk$ (meV)
$\sum_{\bq m\nu}$
sum over the $N_\ell$ wavevectors of the Born–von Kármán grid, the bands $m$ of the intermediate state and the phonon branches $\nu$; the three modes with $\omega=0$ at $\bq=0$ are left out
$\omega_{\bq\nu}$
phonon frequency; $\hbar\omega_{\bq\nu}$ is 10 to 200 meV

Each term is a virtual process: the electron emits a phonon $-\bq\nu$, visits $m\bk+\bq$, and reabsorbs the phonon. The denominator measures how far the intermediate state is from energy conservation. At the bottom of the band every intermediate state of the same band lies higher, so every denominator is negative and the coupling lowers the band bottom. The factor $1/N_\ell$ together with the $N_\ell$ terms of the sum over $\bq$ makes an average over the Brillouin zone, $\frac{1}{N_\ell}\sum_\bq\to\frac{\OV}{(2\pi)^3}\int_{\mathrm{BZ}}\dd^3q$ with $\OV$ the cell volume, so the shift stays finite as the crystal grows. With $|g|$ of order 0.1 eV and denominators of a few tenths of an eV, $\Delta E_{n\bk}$ is a few tens of meV.3

From an energy shift to a self-energy

Formula fails when a denominator vanishes. That happens when the electron has enough energy to emit a real phonon, that is when a state $m\bk+\bq$ with $E_{m\bk+\bq}+\hbar\omega_{\bq\nu}=E_{n\bk}$ exists. The electron then does not merely shift: it decays. The self-energy handles both cases. Replace the band energy in the denominator by a variable energy $\hbar\omega$, the probe energy at which an experiment adds an electron to the state or removes one from it, and give it a small positive imaginary part:

$$\Sigma^{\mathrm{FM}}_{n\bk}(\omega)=\frac{1}{N_\ell}\sum_{\bq m\nu}\frac{|g_{mn\nu}(\bk,\bq)|^2}{\hbar(\omega+\ii\eta)-E_{m\bk+\bq}-\hbar\omega_{\bq\nu}} . \tag{A7.2}$$
$\Sigma^{\mathrm{FM}}_{n\bk}(\omega)$
Fan–Migdal self-energy of state $n\bk$: an energy (eV), written as a function of the angular frequency $\omega$
$\eta$
the positive infinitesimal of retarded functions met in Sections A.3 and A.6, here a frequency; $\eta\to0^+$ at the end of a calculation

This is the Fan–Migdal self-energy of one electron at zero temperature; the general formula derived below, , reduces to it. Its real part at the band energy, $\mathrm{Re}\,\Sigma^{\mathrm{FM}}_{n\bk}(E_{n\bk}/\hbar)$, is the shift wherever no denominator vanishes. Its imaginary part follows from $\mathrm{Im}\,[1/(x+\ii0^+)]=-\pi\delta(x)$ for real $x$:

$$\begin{gathered}\mathrm{Im}\,\Sigma^{\mathrm{FM}}_{n\bk}(\omega)=-\frac{\pi}{N_\ell}\sum_{\bq m\nu}|g_{mn\nu}(\bk,\bq)|^2\\ \times\delta\big(\hbar\omega-E_{m\bk+\bq}-\hbar\omega_{\bq\nu}\big) .\end{gathered}$$

At $\hbar\omega=E_{n\bk}$ this is $-\hbar/2$ times Fermi's golden-rule rate, $(2\pi/\hbar)\sum|g/\sqrt{N_\ell}|^2\delta(\dots)$, for emitting a real phonon. So $2|\mathrm{Im}\,\Sigma|/\hbar$ is the rate at which the electron leaves its state: the inverse of the lifetime $\tau_{n\bk}$ of Section A.8. The sign of $\ii\eta$ is not arbitrary. With $+\ii\eta$, $\Sigma(\omega)$ is the Fourier transform of a response that vanishes before its cause, a causal or retarded function, and its poles lie just below the real axis of $\omega$. Every self-energy in this section is retarded, and its imaginary part is never positive.

A Fermi sea and a warm lattice

Two things change when the band already holds electrons, filled up to the chemical potential $\mu$, and the crystal, electrons and lattice alike, has a temperature $T$.

  • The Pauli principle. An electron cannot move into an occupied state $m\bk+\bq$. The electron already in that state can, however, make the reverse jump into the probed state when that state is empty, filling a hole there. Both processes involve the same pair of states, with the probabilities $1-f_{m\bk+\bq}$ and $f_{m\bk+\bq}$, where $f_{m\bk+\bq}$ is the Fermi–Dirac occupation of $m\bk+\bq$. As a function of an energy $E$ it reads $f(E)=1/\big(\ee^{(E-\mu)/\kB T}+1\big)$, and $f_{m\bk+\bq}=f(E_{m\bk+\bq})$.
  • Thermal phonons. A mode that already holds $n_{\bq\nu}$ phonons receives a new one at a rate enhanced by the factor $n_{\bq\nu}+1$ (stimulated emission), and it can now also give a phonon to the electron, at a rate proportional to $n_{\bq\nu}$.

Counting the four processes that connect the probed state with a state $m\bk+\bq$ gives two weights. When $m\bk+\bq$ lies one phonon energy below, the electron can drop into it by emission, with the weight $(1-f)(n+1)$, and the electron in $m\bk+\bq$ can come up by absorption, with the weight $f\,n$; the sum is $n_{\bq\nu}+1-f_{m\bk+\bq}$. When $m\bk+\bq$ lies one phonon energy above, absorption contributes $(1-f)\,n$ and the reverse emission $f\,(n+1)$; the sum is $n_{\bq\nu}+f_{m\bk+\bq}$. Clamped nuclei as the reference

Formula is the lowest-order term of a systematic theory, the Hedin–Baym equations of Section A.5 (). The review builds the electron self-energy from them in a way that matches practice. Density-functional band structures, and the GW band structures of Section A.5 (in which the self-energy is the product of the propagator $G$ and the screened interaction $W$), are computed with the nuclei clamped at their equilibrium positions. That problem is solved first, and the self-energy then collects only what the motion of the nuclei adds.

The clamped-nuclei problem is Hedin's: electrons in the static potential of the nuclei, interacting through the Coulomb interaction screened by the electrons. Its propagator $G^{\mathrm{cn}}$ has poles at the clamped-nuclei band energies $E_{n\bk}$. Letting the nuclei move changes three things. The electron can exchange phonons with the lattice; the average potential of the vibrating nuclei differs from the potential of nuclei at rest; and the propagator, density and screening of the electrons change slightly. draws the three corresponding terms.

Derivation: why the regrouping is exact

  1. Write the equation of motion of the coupled propagator (Section A.5) in operator form, with $m_{\mathrm e}$ the electron mass, $\nabla^2$ the Laplacian in the electron position and $\partial_t$ the time derivative:

    $$\begin{gathered}G^{-1}=\ii\hbar\partial_t+\frac{\hbar^2}{2m_{\mathrm e}}\nabla^2-\langle V^{\mathrm{en}}+V^{\mathrm H}\rangle\\ -\ii\hbar\,G\Lambda\big(W^{\mathrm{e}}+W^{\ph}\big) .\end{gathered}$$
    An electron moves with its kinetic energy in the average electrostatic field of all charges, and the rest of the interaction is Hedin's self-energy. With moving nuclei the screened interaction contains $W^{\ph}$.

    Clamped nuclei do not move, so only the electrons screen.

The three terms have distinct meanings.

  • Fan–Migdal is Hedin's GW self-energy with the phonon part of the screened interaction. In GW, an added electron polarizes the other electrons, which act back on it; here the polarizable medium is the lattice: the electron pushes the ions, and their displacement pulls back on it. With the vertex set to one it is called the Fan self-energy in semiconductor physics (Fan, 1951) and the Migdal self-energy in the theory of metals (Migdal, 1958). It depends on frequency: it is dynamic.
  • Debye–Waller is the change of the average electrostatic potential when each nucleus is smeared over its zero-point and thermal motion. It is static, a correction to the crystal potential, and takes its name from the Debye–Waller factor of x-ray diffraction, which describes the same smearing.
  • dGW is the change of the electronic GW self-energy because $G$, the electron density and $W^{\mathrm{e}}$ of the coupled system differ from their clamped-nuclei values. The review puts its size at a fraction of the electronic GW correction itself; no calculation of it existed when the review was written. From here on it is dropped, and $\Sigma^{\mathrm{ep}}=\Sigma^{\mathrm{FM}}+\Sigma^{\mathrm{DW}}$.

Both ends of the phonon line carry the screened vertex. In $\Sigma^{\mathrm{FM}}=\ii\hbar\,G\Lambda W^{\ph}$ both ends attach to the propagating electron, so the Fan–Migdal self-energy has two screened vertices. The phonon self-energy of Section A.6 has one bare and one screened vertex. The difference is a matter of counting. In the phonon self-energy the two vertices sit on an electron bubble, and the chain of further bubbles that screens a vertex can be attached to one end only; attaching it to both ends would count it twice. In $W^{\ph}$ no bubble lies between the two ends: each end meets an electron line and needs its own screening. The screened DFPT matrix element of Section A.3 is therefore the right object at both vertices of the Fan–Migdal self-energy.5

Derivation: from displacements to modes

  1. With the displacements $\hat u_{\ell\kappa\alpha}$ as operators, the phonon part of the screened interaction is the screened potential of one displaced atom, the propagator of the displacements, and the screened potential of a second one:

    $$\begin{gathered}W^{\ph}(\br,\br';t)=\sum_{\ell\kappa\alpha}\sum_{\ell'\kappa'\beta}\mathcal V_{\ell\kappa\alpha}(\br)\\ \times\Big(-\frac{\ii}{\hbar}\Big)\big\langle\mathcal T\,\hat u_{\ell\kappa\alpha}(t)\,\hat u_{\ell'\kappa'\beta}(0)\big\rangle\\ \times\mathcal V_{\ell'\kappa'\beta}(\br'),\\ \mathcal V_{\ell\kappa\alpha}=\big(\varepsilon^{\mathrm{e}}\big)^{-1}\frac{\partial V^{\mathrm{en}}}{\partial u_{\ell\kappa\alpha}} .\end{gathered}$$
    The potential of a fluctuation of the nuclear charge is a displacement times the derivative of the nuclear potential with respect to it (Section A.5). The fluctuation propagates from time 0 to time $t$, and its screened field acts on the electron at $\br$; $\mathcal T$ orders the operators in time.

  2. Insert the displacement operator for both displacements, with its zero-point amplitudes, eigenvectors $e_{\kappa\alpha,\nu}(\bq)$ and cell phases $\ee^{\ii\bq\cdot\bR_\ell}$, where $\bR_\ell$ is the lattice vector of cell $\ell$. Only pairs of modes with opposite wavevectors survive, and with $\hat\phi_{-\bq\nu'}=\hat\phi^\dagger_{\bq\nu'}$ and $e_{\kappa'\beta,\nu'}(-\bq)=e^*_{\kappa'\beta,\nu'}(\bq)$,

    $$\begin{gathered}-\frac{\ii}{\hbar}\big\langle\mathcal T\,\hat u_{\ell\kappa\alpha}(t)\,\hat u_{\ell'\kappa'\beta}(0)\big\rangle=\frac{1}{N_\ell}\sum_{\bq\nu\nu'}\\ \Big(\frac{\hbar}{2M_\kappa\omega_{\bq\nu}}\Big)^{1/2}e_{\kappa\alpha,\nu}(\bq)\,\ee^{\ii\bq\cdot\bR_\ell}\,F_{\nu\nu'}(\bq,t)\\ \times\Big(\frac{\hbar}{2M_{\kappa'}\omega_{\bq\nu'}}\Big)^{1/2}e^*_{\kappa'\beta,\nu'}(\bq)\,\ee^{-\ii\bq\cdot\bR_{\ell'}} .\end{gathered}$$
    The crystal is invariant under lattice translations, so $\langle\mathcal T\hat\phi_{\bq\nu}(t)\hat\phi_{\bq'\nu'}(0)\rangle$ vanishes unless $\bq'=-\bq$: crystal momentum is conserved. What remains is the definition of $F_{\nu\nu'}(\bq,t)$ (Section A.5); the eigenvector relation $e(-\bq)=e^*(\bq)$ and $\omega_{-\bq\nu'}=\omega_{\bq\nu'}$ are those Section A.1 derives from time-reversal symmetry.

    $$\begin{gathered}\sum_{\ell\kappa\alpha}\ee^{\ii\bq\cdot\bR_\ell}\Big(\frac{\hbar}{2M_\kappa\omega_{\bq\nu}}\Big)^{1/2}e_{\kappa\alpha,\nu}(\bq)\\ \times\frac{\partial V^{\mathrm{en}}}{\partial u_{\ell\kappa\alpha}}=\Delta_{\bq\nu}V^{\mathrm{en}} ,\end{gathered}$$

    and the primed sum, with $e^*$ and $\ee^{-\ii\bq\cdot\bR_{\ell'}}$, is $(\Delta_{\bq\nu'}V^{\mathrm{en}})^*=\Delta_{-\bq\nu'}V^{\mathrm{en}}$. Fourier transform in time; this gives .

The working formula at finite temperature

Five approximations turn $\Sigma^{\mathrm{FM}}=\ii\hbar\,G\Lambda W^{\ph}$ into a formula that codes evaluate from density-functional quantities:

  1. the vertex is set to one, $\Lambda=1$, as in GW;
  2. the propagator $G$ is replaced by the clamped-nuclei propagator, taken as the propagator $G^0$ of independent electrons with Kohn–Sham (or GW) band energies, one pole per state;
  3. the phonon propagator is replaced by the free, adiabatic one, $F^0_\nu$, with the DFPT frequencies, computed for electrons that follow the nuclei instantly; it is diagonal in the branch index;
  4. the screening of the vertex is the random-phase plus exchange–correlation screening of DFPT, so that $\mathcal V_{\bq\nu}$ becomes $\Delta_{\bq\nu}V^{\mathrm{KS}}$ ();
  5. this screening is taken at zero frequency: the vertex does not depend on frequency.

Approximation 1 drops the vertex corrections, the part of $\Lambda$ beyond one, as Migdal did, and approximations 2 and 3 use free lines, not dressed by the coupling; together they define the Migdal level of first-principles calculations. Approximations 4 and 5 make the vertex the static matrix element $g_{mn\nu}(\bk,\bq)$ of Section A.2, computed with DFPT as in Section A.3.6 The temperature enters through a technique that the following box recalls.

Matsubara functions and frequency sums skip if familiar

A thermal average at temperature $T$ carries the weight $\ee^{-\hat H/\kB T}$, with $\hat H$ the Hamiltonian and $\kB$ Boltzmann's constant. This weight has the form of a time evolution $\ee^{-\ii\hat H\,t/\hbar}$ over the imaginary time $t=-\ii\hbar/\kB T$. Matsubara's method uses this. Propagators are defined on an interval of imaginary time of length $\hbar/\kB T$, on which they are periodic for bosons and antiperiodic for fermions. Their Fourier series therefore contain only discrete frequencies, the fermionic and bosonic Matsubara frequencies

$$\begin{gathered}\omega^{\mathrm F}_p=(2p+1)\frac{\pi\kB T}{\hbar},\qquad \omega^{\mathrm B}_p=2p\,\frac{\pi\kB T}{\hbar},\\ p\ \text{an integer} .\end{gathered}$$

Three rules are all this section needs.

  1. A free propagator at a Matsubara frequency is the same rational function as the retarded one, with $\omega+\ii\eta$ replaced by $\ii\omega^{\mathrm F}_p$ or $\ii\omega^{\mathrm B}_p$. For an electron in state $m\bk$ and for phonon $\bq\nu$,
    $$\begin{gathered}G^0_{m\bk}(\ii\omega^{\mathrm F}_p)=\frac{1}{\ii\hbar\omega^{\mathrm F}_p-(E_{m\bk}-\mu)},\\ F^0_\nu(\bq,\ii\omega^{\mathrm B}_p)=\frac{1}{\hbar}\,\frac{2\omega_{\bq\nu}}{(\ii\omega^{\mathrm B}_p)^2-\omega^2_{\bq\nu}} .\end{gathered}$$
    Electron energies are measured from the chemical potential $\mu$.
  2. A frequency integral over a product of propagators becomes a sum, $\int\frac{\dd\omega'}{2\pi}\to\frac{\ii\kB T}{\hbar}\sum_{p'}$, so that the convolution $\ii\hbar\int\frac{\dd\omega'}{2\pi}GW$ of a GW self-energy becomes $-\kB T\sum_{p'}GW$. The sum of two fermionic frequencies is bosonic, and a fermionic minus a bosonic one is fermionic.
  3. Sums over bosonic frequencies are done by residues. The function $b(z)=1/(\ee^{z/\kB T}-1)$ of a complex energy $z$ has simple poles at $z=\ii\hbar\omega^{\mathrm B}_{p'}$, each with residue $\kB T$. For a function $h(z)$ that decays at least as $1/z^2$, the integral of $h(z)\,b(z)$ around a very large circle vanishes, so the sum of all its residues is zero:
    $$\begin{gathered}\kB T\sum_{p'}h\big(\ii\hbar\omega^{\mathrm B}_{p'}\big)\\ =-\sum_{\text{poles }z_j\text{ of }h}\mathrm{Res}_{z=z_j}\big[h(z)\,b(z)\big] .\end{gathered}$$

The result is known at the discrete points $\ii\omega^{\mathrm F}_p$. It has a unique continuation that is analytic in the upper half of the complex frequency plane and decays at large frequency; evaluated at $\omega+\ii\eta$, this continuation is the retarded function.7

The derivation has four steps.

  1. Take the diagonal matrix element in the Bloch state $\psi_{n\bk}$, $\Sigma^{\mathrm{FM}}_{n\bk}=\langle\psi_{n\bk}|\Sigma^{\mathrm{FM}}|\psi_{n\bk}\rangle$, with $G^0$ written as a sum over Kohn–Sham states. Reading the formula as processes

The first term of has its pole where $\hbar\omega=E_{m\bk+\bq}+\hbar\omega_{\bq\nu}$: an electron at energy $\hbar\omega$ can emit a phonon and land in $m\bk+\bq$. The second has its pole where $\hbar\omega=E_{m\bk+\bq}-\hbar\omega_{\bq\nu}$: the electron can absorb a phonon. Each weight is the process out of the probed state plus its reverse, as counted above.

  • Zero temperature, an electron above the Fermi level. With $n=0$ only the emission term can be resonant, into empty states, $f=0$, and only if the electron lies more than one phonon energy above the Fermi level.
  • Zero temperature, a hole below the Fermi level. The resonant term is the second one, with weight $f=1$. An electron from an occupied state one phonon energy higher falls into the hole and emits a phonon: the hole emits a phonon by moving to a state closer to the Fermi level.
  • High temperature, $\kB T\gg\hbar\omega_{\bq\nu}$. Both weights approach $n_{\bq\nu}\simeq\kB T/\hbar\omega_{\bq\nu}$, so the self-energy grows linearly with temperature. Section A.8 uses this for the temperature dependence of band gaps.

In the real part each intermediate state pushes the probed energy away from its own energy, $E_{m\bk+\bq}+\hbar\omega_{\bq\nu}$ in the first term and $E_{m\bk+\bq}-\hbar\omega_{\bq\nu}$ in the second, in proportion to its weight: second-order level repulsion, which Section A.8 uses to explain why band gaps shrink. The imaginary part collects the real processes:

$$\begin{gathered}\mathrm{Im}\,\Sigma^{\mathrm{FM}}_{n\bk}(\omega)=-\frac{\pi}{N_\ell}\sum_{\bq m\nu}|g_{mn\nu}(\bk,\bq)|^2\\ \times\Big[\big(n_{\bq\nu}+1-f_{m\bk+\bq}\big)\,\delta\big(\hbar\omega-E_{m\bk+\bq}-\hbar\omega_{\bq\nu}\big)\\ +\big(n_{\bq\nu}+f_{m\bk+\bq}\big)\,\delta\big(\hbar\omega-E_{m\bk+\bq}+\hbar\omega_{\bq\nu}\big)\Big] .\end{gathered} \tag{A7.9}$$

This is negative or zero for every state and every frequency, because both weights are non-negative; it is the sign convention of the whole primer for retarded self-energies. At the band energy, $2|\mathrm{Im}\,\Sigma^{\mathrm{FM}}_{n\bk}(E_{n\bk}/\hbar)|/\hbar$ is the golden-rule rate of scattering by emission and absorption of phonons, the inverse lifetime $1/\tau_{n\bk}$ of Section A.8 (). For example, $|\mathrm{Im}\,\Sigma|=50$ meV gives $\tau=\hbar/(2\times50\ \text{meV})=6.6$ fs. The Debye–Waller self-energy is real and contributes nothing to the width.9

What the working formula leaves out

Migdal level. With $\Lambda=1$ the diagrams in which phonon lines cross, the vertex corrections, are omitted. Migdal's theorem makes them small in metals whose Fermi energy is far above the phonon energies: smaller by about a phonon energy over the Fermi energy, of the order of $(m_{\mathrm e}/M_\kappa)^{1/2}$ with $M_\kappa$ an atomic mass. They are not small in lightly doped semiconductors, where the Fermi energy is comparable with a phonon energy, or at strong coupling; the cumulant at the end of this section goes beyond this level. With free lines, not dressed by the coupling (approximations 2 and 3), the diagrams in which one phonon line lies inside another are omitted as well; self-consistent (Migdal–Eliashberg) calculations keep them.

Adiabatic phonons. By approximation 3, the phonon shifts and widths of Section A.6 do not feed back into the electron.

Static vertex. By approximations 4 and 5, the frequency dependence of the screening at the vertex is lost; it matters in doped polar semiconductors, where plasmons, the collective oscillations of the doped electrons, have phonon energies.

Three meanings of “retarded”. Here retarded means causal, with poles below the real axis. The review also calls the frequency dependence of the screening retardation; Section A.5 calls it dynamical screening. In the EPP manuscript of Part B (Section X.1) retardation means the finite speed of light.

Debye–Waller: the crystal potential, blurred

Start again with the simplest case: one ion of the crystal, with potential energy $V_\kappa$ for an electron near it, vibrating about its equilibrium site $\btau_{\ell\kappa}$ with displacement $\bu_{\ell\kappa}$. Averaged over the vibration, the potential at the point $\br$ is found in three steps:

  1. Expand to second order in the displacement:

    $$\begin{gathered}V_\kappa(\br-\btau_{\ell\kappa}-\bu_{\ell\kappa})\simeq V_\kappa-\sum_\alpha u_{\ell\kappa\alpha}\,\partial_\alpha V_\kappa\\ +\frac12\sum_{\alpha\beta}u_{\ell\kappa\alpha}u_{\ell\kappa\beta}\,\partial_\alpha\partial_\beta V_\kappa ,\end{gathered}$$
    with $V_\kappa$ and its derivatives taken at $\br-\btau_{\ell\kappa}$. The displacement, a few hundredths of an ångström, is small compared with the distance over which the potential (in practice a smooth pseudopotential) changes.

  2. Average over the vibration: $\langle u_{\ell\kappa\alpha}\rangle=0$ and $\langle u_{\ell\kappa\alpha}u_{\ell\kappa\beta}\rangle=u_{\mathrm{rms}}^2\,\delta_{\alpha\beta}$ for an atom on a site of cubic symmetry.

    A harmonic vibration is centred on the equilibrium site, and cubic symmetry makes the mean-square displacement the same, $u_{\mathrm{rms}}^2$, along every direction, with no correlation between directions. Section A.1 computes $u_{\mathrm{rms}}$ from the modes.

  3. The first-order term drops out, and the second-order term becomes a Laplacian:

    $$\begin{gathered}\big\langle V_\kappa(\br-\btau_{\ell\kappa}-\bu_{\ell\kappa})\big\rangle\\ \simeq V_\kappa(\br-\btau_{\ell\kappa})+\frac{u_{\mathrm{rms}}^2}{2}\,\nabla^2V_\kappa(\br-\btau_{\ell\kappa}) .\end{gathered} \tag{A7.10}$$
    $\sum_{\alpha\beta}\delta_{\alpha\beta}\,\partial_\alpha\partial_\beta=\nabla^2$.
$u_{\mathrm{rms}}$
root-mean-square displacement of the ion along one direction (Å): about 0.08 Å in silicon at room temperature, and roughly 0.05 Å from zero-point motion alone

The averaged potential is the bare one smoothed over the size of the vibration. In terms of the Fourier components of the crystal potential, the averaging multiplies each component of wavevector $\bG$ by $\ee^{-|\bG|^2u_{\mathrm{rms}}^2/2}$, and is the first-order term of this factor. The reason is that a harmonic displacement has a Gaussian distribution, for which $\langle\ee^{-\ii\bG\cdot\bu}\rangle=\ee^{-\langle(\bG\cdot\bu)^2\rangle/2}$. The same factor, squared, weakens the Bragg peaks of x-ray and neutron diffraction: it is the Debye–Waller factor. In silicon at room temperature the shortest reciprocal-lattice vectors, those of the (111) planes, have $|\bG|\approx2.0$ Å⁻¹, so the corresponding Fourier components of the potential are weaker by about 1.3%. Band energies depend on these components, so they move by tens of meV.

The general formula: all atoms, with the electrons' response

The same averaging applies to the full Kohn–Sham potential, in which all atoms move and the electrons rearrange (Section A.3). Step by step:

  1. Expand $V^{\mathrm{KS}}$ to second order in all displacements. The first-order term gives the coupling $g$ of Section A.2. The second-order term is

    $$\frac12\sum_{\ell\kappa\alpha}\sum_{\ell'\kappa'\beta}\frac{\partial^2V^{\mathrm{KS}}}{\partial u_{\ell\kappa\alpha}\,\partial u_{\ell'\kappa'\beta}}\,u_{\ell\kappa\alpha}u_{\ell'\kappa'\beta} .$$
    The first-order term, taken twice in the Fan–Migdal self-energy, gives an effect of order $u^2$. The second-order term, taken once, is of the same order and must be kept with it.

  2. Insert the displacement operator for each displacement. The second-order term becomes $\frac{1}{2N_\ell}\sum_{\bq\nu,\bq'\nu'}\Delta_{\bq\nu}\Delta_{\bq'\nu'}V^{\mathrm{KS}}\,\hat\phi_{\bq\nu}\hat\phi_{\bq'\nu'}$, where $\Delta_{\bq\nu}\Delta_{\bq'\nu'}V^{\mathrm{KS}}$ is the second derivative of $V^{\mathrm{KS}}$ projected on the two modes, as in Section A.3.

    Each displacement brings $N_\ell^{-1/2}$ times a zero-point amplitude, an eigenvector component, a phase and a phonon field. The sums over atoms build the two projections, as the single sum built $\Delta_{\bq\nu}V$ in Section A.2.

  3. Write this term in second quantization. It is the second-order coupling $\hat H^{\mathrm{DW}}$, the last term of the electron–phonon Hamiltonian of Section A.2 ():

    $$\begin{gathered}\hat H^{\mathrm{DW}}=\frac{1}{N_\ell}\sum_{\bk\bq\bq'mn\nu\nu'}g^{\mathrm{DW}}_{mn\nu\nu'}(\bk,\bq,\bq')\\ \times c^\dagger_{m\bk+\bq+\bq'}c_{n\bk}\,\hat\phi_{\bq\nu}\hat\phi_{\bq'\nu'} .\end{gathered} \tag{A7.11}$$
    As for $\hat H^{\mathrm{e\text{-}ph}}$, a one-body potential becomes a sum of $c^\dagger c$ weighted by its matrix elements. This potential carries the crystal momentum $\hbar(\bq+\bq')$, so it connects $n\bk$ with $m\bk+\bq+\bq'$.

  4. Take $\hat H^{\mathrm{DW}}$ to first order, averaged over the thermal state of the phonons, with $\langle\hat\phi_{\bq\nu}\hat\phi_{\bq'\nu'}\rangle=\delta_{\bq',-\bq}\,\delta_{\nu\nu'}\,(2n_{\bq\nu}+1)$ from Section A.1:

    $$\begin{gathered}\Sigma^{\mathrm{DW}}_{n\bk}=\frac{1}{N_\ell}\sum_{\bq\nu}g^{\mathrm{DW}}_{nn\nu\nu}(\bk,\bq,-\bq)\\ \times\big(2n_{\bq\nu}+1\big) .\end{gathered} \tag{A7.12}$$
    A thermal state is diagonal in the phonon numbers, so only products that remove and restore the same phonon survive. With $\bq'=-\bq$ the electron returns to $n\bk$, which gives the diagonal element.

$V^{\mathrm{DW}}$ is a static, local, real potential with the period of the lattice, since the phases $\ee^{\pm\ii\bq\cdot\br}$ of the two projections cancel; it generalizes to the whole crystal, with correlated displacements of different atoms and the response of the electrons included. Being independent of frequency, it produces no kink; being real, it gives no lifetime. In a homogeneous electron gas, where $|\psi_{n\bk}|^2$ is a constant, it shifts every state by the same amount. In a real crystal it varies smoothly with $\bk$ within a band but differs strongly between bands.11 That is why analyses of kinks in metals drop it, while calculations of band-gap renormalization must keep it (Section A.8).

Fan–Migdal, second order in $g$, and Debye–Waller, first order in $g^{\mathrm{DW}}$, are of the same order in the mean-square displacement, and they partly cancel. The acoustic sum rule of Section A.1 ties them together; Section A.8 uses it to write Debye–Waller with first-order matrix elements ().

The spectral function and the quasiparticle

The self-energy changes the propagator through the Dyson equation . In the basis of clamped-nuclei Kohn–Sham states, for nondegenerate states and with the off-diagonal elements of $\Sigma^{\mathrm{ep}}$ neglected, two steps give the propagator of one state:

  1. The clamped-nuclei propagator of state $n\bk$ has one pole, at the band energy: $G^{\mathrm{cn}}_{n\bk}(\omega)=1/[\hbar(\omega+\ii\eta)-E_{n\bk}]$.

    In the Kohn–Sham or GW description the band states are the eigenstates of the clamped problem. The $+\ii\eta$ makes the propagator retarded.

  2. In this basis reads $G_{n\bk}=G^{\mathrm{cn}}_{n\bk}+G^{\mathrm{cn}}_{n\bk}\Sigma^{\mathrm{ep}}_{n\bk}G_{n\bk}$. Solved for $G_{n\bk}$,

    $$G_{n\bk}(\omega)=\frac{1}{\hbar(\omega+\ii\eta)-E_{n\bk}-\Sigma^{\mathrm{ep}}_{n\bk}(\omega)} . \tag{A7.14}$$
    Multiplying by $1/G^{\mathrm{cn}}_{n\bk}$ and by $1/G_{n\bk}$ gives $1/G_{n\bk}=1/G^{\mathrm{cn}}_{n\bk}-\Sigma^{\mathrm{ep}}_{n\bk}$.
$G_{n\bk}(\omega)$
retarded propagator of state $n\bk$ (1/eV), with $\Sigma^{\mathrm{ep}}_{n\bk}=\Sigma^{\mathrm{FM}}_{n\bk}+\Sigma^{\mathrm{DW}}_{n\bk}$ retarded

Its imaginary part defines the spectral function:

$$\begin{gathered}\mathcal A_{n\bk}(\omega)=-\frac{1}{\pi}\,\mathrm{Im}\,G_{n\bk}(\omega)\\ =\frac{1}{\pi}\,\frac{\big|\mathrm{Im}\,\Sigma^{\mathrm{ep}}_{n\bk}\big|}{\big[\hbar\omega-E_{n\bk}-\mathrm{Re}\,\Sigma^{\mathrm{ep}}_{n\bk}\big]^2+\big[\mathrm{Im}\,\Sigma^{\mathrm{ep}}_{n\bk}\big]^2} .\end{gathered} \tag{A7.15}$$

Without coupling, $\mathcal A_{n\bk}$ is a delta function at $E_{n\bk}$. With coupling it spreads, but its integral stays one, $\int\dd(\hbar\omega)\,\mathcal A_{n\bk}(\omega)=1$: the electron is still there, distributed over energies. It is a density of states resolved in band and crystal momentum, and it is what angle-resolved photoemission spectroscopy (ARPES) measures. Light ejects electrons from the crystal, and their kinetic energy and emission angle give the energy and the crystal momentum, parallel to the surface, of the state they came from. Only occupied states can emit, so the intensity is proportional to $\sum_n\mathcal A_{n\bk}(\omega)\,f(\hbar\omega)$ times matrix elements of the light coupling, with $f$ the Fermi–Dirac function introduced above, here evaluated at the probe energy. Only the retarded self-energy, with $\mathrm{Im}\,\Sigma\le0$, makes $\mathcal A$ positive; the static Debye–Waller term enters the real part only.

Velocity and mass

The quasiparticle band $E^{\mathrm{qp}}_{n\bk}$ has a different slope from the clamped-nuclei band. Define the band velocity $\mathbf v_{n\bk}=\hbar^{-1}\nabla_\bk E_{n\bk}$ and the quasiparticle velocity $\mathbf v^{\mathrm{qp}}_{n\bk}=\hbar^{-1}\nabla_\bk E^{\mathrm{qp}}_{n\bk}$. Then:

  1. Differentiate with respect to $\bk$, neglecting the explicit dependence of $\Sigma$ on $\bk$:

    $$\nabla_\bk E^{\mathrm{qp}}_{n\bk}=\nabla_\bk E_{n\bk}+\frac1\hbar\frac{\partial\,\mathrm{Re}\,\Sigma^{\mathrm{ep}}_{n\bk}}{\partial\omega}\,\nabla_\bk E^{\mathrm{qp}}_{n\bk} .$$
    The self-energy changes on the scale of a phonon energy as $\hbar\omega$ varies, but only on the scale of the Brillouin zone as $\bk$ varies, so near a given energy its explicit $\bk$ dependence is much weaker (Migdal).

  2. Solve for $\nabla_\bk E^{\mathrm{qp}}_{n\bk}$:

    $$\begin{gathered}\mathbf v^{\mathrm{qp}}_{n\bk}=Z^{\mathrm{qp}}_{n\bk}\,\mathbf v_{n\bk}=\frac{\mathbf v_{n\bk}}{1+\lambda_{n\bk}},\\ \lambda_{n\bk}=-\frac1\hbar\,\frac{\partial\,\mathrm{Re}\,\Sigma^{\mathrm{ep}}_{n\bk}}{\partial\omega}\Big|_{\omega=E^{\mathrm{qp}}_{n\bk}/\hbar} .\end{gathered} \tag{A7.19}$$
    The factor that multiplies $\nabla_\bk E^{\mathrm{qp}}$ after collecting terms is $1-\hbar^{-1}\partial_\omega\mathrm{Re}\,\Sigma=1/Z^{\mathrm{qp}}=1+\lambda_{n\bk}$.

  3. For a band that is parabolic near the Fermi level, $E_{n\bk}=\hbar^2k^2/2m^*$ with $k=|\bk|$, measured from its bottom, the coupling leaves the Fermi wavevector $k_{\mathrm F}$ unchanged. A smaller velocity at $k_{\mathrm F}$ then means a larger mass, $m^{\mathrm{qp}}=m^*(1+\lambda_{n\bk})$.

    By Luttinger's theorem the volume enclosed by the Fermi surface is fixed by the number of electrons and does not change with interactions; at fixed $k_{\mathrm F}$ the Fermi velocity $\hbar k_{\mathrm F}/m$ falls only through the mass.

The ARPES kink

Near the Fermi level of a metal the self-energy has a simple structure, set by the window of . Take one dispersionless phonon of energy $\hbar\omega_0$ and zero temperature, and follow a hole at a depth $|\hbar\omega-E_{\mathrm F}|$ below the Fermi level $E_{\mathrm F}$. It decays by emitting a phonon only if an electron from an occupied state $\hbar\omega_0$ higher can fill it, which requires $|\hbar\omega-E_{\mathrm F}|\gt\hbar\omega_0$. Inside the window the hole is dressed by virtual phonons only: it is heavy and long-lived. Outside, emission is open and the hole decays fast. The real part of the self-energy falls linearly inside the window, with the slope $-\lambda_{n\bk}$ of , so the band flattens by the factor $1/(1+\lambda_{n\bk})$; it peaks at the window edges, and beyond them it decays, so the band returns to its bare dispersion, broadened. The boundary at $E_{\mathrm F}-\hbar\omega_0$ shows up in ARPES as a kink in the measured band. computes it for a model band.

The model of , after the review, is a parabolic band $E_k=\hbar^2k^2/2m^*$ with $m^*=0.1\,m_{\mathrm e}$, filled up to the Fermi energy $E_{\mathrm F}$ measured from its bottom. It couples with a constant matrix element to one Einstein phonon of $\hbar\omega_0=100$ meV, and the intermediate states run from the band bottom to a cutoff $E_{\mathrm c}=5$ eV above the Fermi level. With a constant density of states, the energy integral of can be done in closed form:

  1. At zero temperature $n=0$ and $f$ is one below the Fermi level and zero above it. The first term of runs over the empty states, from $E_{\mathrm F}$ to $E_{\mathrm F}+E_{\mathrm c}$, the second over the occupied ones, from the band bottom to $E_{\mathrm F}$.

    These are the two weights $1-f$ and $f$ at $T=0$.

  2. Replace $\frac{1}{N_\ell}\sum_{\bq m}$ by $\int\dd E\,N(E)$, with the density of states per spin and per cell $N(E)=\frac1{N_\ell}\sum_{n\bk}\delta(E-E_{n\bk})$ taken constant, $N(E)=N_{\mathrm F}$, and the squared matrix element constant, $|g|^2N_{\mathrm F}=\lambda_{\mathrm{ep}}\hbar\omega_0/2$.

    For a dispersionless phonon and a constant $|g|^2$, the sum over intermediate states only counts states at each energy.

  3. Integrate $1/(z\mp\hbar\omega_0-E)$ over each energy range. With $z=\hbar(\omega+\ii\eta)-E_{\mathrm F}$, the probe energy measured from the Fermi level, and $E$ also measured from the Fermi level:

    $$\begin{gathered}\Sigma(\omega)=\frac{\lambda_{\mathrm{ep}}\hbar\omega_0}{2}\Big[\ln\frac{z-\hbar\omega_0}{z-\hbar\omega_0-E_{\mathrm c}}\\ +\ln\frac{z+\hbar\omega_0+E_{\mathrm F}}{z+\hbar\omega_0}\Big] .\end{gathered} \tag{A7.20}$$
    $\int_a^b\dd E/(y-E)=\ln[(y-a)/(y-b)]$. The occupied states lie between $-E_{\mathrm F}$ and 0, the empty ones between 0 and $E_{\mathrm c}$. With $\mathrm{Im}\,z\gt0$ the logarithms are continuous along the real axis.

$E_k$, $E_{\mathrm F}$, $E_{\mathrm c}$

$\lambda_{\mathrm{ep}}$

When the band and the cutoff are far away, $E_{\mathrm F},E_{\mathrm c}\gg\hbar\omega_0$, the real part near the Fermi level is $\frac{\lambda_{\mathrm{ep}}\hbar\omega_0}{2}\ln\big|\frac{\hbar\omega-E_{\mathrm F}-\hbar\omega_0}{\hbar\omega-E_{\mathrm F}+\hbar\omega_0}\big|$ plus the constant $\frac{\lambda_{\mathrm{ep}}\hbar\omega_0}{2}\ln(E_{\mathrm F}/E_{\mathrm c})$ from the far ends of the two ranges; its slope at the Fermi level is $-\lambda_{\mathrm{ep}}$. So $\lambda_{\mathrm{ep}}$ is the mass-enhancement parameter of , the same for every state near the Fermi level because the model self-energy does not depend on $\bk$. The imaginary part is $-\pi\lambda_{\mathrm{ep}}\hbar\omega_0/2$ outside the window and zero inside it. The figure also offers the density of states $N(E)\propto\sqrt{E}$ of a three-dimensional band, which changes details but not the kink. As in the review, the self-energy is shifted by a constant so that $\mathrm{Re}\,\Sigma=0$ at the Fermi level; this keeps $k_{\mathrm F}$ fixed, as Luttinger's theorem requires.14

With $E_{\mathrm F}=10\hbar\omega_0$, a metal, the quasiparticle band is flattened within $\hbar\omega_0$ of the Fermi level and sharp there, and it returns to the bare band, broadened, more than $\hbar\omega_0$ below.

The figure, like the review, keeps $\eta$ finite, which rounds off the logarithms. The slope of $\mathrm{Re}\,\Sigma$ just beyond the window edge then reaches about $\lambda_{\mathrm{ep}}(\omega_0/4\eta-1/4)$: the first term comes from the logarithm of that edge, rounded off over $\hbar\eta$, and the second from the logarithm of the opposite edge. For the review's $\eta=\omega_0/5$ this is $\lambda_{\mathrm{ep}}$, and the finite band raises it to about $1.1\lambda_{\mathrm{ep}}$. Where the slope exceeds one, has three solutions for one $k$, and the quasiparticle band bends into an S, the shape seen in ARPES of metal surfaces since the late 1990s; in the model this needs $\lambda_{\mathrm{ep}}\gtrsim0.9$.

With $E_{\mathrm F}=2\hbar\omega_0$, a degenerately doped semiconductor, the whole occupied band lies within a few phonon energies of the Fermi level. The quasiparticle band is heavier, and a broad polaron satellite appears below it. For the three-dimensional density of states no solution of follows the satellite; for the constant one the logarithm at the band bottom gives the equation extra solutions near it.

In graphene the kink lies between 150 and 200 meV below the Fermi level and is weak, because $\lambda_{n\bk}\approx0.1$ there.

The Eliashberg function and the coupling strength

The material enters only through the matrix elements, the band energies and the phonon frequencies. Insert $1=\int\dd E\,\delta(E-E_{m\bk+\bq})$ and $1=\int_0^\infty\hbar\,\dd\omega'\,\delta(\hbar\omega'-\hbar\omega_{\bq\nu})$ into each term. The occupations then depend only on the electron energy $E$ and the phonon frequency $\omega'$, and everything material-specific collects into one function:

$$\begin{gathered}\Sigma^{\mathrm{FM}}_{n\bk}(\omega)=\int\dd E\int_0^\infty\hbar\,\dd\omega'\,\alpha^2F_{n\bk}(E,\omega')\\ \times\Big[\frac{1-f(E)+n_{\mathrm B}(\omega')}{\hbar(\omega+\ii\eta)-E-\hbar\omega'}\\ +\frac{f(E)+n_{\mathrm B}(\omega')}{\hbar(\omega+\ii\eta)-E+\hbar\omega'}\Big] ,\end{gathered} \tag{A7.21}$$
$$\begin{gathered}\alpha^2F_{n\bk}(E,\omega)=\frac{1}{N_\ell}\sum_{\bq m\nu}|g_{mn\nu}(\bk,\bq)|^2\\ \times\delta\big(E-E_{m\bk+\bq}\big)\,\delta\big(\hbar\omega-\hbar\omega_{\bq\nu}\big),\\ \alpha^2F(\omega)=\frac{1}{N_{\mathrm F}N_\ell}\sum_{n\bk}\delta\big(E_{n\bk}-E_{\mathrm F}\big)\\ \times\alpha^2F_{n\bk}(E_{\mathrm F},\omega) .\end{gathered} \tag{A7.22}$$
$\alpha^2F_{n\bk}(E,\omega)$
Eliashberg function of state $n\bk$ (dimensionless): $E$ is an electron energy (eV), $\omega$ a phonon frequency
$\alpha^2F(\omega)$
its average over the Fermi surface, the isotropic Eliashberg function; "$\alpha^2F$" is one traditional name, not a product
$n_{\mathrm B}(\omega')$
Bose–Einstein function of a phonon frequency, $1/(\ee^{\hbar\omega'/\kB T}-1)$

The Eliashberg function is positive, independent of temperature, and holds all the material-specific information. It is proportional to the rate at which the state $n\bk$ scatters into states of energy $E$ by emitting or absorbing a phonon of frequency $\omega$. Its Fermi-surface average, the form used in the theory of superconductivity (Section A.9), weights each phonon frequency by the coupling between states at the Fermi level.

Three approximations make transparent near the Fermi level: $\alpha^2F_{n\bk}$ is replaced by its average over the states of the same energy (isotropy); phonon energies are neglected next to electron energies, so that $\alpha^2F$ can be taken at $E\approx\hbar\omega$; and the density of states is symmetric about the Fermi level (particle–hole symmetry). With energies measured from the Fermi level, take the imaginary part of with $\mathrm{Im}\,[1/(x+\ii0^+)]=-\pi\delta(x)$. The delta functions fix $E=\hbar\omega\mp\hbar\omega'$ in the two terms, and the integral over $E$ gives

$$\begin{gathered}\big|\mathrm{Im}\,\Sigma^{\mathrm{FM}}(\omega)\big|=\pi\int_0^\infty\hbar\,\dd\omega'\,\alpha^2F(\omega')\\ \times\big[1+2n_{\mathrm B}(\omega')\\ +f(\hbar\omega+\hbar\omega')-f(\hbar\omega-\hbar\omega')\big]\\ =\pi\int_0^{|\omega|}\hbar\,\dd\omega'\,\alpha^2F(\omega')\quad\text{at }T=0 .\end{gathered} \tag{A7.23}$$

The bracket is the sum of the two weights, $1-f(\hbar\omega-\hbar\omega')+n_{\mathrm B}$ and $f(\hbar\omega+\hbar\omega')+n_{\mathrm B}$. At zero temperature it is one for phonons softer than the distance $|\hbar\omega|$ from the Fermi level and zero otherwise. So $|\mathrm{Im}\,\Sigma|$ is the cumulative integral of $\alpha^2F$: zero at the Fermi level, rising through the phonon band and constant beyond the highest phonon energy. For an Einstein spectrum it is the step of the kink model. First-principles calculations for the surface state of Be(0001) reproduce a measured onset of this kind between 40 and 80 meV.

The real part gives the coupling strength, in three steps at zero temperature:

  1. With $\alpha^2F$ independent of $E$ over a window symmetric about the Fermi level, the $E$ integrals of give logarithms:

    $$\mathrm{Re}\,\Sigma^{\mathrm{FM}}(\omega)=\int_0^\infty\hbar\,\dd\omega'\,\alpha^2F(\omega')\,\ln\Big|\frac{\omega'-\omega}{\omega'+\omega}\Big| .$$
    For a window from $-E_{\mathrm w}$ to $E_{\mathrm w}$ the empty states give $\ln|\hbar\omega-\hbar\omega'|-\ln|\hbar\omega-\hbar\omega'-E_{\mathrm w}|$ and the occupied ones $\ln|\hbar\omega+\hbar\omega'+E_{\mathrm w}|-\ln|\hbar\omega+\hbar\omega'|$. When $E_{\mathrm w}$ is much larger than the phonon energies, the two terms with $E_{\mathrm w}$ cancel.

  2. Differentiate at the Fermi level, $\omega=0$:

    $$\frac1\hbar\,\frac{\partial\,\mathrm{Re}\,\Sigma^{\mathrm{FM}}}{\partial\omega}\Big|_{\omega=0}=-2\int_0^\infty\dd\omega'\,\frac{\alpha^2F(\omega')}{\omega'} .$$
    The derivative of $\ln|\omega'-\omega|-\ln|\omega'+\omega|$ at $\omega=0$ is $-2/\omega'$.

  3. Compare with $\lambda_{n\bk}$ of , averaged over the Fermi surface:

    $$\lambda_{\mathrm{ep}}=2\int_0^\infty\dd\omega\,\frac{\alpha^2F(\omega)}{\omega} . \tag{A7.24}$$
    $\lambda$ is minus the slope of $\mathrm{Re}\,\Sigma$ in units of $\hbar\omega$, and the isotropic $\alpha^2F$ is the Fermi-surface average.
$\lambda_{\mathrm{ep}}$
electron–phonon coupling strength (dimensionless), the Fermi-surface average of $\lambda_{n\bk}$

Soft phonons count more, through the factor $1/\omega$. For the Einstein spectrum of the kink model, $\alpha^2F(\omega)=\frac{\lambda_{\mathrm{ep}}\hbar\omega_0}{2}\,\delta(\hbar\omega-\hbar\omega_0)$; then returns $\lambda_{\mathrm{ep}}$ and gives the step $\pi\lambda_{\mathrm{ep}}\hbar\omega_0/2$. The Fermi-surface average $\lambda_{\mathrm{ep}}$ and the $\lambda_{n\bk}$ of a single state are related but not equal: $\lambda_{\mathrm{ep}}$ enters superconductivity (), $\lambda_{n\bk}$ the velocity of a particular band. Literature values of $\lambda_{\mathrm{ep}}$ are about 0.4 for aluminium and 1.5 for lead.15

The same factor appears in thermodynamics. Within the window every quasiparticle energy is compressed by $Z^{\mathrm{qp}}=1/(1+\lambda_{\mathrm{ep}})$, so the density of quasiparticle states at the Fermi level is $N_{\mathrm F}(1+\lambda_{\mathrm{ep}})$. Sommerfeld's electronic heat capacity, proportional to that density, becomes

$$C_{\mathrm e}=\frac{2\pi^2}{3}\,\kB^2N_{\mathrm F}\,\big(1+\lambda_{\mathrm{ep}}\big)\,T . \tag{A7.25}$$
$C_{\mathrm e}$
electronic heat capacity per cell (meV/K); the factor 2 counts the spins, since $N_{\mathrm F}$ is per spin

The review obtains from Grimvall's formula for the electronic entropy, in which $\mathrm{Re}\,\Sigma^{\mathrm{FM}}\simeq-\lambda_{\mathrm{ep}}\hbar\omega$ for the thermally excited energies $|\hbar\omega|\lesssim2\kB T$. It holds only for $\kB T$ well below the phonon energies; at high temperature the enhancement disappears. Comparing the measured coefficient of $T$ with the density of states of the band structure was the classic way to measure $\lambda_{\mathrm{ep}}$ in simple metals.16

Reading λ off a measured band

ARPES estimates of the coupling often take the ratio of the band velocities above and below the kink. That needs the bare velocity, which is not measured and is fitted with ad hoc models; near van Hove singularities, sharp peaks of the density of states, this overestimates the coupling significantly. Without particle–hole symmetry, as in doped graphene, $\mathrm{Re}\,\Sigma$ does not decay beyond the phonon energies but tends to a linear asymptote, which also changes the apparent bare band.

Beyond Dyson: the cumulant and polaron satellites

The Dyson equation with a one-phonon self-energy such as never has two phonons present at the same time. It can produce at most one satellite per phonon mode, and that satellite sits in the wrong place. Photoemission from doped oxides such as TiO₂ shows instead a ladder of replicas of the quasiparticle band, spaced by one phonon energy: the electron is removed together with zero, one, two or more phonons of its distortion cloud. This is a signature of the polaron. GW calculations show the same failure for plasmon satellites, and the cure is also the same, the cumulant expansion. Instead of solving a Dyson equation, one writes the propagator of a hole, for times $t\lt0$, as an exponential:

$$G_{n\bk}(t)=\frac{\ii}{\hbar}\exp\Big[-\frac{\ii}{\hbar}E_{n\bk}t+\mathcal C_{n\bk}(t)\Big] . \tag{A7.26}$$
$G_{n\bk}(t)$
propagator of state $n\bk$ in time; for $t\lt0$ it describes a removed electron (a hole), the case ARPES measures
$\mathcal C_{n\bk}(t)$
cumulant (dimensionless), fixed by requiring the expanded exponential to reproduce a low-order self-energy such as Fan–Migdal

The cumulant is computed from the second-order self-energy, and the exponential then generates diagrams with any number of phonons. The simplest test is one occupied level coupled to one phonon. A level of energy $E_0$ is coupled with a constant matrix element $g_0$ to a dispersionless phonon of frequency $\omega_0$; the strength of the coupling is the Huang–Rhys factor $S=|g_0|^2/(\hbar\omega_0)^2$, the mean number of phonons in the distortion cloud. Removing the electron leaves the lattice distorted, and the distortion relaxes by emitting phonons. compares the two approaches.

For this model both approaches can be solved exactly (derivation box below). The Dyson equation with the Fan–Migdal self-energy has exactly two poles, a quasiparticle and one satellite. The cumulant gives a ladder with Poisson weights:

$$\begin{gathered}\mathcal A(\omega)=\sum_{s=0}^{\infty}\ee^{-S}\,\frac{S^s}{s!}\\ \times\delta\big(\hbar\omega-E_0-S\hbar\omega_0+s\hbar\omega_0\big) .\end{gathered} \tag{A7.27}$$
$\mathcal A(\omega)$
spectral function of the single level (1/eV)
$s$
number of phonons left behind, $0,1,2,\dots$

The quasiparticle sits at $E_0+S\hbar\omega_0$, closer to the Fermi level, with weight $\ee^{-S}$, and the replicas follow at exact multiples of $\hbar\omega_0$ below it with Poisson (Franck–Condon) weights. For this model the cumulant is exact. The two approaches agree to first order in $S$; at larger $S$ the Dyson equation places its single satellite $\hbar\omega_0\sqrt{1+4S}$ below the quasiparticle and keeps too much weight in the quasiparticle. Verdi, Caruso and Giustino used the cumulant with first-principles Fan–Migdal self-energies to reproduce the polaron satellites measured in $n$-doped anatase TiO₂.17

Derivation: one level, one phonon

  1. Dyson. The occupied level has $f=1$, and at zero temperature $n=0$, so only the second term of survives, with the level itself as the intermediate state:

    $$\Sigma(\omega)=\frac{|g_0|^2}{\hbar(\omega+\ii\eta)-E_0+\hbar\omega_0} .$$
    There is one level and one mode, so the sums reduce to one term.

  2. The poles of $1/(\hbar\omega-E_0-\Sigma)$ solve $x(x+\hbar\omega_0)=|g_0|^2$ with $x=\hbar\omega-E_0$:

    $$\begin{gathered}x_\pm=\frac{\hbar\omega_0}{2}\big(-1\pm\sqrt{1+4S}\big),\\ Z_\pm=\Big[1+\frac{S(\hbar\omega_0)^2}{(x_\pm+\hbar\omega_0)^2}\Big]^{-1} .\end{gathered}$$
    The weight of a pole is $Z=1/(1-\partial\Sigma/\partial x)$, as in , and $\partial\Sigma/\partial x=-|g_0|^2/(x+\hbar\omega_0)^2$. The two weights add up to one, and their mean energy is $E_0$: $Z_+x_++Z_-x_-=0$.

  3. Cumulant. To second order in $g_0$ the hole propagator is $G^0+G^0\Sigma G^0$, with $G^0(t)=(\ii/\hbar)\ee^{-\ii E_0t/\hbar}$. In frequency, with $x=\hbar\omega-E_0$ and in partial fractions,

    $$\begin{gathered}G^0\Sigma G^0=\frac{|g_0|^2}{x^2(x+\hbar\omega_0)}\\ =S\Big[\frac{\hbar\omega_0}{x^2}-\frac1x+\frac{1}{x+\hbar\omega_0}\Big] .\end{gathered}$$

    In time, this is $G^0(t)\,\mathcal C(t)$ with

    $$\mathcal C(t)=S\big(\ee^{\ii\omega_0t}-1-\ii\omega_0t\big) .$$
    In frequency $G^0=1/x$, and $\Sigma=|g_0|^2/(x+\hbar\omega_0)$ by step 1. The simple pole at $x=-\hbar\omega_0$ oscillates as $G^0(t)\,\ee^{\ii\omega_0t}$, the pole at $x=0$ is $G^0(t)$, and the double pole is the derivative of $G^0$ with respect to $E_0$, which is $-(\ii t/\hbar)G^0(t)$. The cumulant replaces $G^0(1+\mathcal C)$ by $G^0\ee^{\mathcal C}$; since $\mathcal C(0)=0$ and $\dd\mathcal C/\dd t=0$ at $t=0$, the total weight and the mean energy $E_0$ of the level are kept.

  4. Expanding the exponential,

    $$\ee^{\mathcal C(t)}=\ee^{-S}\,\ee^{-\ii S\omega_0t}\sum_{s\ge0}\frac{S^s}{s!}\,\ee^{\ii s\omega_0t} ,$$
    $\ee^{S\ee^{\ii\omega_0t}}=\sum_sS^s\ee^{\ii s\omega_0t}/s!$. In each term oscillates as $\ee^{-\ii(E_0+S\hbar\omega_0-s\hbar\omega_0)t/\hbar}$, and a term $\ee^{-\ii Et/\hbar}$ of the propagator is a delta function at $E$ in the spectral function. This gives .

  5. For small $S$, $x_+\simeq S\hbar\omega_0$ and $Z_+\simeq1-S\simeq\ee^{-S}$: the two approaches agree to first order.

    $\sqrt{1+4S}\simeq1+2S$.

The single-level model is the simplest of the model Hamiltonians built from the electron–phonon Hamiltonian of Section A.2 () with bands, phonons and couplings chosen by hand: the Fröhlich, Holstein and Su–Schrieffer–Heeger models and their extensions. Such models can be solved without perturbation theory, by canonical transformations, path integrals, exact diagonalization or Monte Carlo methods, and answer questions a Dyson-level calculation cannot: weak against strong coupling, large against small polarons, band-like transport against hopping. The cumulant is one bridge between such models and first-principles calculations. In 2017 the review judged that the study of polarons with first-principles many-body methods was yet to begin.

Check yourself: three short questions

  1. At zero temperature, an electron sits 30 meV above the Fermi level of a metal whose phonons all have energies above 40 meV. Why can it not decay by emitting or absorbing a phonon?

    Answer. Emission would take it at least 40 meV down, to a state 10 meV or more below the Fermi level, which is occupied: the weight $n+1-f$ of is zero there. Absorption needs a phonon, and $n_{\mathrm B}=0$ at $T=0$. Both terms of vanish, so $\mathrm{Im}\,\Sigma=0$: the state lies inside the window of .

  2. A metal has $\lambda_{\mathrm{ep}}=0.5$. How much are the quasiparticle weight and the Fermi velocity reduced, and how does the low-temperature heat capacity change?

    Answer. By and , $Z^{\mathrm{qp}}=1/(1+\lambda)=0.67$, and the Fermi velocity falls to $v_{\mathrm F}/1.5$, a reduction by one third; the mass grows by a factor 1.5. By the coefficient of $T$ in the heat capacity grows by the same factor.

  3. In the single-level model of , take $S=0.25$ and $\hbar\omega_0=100$ meV. Compare the quasiparticle shift and weight of the Dyson and cumulant solutions.

    Answer. Dyson: $x_+=\frac{\hbar\omega_0}{2}(\sqrt2-1)=20.7$ meV and $Z_+=1/[1+0.25/1.207^2]=0.85$, with one satellite $\sqrt2\,\hbar\omega_0=141$ meV below the quasiparticle.
