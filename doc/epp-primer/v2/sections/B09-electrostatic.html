The electrostatic limit: recovering Part A

Part A builds everything from the instantaneous Coulomb interaction; the EPP formalism replaces it with the full electromagnetic field. This section lets the speed of light go to infinity in the phonon self-energy of Section B.7 and watches Part A come back, piece by piece. Of the four sectors of the photon propagator dressed by electrons, only the Coulomb sector survives: it is the screened interaction of Section A.5, and phonons dressed by it acquire the dipole–dipole force constants and the LO–TO splitting of Section A.4. Keeping the frequency dependence of the screening then gives something that adiabatic phonons cannot: the coupled LO-phonon–plasmon modes of doped polar semiconductors.

What you need: the phonon self-energy of Section B.7 and its vertices; the bare lattice of Section B.1, built from the short-range force constants alone; the free propagators and the propagator dressed by electrons of Section B.5; the four sectors of a propagator and their longitudinal and transverse projections from Section B.6; the Lorentz oscillator and the Lyddane–Sachs–Teller relation of Section B.3; Born charges, the dipole–dipole force constants and the LO–TO splitting of Section A.4; the screened interaction and the dielectric function of Section A.5; the adiabatic and nonadiabatic parts of the phonon self-energy of Section A.6. The picture: what an infinite speed of light removes

In Part A every interaction line is a Coulomb line. Two charges interact instantly through the Coulomb interaction $v$, the electrons screen it into $W^{\mathrm e}$, and the moving nuclei add $W^{\mathrm{ph}}$ (Section A.5). Light appears only as an external probe. The EPP formalism instead propagates the whole electromagnetic field. Its interaction lines are the propagator $D$ of the vector potential, which has a longitudinal part, Coulomb's interaction written in another form, and a transverse part, the photons (Sections X.1 and B.5). If both pictures are right, the EPP formalism must reduce to Part A when the transverse field is switched off. The clean way to switch it off is to let the speed of light $c$ go to infinity while the wavevector $\bq$ and the frequency $\omega$ stay fixed. This is the electrostatic limit.

Physical picture: what the limit removes

The transverse field obeys a wave equation. It reaches a distant charge only after travelling at the speed of light, and its propagator has poles on the light line of the medium, $\omega=cq/\sqrt{\varepsilon}$, where $\varepsilon$ is the dielectric function of the medium. The longitudinal field is fixed at every instant by Gauss's law, $\nabla\cdot$$\bE$$\,=4\pi$$\rho$, whatever $c$ is, with $\bE$ the electric field and $\rho$ the charge density. Sending $c\to\infty$ shrinks the wavevectors where the transverse field matters, $q\lesssim\sqrt{\varepsilon}\,\omega/c$, to the single point $\bq=0$, and leaves only the longitudinal field. For the longitudinal optical (LO) phonon of GaAs, with frequency $\omega_{\mathrm{LO}}$ ($\hbar\omega_{\mathrm{LO}}=36.2$ meV) and electronic dielectric constant $\varepsilon_\infty$$\,=10.9$, the light cone ends at $\sqrt{\varepsilon_\infty}\,\omega_{\mathrm{LO}}/c=6.1\times10^{-5}$ Å⁻¹, while the Brillouin zone extends to about $2\pi/a=1.1$ Å⁻¹ (lattice constant $a=5.65$ Å). The electrostatic limit therefore describes the whole zone except a sliver about $5\times10^{-5}$ of its size ().

The electrostatic limit is not the static limit. With $c\to\infty$ the field acts instantly, but the electrons that screen it can still respond with a delay: the screened interaction $W^{\mathrm e}(\bq,\omega)$ keeps its dependence on frequency. This is dynamic screening, the source of the nonadiabatic effects of Section A.6. This section takes the two limits one at a time, first $c\to\infty$, then, where it is justified, $\omega\to0$. The plan:

  1. show that only the Coulomb sector of the dressed photon propagator survives $c\to\infty$;
  2. identify the survivor as the screened interaction $W^{\mathrm e}$ of Part A;
  3. insert it into the phonon self-energy and, at $\omega=0$, recover the dipole–dipole force constants and the LO–TO splitting;
  4. keep $\omega$, and find the coupled LO-phonon–plasmon modes of doped semiconductors;
  5. list what Part A gets exactly, and what it misses.

Four sectors, one survivor

The object to take apart is the photon propagator dressed by the electrons alone, $D^{\mathrm e}_{\alpha\beta}(\bq,\omega)$ of Section B.5, a $3\times3$ matrix in the Cartesian indices $\alpha,\beta$. It solves the Dyson equation $D^{\mathrm e}=D^0+D^0\Pi^{\mathrm e}D^{\mathrm e}$ , where $\Pi^{\mathrm e}_{\alpha\beta}$ is the photon self-energy of the electrons, with units of 1/(length × time) so that $D^0\Pi^{\mathrm e}$ is a pure number. The free propagator $D^0$ is the sum of a longitudinal and a transverse line , :2

$$\begin{gathered}D^{0\mathrm L}_{\alpha\beta}(\bq,\omega)=-\frac{4\pi c}{\omega^2}\,\hat q_\alpha\hat q_\beta,\\ D^{0\mathrm T}_{\alpha\beta}(\bq,\omega)=-\frac{4\pi c\,(\delta_{\alpha\beta}-\hat q_\alpha\hat q_\beta)}{(\omega+\ii\eta)^2-c^2q^2}.\end{gathered} \tag{B9.1}$$
$D^{0\mathrm L}$, $D^{0\mathrm T}$
free longitudinal and transverse lines, in units of length × time like every $3\times3$ propagator $D_{\alpha\beta}$ of Part B
$\hat\bq=\bq/q$
unit vector along the wavevector, with components $\hat q_\alpha$
$\eta$

In matrix notation write $\hat\bq\hat\bq^{\mathsf T}$ for the $3\times3$ matrix with elements $\hat q_\alpha\hat q_\beta$, and $\mathbf 1$ for the unit matrix. Acting on a vector, $\hat\bq\hat\bq^{\mathsf T}$ keeps its component along $\hat\bq$ and $\mathbf 1-\hat\bq\hat\bq^{\mathsf T}$ keeps the part perpendicular to it. These two projectors, called $\mathcal P^{\mathrm L}$ and $\mathcal P^{\mathrm T}$ in Section B.6, are orthogonal and add up to $\mathbf 1$. The longitudinal line is a multiple of the first, the transverse line a multiple of the second.

Expanding the Dyson equation gives a series of chains, $D^0+D^0\Pi^{\mathrm e}D^0+D^0\Pi^{\mathrm e}D^0\Pi^{\mathrm e}D^0+\cdots$ (background box below), in which every free line $D^0$ is either longitudinal (L) or transverse (T). Section B.6 split the propagator into four sectors, by whether each end couples to a charge, through the scalar potential, or to a current, through the vector potential: $D_{\rho\rho}$, $D_{\rho\beta}$, $D_{\alpha\rho}$ and $D^{\mathrm T}_{\alpha\beta}$. In the series, the sector of a chain is fixed by its two outer lines: Coulomb (L) at both ends, L at one end and a photon line (T) at the other, in two orders, and T at both ends. Because the outer lines are projectors, each sector is one projection of the $3\times3$ tensor, one of the parts $D^{\mathrm L}$, $D^{\mathrm{LT}}$, $D^{\mathrm{TL}}$, $D^{\mathrm T}$ of Section B.6 . That section writes them as a scalar, two vectors and a $2\times2$ block; here they are kept as $3\times3$ matrices, which add up to $D^{\mathrm e}$:3

$$\begin{gathered}D^{\mathrm e}=D^{\mathrm{e,L}}+D^{\mathrm{e,LT}}+D^{\mathrm{e,TL}}+D^{\mathrm{e,T}},\\ D^{\mathrm{e,L}}=\hat\bq\hat\bq^{\mathsf T}D^{\mathrm e}\,\hat\bq\hat\bq^{\mathsf T},\\ D^{\mathrm{e,LT}}=\hat\bq\hat\bq^{\mathsf T}D^{\mathrm e}\,(\mathbf 1-\hat\bq\hat\bq^{\mathsf T}),\\ D^{\mathrm{e,TL}}=(\mathbf 1-\hat\bq\hat\bq^{\mathsf T})\,D^{\mathrm e}\,\hat\bq\hat\bq^{\mathsf T},\\ D^{\mathrm{e,T}}=(\mathbf 1-\hat\bq\hat\bq^{\mathsf T})\,D^{\mathrm e}\,(\mathbf 1-\hat\bq\hat\bq^{\mathsf T}).\end{gathered} \tag{B9.2}$$

A Dyson equation is a geometric series skip if familiar

Take the scalar equation $W=v+v\chi W$, with numbers $v$ and $\chi$. Its solution is $W=v/(1-v\chi)$. Expanding the denominator gives $W=v+v\chi v+v\chi v\chi v+\cdots$: a chain of bare lines $v$ joined by bubbles $\chi$, one diagram per term. Each term is the previous one with one more bubble and one more line. That is the content of the equation: $W$ is a bare line, or a bare line followed by a bubble and then by the full $W$ again.

The matrix equation $D=D^0+D^0\Pi D$ works the same way, with matrix products: $D=(\mathbf 1-D^0\Pi)^{-1}D^0$, or, multiplying by the inverses, $D^{-1}=(D^0)^{-1}-\Pi$. Two lessons recur in this section. First, the series can be sorted term by term, for instance by the kind of line at each end, before it is summed. Second, the sum has poles that no finite number of terms has: each term is singular only where $D^0$ is, while the sum is singular where $\mathbf 1-D^0\Pi$ cannot be inverted. Resumming is what moves a resonance.

The simplest medium: a constant dielectric tensor

Start with a medium simple enough to solve exactly: an insulator whose electrons respond with a frequency-independent dielectric tensor $\boldsymbol\varepsilon$, so that $\Pi^{\mathrm e}=\frac{\omega^2}{4\pi c}(\boldsymbol\varepsilon-\mathbf 1)$, as in Section B.5 . This models an undoped semiconductor at phonon frequencies, with $\boldsymbol\varepsilon$ the electronic tensor $\boldsymbol\varepsilon_\infty$. The Dyson equation then has a closed solution, derived in the box below:4

$$\begin{gathered}\frac{\omega^2}{4\pi c}\,D^{\mathrm e}(\bq,\omega)\\ =\Big[\frac{c^2q^2}{\omega^2}\big(\mathbf 1-\hat\bq\hat\bq^{\mathsf T}\big)-\boldsymbol\varepsilon\Big]^{-1}.\end{gathered} \tag{B9.3}$$

For an isotropic medium, $\boldsymbol\varepsilon=\varepsilon\mathbf 1$, the inverse splits into the two projections:

$$\begin{gathered}D^{\mathrm e}=-\frac{4\pi c}{\varepsilon\,\omega^2}\,\hat\bq\hat\bq^{\mathsf T}\\ -\frac{4\pi c\,\big(\mathbf 1-\hat\bq\hat\bq^{\mathsf T}\big)}{\varepsilon\,\omega^2-c^2q^2}.\end{gathered} \tag{B9.4}$$

Read it at fixed $\bq$ and $\omega$. The longitudinal sector is the free longitudinal line divided by $\varepsilon$: it grows in proportion to $c$, like $D^{0\mathrm L}$. The transverse sector has its poles on the light line of the medium, $cq=\sqrt{\varepsilon}\,\omega$. Well outside the light cone, $cq\gg\sqrt{\varepsilon}\,\omega$, it is $\frac{4\pi}{cq^2}(\mathbf 1-\hat\bq\hat\bq^{\mathsf T})$, which falls as $1/c$. The mixed sectors vanish. In an isotropic medium a longitudinal field polarizes the medium along $\bq$ only, so it never feeds the transverse field. In an anisotropic crystal it does, unless $\hat\bq$ lies along a principal axis of $\boldsymbol\varepsilon$: the polarization $\bP$$\,=\frac{1}{4\pi}(\boldsymbol\varepsilon-\mathbf 1)\bE$ of a longitudinal field has a component across $\bq$. The mixed sectors are then nonzero, but still small outside the light cone.

computes all four sectors for hexagonal boron nitride (hBN), a uniaxial crystal: its dielectric tensor has one principal value, $\varepsilon_{zz}$, along a single symmetry axis $z$, the optic axis (normal to the layers of hBN), and another, $\varepsilon_{xx}=\varepsilon_{yy}$, in every direction across it. Derivation: the closed form and its behaviour for large $cq/\omega$

  1. Write the free propagator in units of $4\pi c/\omega^2$, away from the light line, where $\eta$ can be dropped:

    $$\frac{\omega^2}{4\pi c}\,D^0=-\hat\bq\hat\bq^{\mathsf T}+\frac{\mathbf 1-\hat\bq\hat\bq^{\mathsf T}}{c^2q^2/\omega^2-1} .$$
    $\frac{\omega^2}{4\pi c}D^{0\mathrm L}=-\hat\bq\hat\bq^{\mathsf T}$ directly; for $D^{0\mathrm T}$, divide the numerator and the denominator by $-\omega^2$.

  2. Invert it. A matrix $a\,\hat\bq\hat\bq^{\mathsf T}+b\,(\mathbf 1-\hat\bq\hat\bq^{\mathsf T})$ has the inverse $a^{-1}\hat\bq\hat\bq^{\mathsf T}+b^{-1}(\mathbf 1-\hat\bq\hat\bq^{\mathsf T})$, so

    $$\begin{gathered}\Big[\frac{\omega^2}{4\pi c}\,D^0\Big]^{-1}=-\hat\bq\hat\bq^{\mathsf T}\\ +\Big(\frac{c^2q^2}{\omega^2}-1\Big)\big(\mathbf 1-\hat\bq\hat\bq^{\mathsf T}\big) .\end{gathered}$$
    The two projectors are orthogonal, $\hat\bq\hat\bq^{\mathsf T}(\mathbf 1-\hat\bq\hat\bq^{\mathsf T})=0$, and each squares to itself, so they are inverted separately.

  3. Multiply the Dyson equation $D^{\mathrm e}=D^0+D^0\Pi^{\mathrm e}D^{\mathrm e}$ by $(D^0)^{-1}$ on the left and by $(D^{\mathrm e})^{-1}$ on the right: $(D^{\mathrm e})^{-1}=(D^0)^{-1}-\Pi^{\mathrm e}$. With $\frac{4\pi c}{\omega^2}\Pi^{\mathrm e}=\boldsymbol\varepsilon-\mathbf 1$,

    $$\Big[\frac{\omega^2}{4\pi c}\,D^{\mathrm e}\Big]^{-1}=\frac{c^2q^2}{\omega^2}\big(\mathbf 1-\hat\bq\hat\bq^{\mathsf T}\big)-\boldsymbol\varepsilon .$$
    The terms $-\hat\bq\hat\bq^{\mathsf T}-(\mathbf 1-\hat\bq\hat\bq^{\mathsf T})+\mathbf 1$ cancel, which leaves . The vacuum light line $cq=\omega$ has dropped out: in the medium the transverse poles sit where the determinant of this matrix vanishes, on the light lines of the medium.

  4. For $cq\gg\omega$, write the matrix in the basis $\hat\bq$, $\mathbf t_1$, $\mathbf t_2$, where $\mathbf t_1$ and $\mathbf t_2$ are unit vectors perpendicular to $\hat\bq$ and to each other. Its transverse block is $(c^2q^2/\omega^2)\,\mathbf 1$ minus a fixed matrix, so only the longitudinal direction escapes the large term:

    $$\frac{\omega^2}{4\pi c}\,D^{\mathrm e}\simeq-\frac{\hat\bq\hat\bq^{\mathsf T}}{\hat\bq\cdot\boldsymbol\varepsilon\cdot\hat\bq}+O\Big(\frac{\omega^2}{c^2q^2}\Big) .$$
    For a matrix in blocks, the longitudinal element of the inverse is one over [the longitudinal element minus the mixed row times the inverse of the transverse block times the mixed column]. Here the longitudinal element is $-\hat\bq\cdot\boldsymbol\varepsilon\cdot\hat\bq$ and the inverse of the transverse block is of order $\omega^2/c^2q^2$, so the longitudinal element of the inverse is $-1/(\hat\bq\cdot\boldsymbol\varepsilon\cdot\hat\bq)$ up to corrections of relative order $\omega^2/c^2q^2$. The mixed and transverse blocks of the inverse contain the same inverse transverse block and are themselves of order $\omega^2/c^2q^2$. These corrections hold all of $D^{\mathrm{e,LT}}$, $D^{\mathrm{e,TL}}$ and $D^{\mathrm{e,T}}$. For $\boldsymbol\varepsilon=\varepsilon\mathbf 1$ the inverse is exact and gives .

Any medium: counting powers of $c$

The constant-$\boldsymbol\varepsilon$ medium suggests a rule. It holds for any electronic response, as a count of the powers of $c$ at fixed $\bq$ and $\omega$ shows.

  1. The free longitudinal line grows as $c$, and the free transverse line falls as $1/c$ once $cq\gg\omega$: $D^{0\mathrm T}\to\frac{4\pi}{cq^2}(\mathbf 1-\hat\bq\hat\bq^{\mathsf T})$.

    Read off . In $D^{0\mathrm T}$ the term $c^2q^2$ of the denominator wins over $\omega^2$.

  2. The self-energy $\Pi^{\mathrm e}$ falls as $1/c$.

    It is $1/c$ times a correlation function of the electronic currents , which knows nothing about light. The constant-$\boldsymbol\varepsilon$ medium shows it explicitly: $\Pi^{\mathrm e}=\frac{\omega^2}{4\pi c}(\boldsymbol\varepsilon-\mathbf 1)$.

  3. Every term of the Dyson series is a chain $D^0\Pi^{\mathrm e}D^0\Pi^{\mathrm e}\cdots D^0$, and every $D^0$ in it is either an L or a T line. A chain of L lines only scales as $c\,(c^{-1}\,c)^n=c$, whatever the number $n$ of bubbles. Replacing any one L line by a T line multiplies the chain by $c^{-2}$.

    Each L line brings a factor $c$, each T line a factor $c^{-1}$, and each bubble a factor $c^{-1}$.

  4. So the purely longitudinal chain grows as $c$, while every other term is at most of order $c^{-1}$. The other terms include the whole of $D^{\mathrm{e,LT}}$, $D^{\mathrm{e,TL}}$ and $D^{\mathrm{e,T}}$, and the terms of $D^{\mathrm{e,L}}$ with a T line inside.

    Each of them contains at least one T line. The largest, those with exactly one T line, such as the free transverse line itself and $D^{0\mathrm L}\Pi^{\mathrm e}D^{0\mathrm T}$, are of order $c^{-1}$.

  5. A physical quantity contains $D^{\mathrm e}/c$, not $D^{\mathrm e}$. On that scale the longitudinal chain is of order $c^0$ and everything else of order $c^{-2}$.

    The coupling of matter to the field is $-\frac1c\int$$\bj$$\cdot$$\bA$, the current density times the vector potential. A line gives the vector potential $\bA=D\bj_1$ that a current $\bj_1$ produces, so a second current $\bj_2$ feels it through $-\frac1c\,\bj_2^*\cdot D\cdot\bj_1$ (Section B.5): every line with a vertex at each end comes with $1/c$. The EPP manuscript splits this factor as $c^{-1/2}$ per vertex; for the ions it is the factor $(\hbar/2c\OV\omega_{\bq\nu})^{1/2}$ of each dynamic ionic vertex $J_{\alpha\nu}$ of Section B.7 , with $\OV$ the volume of the unit cell and $\omega_{\bq\nu}$ the frequency of phonon $\bq\nu$.

In formulas, with matrix products over the Cartesian indices:

$$\begin{gathered}\lim_{c\to\infty}\frac{D^{\mathrm e}}{c}=\lim_{c\to\infty}\frac1c\Big[D^{0\mathrm L}+D^{0\mathrm L}\Pi^{\mathrm e}D^{0\mathrm L}\\ +D^{0\mathrm L}\Pi^{\mathrm e}D^{0\mathrm L}\Pi^{\mathrm e}D^{0\mathrm L}+\cdots\Big].\end{gathered} \tag{B9.5}$$

Four sectors, one survivor: of the four sectors only the longitudinal one survives, and of it only the chains built from longitudinal lines alone. In the language of Section B.6, the survivor is the Coulomb sector $D_{\rho\rho}$; the three sectors with a photon at either end are gone. This is the precise sense of the statement that there are no photons in the electrostatic limit.5

The survivor is the screened interaction $W^{\mathrm e}$

The longitudinal chain can be summed. Two facts do it. Between two longitudinal lines a bubble acts only through its longitudinal element. And charge conservation turns that element into a density response, the language of Part A.

  1. Between two L lines the bubble enters through one number:

    $$D^{0\mathrm L}\Pi^{\mathrm e}D^{0\mathrm L}=\Big(\frac{4\pi c}{\omega^2}\Big)^2\big(\hat\bq\cdot\Pi^{\mathrm e}\cdot\hat\bq\big)\,\hat\bq\hat\bq^{\mathsf T} .$$
    Each L line is a multiple of $\hat\bq\hat\bq^{\mathsf T}$, and $\hat\bq\hat\bq^{\mathsf T}\Pi^{\mathrm e}\hat\bq\hat\bq^{\mathsf T}=(\hat\bq\cdot\Pi^{\mathrm e}\cdot\hat\bq)\,\hat\bq\hat\bq^{\mathsf T}$. Only the component of the induced current along $\bq$ talks to a longitudinal field.

  2. Charge conservation relates that element to the charge–charge correlator $\Pi^{\mathrm e}_{\rho\rho}$ . In the random-phase approximation (RPA) of Part B the charge–charge correlator is $e^2$ times the independent-particle density response $\chi^0$ of Section A.3:

    $$\hat\bq\cdot\Pi^{\mathrm e}\cdot\hat\bq=-\frac{\omega^2}{c\,q^2}\,\Pi^{\mathrm e}_{\rho\rho},\qquad \Pi^{\mathrm e}_{\rho\rho}=e^2\chi^0 .$$
    A current along $\bq$ is the only kind that piles up charge: for a wave $\ee^{\ii(\bq\cdot\br-\omega t)}$ the continuity equation reads $\omega\rho=\bq\cdot\bj$. The bubble $\Pi^{\mathrm e}$ is built from independent electrons (Section B.5), each of charge $-e$, where $e$ is the elementary charge.

  3. Insert this into step 1. Each bubble, together with the L line after it, multiplies the chain by $v\chi^0$:

    $$D^{0\mathrm L}\Pi^{\mathrm e}D^{0\mathrm L}=D^{0\mathrm L}\;v(\bq)\,\chi^0(\bq,\omega) .$$
    $\big(\frac{4\pi c}{\omega^2}\big)^2\big(-\frac{\omega^2}{cq^2}\big)e^2\chi^0=\big(-\frac{4\pi c}{\omega^2}\big)\frac{4\pi e^2}{q^2}\chi^0$, and $v(\bq)=4\pi e^2/q^2$ is the bare Coulomb interaction.

  4. Sum the geometric series and use the RPA dielectric function $\varepsilon^{\mathrm e}=1-v\chi^0$:

    $$\begin{gathered}D^{0\mathrm L}\big[1+v\chi^0+(v\chi^0)^2+\cdots\big]\\ =\frac{D^{0\mathrm L}}{1-v\chi^0}=\frac{D^{0\mathrm L}}{\varepsilon^{\mathrm e}(\bq,\omega)} .\end{gathered}$$
    This is the geometric series of the scalar Dyson equation in the background box above, with $\chi^0$ in the role of the bubble $\chi$: each term is the one before it times $v\chi^0$.

  5. Write the free longitudinal line with the Coulomb interaction, $D^{0\mathrm L}_{\alpha\beta}=-\frac{c}{e^2\omega^2}\,q_\alpha q_\beta\,v(\bq)$.

    $\hat q_\alpha\hat q_\beta=q_\alpha q_\beta/q^2$ and $4\pi/q^2=v(\bq)/e^2$.

So the longitudinal chain is $-\frac{c}{e^2\omega^2}\,q_\alpha q_\beta\,v/\varepsilon^{\mathrm e}$ at any $c$, and by it is all that survives on the physical scale $D^{\mathrm e}/c$. The result is the central formula of the section:6

$$\begin{gathered}\lim_{c\to\infty}\frac{D^{\mathrm e}_{\alpha\beta}(\bq,\omega)}{c}=-\frac{q_\alpha q_\beta}{e^2\omega^2}\,W^{\mathrm e}(\bq,\omega),\\ W^{\mathrm e}(\bq,\omega)=v+v\,\chi^0\,W^{\mathrm e}=\frac{v(\bq)}{\varepsilon^{\mathrm e}(\bq,\omega)} .\end{gathered} \tag{B9.6}$$
$W^{\mathrm e}(\bq,\omega)$
screened Coulomb interaction of Section A.5, with the nuclei clamped and the electrons in the RPA (energy × volume, eV Å³)
$v(\bq)$
bare Coulomb interaction $4\pi e^2/q^2$ (eV Å³)
$\chi^0(\bq,\omega)$
independent-particle density response of the electrons, spin included (eV⁻¹ Å⁻³), negative at $\omega=0$
$\varepsilon^{\mathrm e}(\bq,\omega)$
electronic dielectric function, $1-v\chi^0$, larger than 1 in a static field

Only the Coulomb sector has survived, and it is Part A's screened interaction. For large $c$ the dressed propagator itself is $D^{\mathrm e}_{\alpha\beta}\simeq-\frac{c}{e^2\omega^2}\,q_\alpha q_\beta\,W^{\mathrm e}$, up to terms of order $1/c$. In the four-sector language of Section B.6, where the Coulomb sector is the potential per unit charge and equals $v/e^2$ in vacuum, $e^2D^{\mathrm e}_{\rho\rho}=W^{\mathrm e}$: by the longitudinal block is $-\frac{c}{\omega^2}q_\alpha q_\beta D^{\mathrm e}_{\rho\rho}$, which is this form.

Light has dropped out completely: the prefactor $-c/(e^2\omega^2)$ and the components $q_\alpha q_\beta$ only translate a Coulomb potential into a vector potential. In the gauge of Part B there is no scalar potential, $A_0=0$, and an electric field that oscillates as $\ee^{-\ii\omega t}$ is carried by the vector potential $\bA=-(\ii c/\omega)\bE$, because $\bE=-\frac1c\partial_t\bA=(\ii\omega/c)\bA$. A Coulomb field is minus the gradient of the Coulomb potential, so a propagator of $\bA$ is $c^2/\omega^2$ times two gradients of a propagator of that potential, and the $1/c$ in the definition of $D$ leaves $c/\omega^2$.

In real space the same steps give $-\frac{c}{e^2\omega^2}\partial_\alpha\partial'_\beta W^{\mathrm e}(\br,\br',\omega)$, the gradients at both ends of the screened potential between the points $\br$ and $\br'$. That form holds even where $W^{\mathrm e}$ is not a function of $\br-\br'$ alone, as in a crystal.

In an anisotropic crystal the denominator $\varepsilon^{\mathrm e}(\bq,\omega)$ of is the longitudinal element of the electronic dielectric tensor. Define the tensor $\varepsilon^{\mathrm e}_{\alpha\beta}(\bq,\omega)=\delta_{\alpha\beta}+\frac{4\pi c}{\omega^2}\Pi^{\mathrm e}_{\alpha\beta}(\bq,\omega)$, the dielectric tensor of Section B.5 at finite $\bq$. By step 2, $1-v\chi^0=1+\frac{4\pi c}{\omega^2}\,\hat\bq\cdot\Pi^{\mathrm e}\cdot\hat\bq$, so

$$\begin{gathered}W^{\mathrm e}(\bq,\omega)=\frac{4\pi e^2}{\bq\cdot\boldsymbol\varepsilon^{\mathrm e}(\bq,\omega)\cdot\bq},\\ \hat\bq\cdot\boldsymbol\varepsilon^{\mathrm e}\cdot\hat\bq=1-v\chi^0 .\end{gathered} \tag{B9.7}$$

For an insulator at low frequency $\boldsymbol\varepsilon^{\mathrm e}$ is the clamped-ion tensor $\boldsymbol\varepsilon_\infty$, and $W^{\mathrm e}(\bq,0)=4\pi e^2/(\bq\cdot\boldsymbol\varepsilon_\infty\cdot\bq)$: the screened interaction behind the long-range fields of Section A.4.7

What this $W^{\mathrm e}$ leaves out

From Section B.6 on, the EPP formalism keeps only the macroscopic element of every response function, the one with no reciprocal-lattice vector added to $\bq$, and its electrons are independent apart from the RPA chain. Its $\varepsilon^{\mathrm e}$ is therefore the head of the RPA dielectric matrix $\varepsilon^{\mathrm e}_{\bG\bG'}(\bq)$ of Section A.3, its element with $\bG=\bG'=0$, where $\bG$ and $\bG'$ are reciprocal-lattice vectors: no local fields and no exchange–correlation. The electronic dielectric constant of a DFPT calculation, $\varepsilon_\infty$ of Section A.4, is the macroscopic one, one over the head of the inverse matrix, and contains both the local fields and the exchange–correlation kernel $f^{\mathrm{xc}}$. In sp-bonded semiconductors such as Si, dropping the local fields raises $\varepsilon_\infty$ by roughly 10 percent (literature values, illustrative). From here on $\boldsymbol\varepsilon_\infty$ stands for whichever electronic dielectric tensor one trusts.

Phonons dressed by $W^{\mathrm e}$: the Gonze–Lee term

Now let the survivor act on the lattice. A polar phonon carries oscillating Born-charge dipoles, and an oscillating dipole is a current. In Section B.7 the phonon self-energy came from this current emitting a field line, dressed by the electrons, and reabsorbing it. With $c\to\infty$ only the longitudinal line remains, and it couples only to the component of the mode current along $\bq$: the component that piles up the bound charge $-\nabla\cdot\bP$ of Section A.4. The self-energy becomes the energy of the mode's polarization wave in its own screened Coulomb field. That is the dipole–dipole interaction of Part A.

The ingredients come from Sections B.5 and B.7. The phonon self-energy $\Pi^{\mathrm{ph}}_{\nu\nu'}(\bq,\omega)$, an energy, is $-\sum_{\alpha\beta}J^*_{\alpha\nu}D^{\mathrm e}_{\alpha\beta}J_{\beta\nu'}$ . Its vertices are the dynamic ionic vertices $J_{\alpha\nu}(\bq,\omega)=(\hbar/2c\OV\omega_{\bq\nu})^{1/2}\,\omega\,j_{\alpha\nu}(\bq)$, built from the ionic current vertex $j_{\alpha\nu}(\bq)$ of mode $\nu$ , here at $\bG=0$:

$$j_{\alpha\nu}(\bq)=\sum_{\kappa\beta}\frac{e\,Z^*_{\kappa,\alpha\beta}}{\sqrt{M_\kappa}}\,e_{\kappa\beta,\nu}(\bq)\,\ee^{-\ii\bq\cdot\btau_\kappa} .$$

Here $Z^*_{\kappa,\alpha\beta}$ is the dimensionless Born charge of atom $\kappa$, with mass $M_\kappa$ and position $\btau_\kappa$ in the cell of volume $\OV$. Write $\bj_\nu(\bq)$ for the vector with components $j_{\alpha\nu}(\bq)$, called the mode current here: it is the Born-charge dipole that mode $\nu$ carries per unit mass-weighted amplitude (charge/√mass), and an oscillation at $\omega$ turns it into a current $\omega$ times larger.

In Part B the frequencies $\omega_{\bq\nu}$ and eigenvectors $e_{\kappa\alpha,\nu}(\bq)$ belong to the bare lattice: they come from the short-range force constants $\Phi^{\mathrm{sr}}$ alone, without the dipole–dipole part $\Phi^{\mathrm{dd}}$ (Section B.1). The field restores the dipole–dipole part, as the rest of this subsection shows.

The phonon propagator $F$ obeys $F=F^0+F^0\Pi^{\mathrm{ph}}F$ . If the off-diagonal elements of $\Pi^{\mathrm{ph}}$ are dropped, the diagonal approximation of Section B.7, its poles satisfy

$$\omega^2=\omega^2_{\bq\nu}+\frac{2\,\omega_{\bq\nu}}{\hbar}\,\Pi^{\mathrm{ph}}_{\nu\nu}(\bq,\omega) .$$

The diagonal element is $F_\nu=F^0_\nu/(1-F^0_\nu\Pi^{\mathrm{ph}}_{\nu\nu})$, and with the free line $F^0_\nu=\frac1\hbar\,2\omega_{\bq\nu}/(\omega^2-\omega^2_{\bq\nu})$ the denominator vanishes where $\omega^2-\omega^2_{\bq\nu}=2\omega_{\bq\nu}\Pi^{\mathrm{ph}}_{\nu\nu}/\hbar$.

The phonon self-energy in the electrostatic limit

  1. Insert the limit into the self-energy:

    $$\begin{gathered}\lim_{c\to\infty}\Pi^{\mathrm{ph}}_{\nu\nu'}(\bq,\omega)\\ =\frac{\hbar\,W^{\mathrm e}(\bq,\omega)}{2e^2\OV\sqrt{\omega_{\bq\nu}\omega_{\bq\nu'}}}\\ \times\big[\bq\cdot\bj_\nu(\bq)\big]^*\big[\bq\cdot\bj_{\nu'}(\bq)\big] .\end{gathered} \tag{B9.8}$$
    With the two vertices, $\Pi^{\mathrm{ph}}_{\nu\nu'}=-\frac{\hbar\omega^2}{2c\OV\sqrt{\omega_{\bq\nu}\omega_{\bq\nu'}}}\sum_{\alpha\beta}j^*_{\alpha\nu}D^{\mathrm e}_{\alpha\beta}j_{\beta\nu'}$. The limit $D^{\mathrm e}_{\alpha\beta}\to-\frac{c}{e^2\omega^2}q_\alpha q_\beta W^{\mathrm e}$ brings a second minus sign and factorizes the sum into $(\bq\cdot\bj_\nu)^*(\bq\cdot\bj_{\nu'})$. The factor $\omega^2/c$ of the vertices cancels the $c/\omega^2$ of the line, so the result is finite and independent of $c$.

  2. Insert the diagonal element into the pole condition:

    $$\begin{gathered}\omega^2=\omega^2_{\bq\nu}\\ +\frac{W^{\mathrm e}(\bq,\omega)}{e^2\OV}\,\big|\bq\cdot\bj_\nu(\bq)\big|^2 .\end{gathered} \tag{B9.9}$$

  3. Set $\omega=0$ inside $W^{\mathrm e}$. The dressed mode then has the frequency $\bar\omega_{\bq\nu}$, with

    $$\begin{gathered}\bar\omega^2_{\bq\nu}=\omega^2_{\bq\nu}\\ +\frac{W^{\mathrm e}(\bq,0)}{e^2\OV}\,\big|\bq\cdot\bj_\nu(\bq)\big|^2,\\ W^{\mathrm e}(\bq,0)=\frac{4\pi e^2}{\bq\cdot\boldsymbol\varepsilon_\infty\cdot\bq}\ \ \text{(insulator)}.\end{gathered} \tag{B9.10}$$
    This assumes that the electrons follow the lattice instantly: their response time, $\hbar$ over the energy of their excitations, must be much shorter than a phonon period. In an undoped semiconductor the smallest excitation energy is the band gap, 1.42 eV in GaAs against $\hbar\omega_{\mathrm{LO}}=36$ meV, and $\varepsilon^{\mathrm e}$ changes between $\omega=0$ and $\omega_{\mathrm{LO}}$ by less than one part in a thousand. It is Part A's adiabatic approximation, which sets $\omega=0$ in the phonon self-energy (Section A.6).

Physical picture: why $c$ and $\omega$ cancel

The two vertices bring $\omega^2/c$. A dipole $\mathbf p$ that oscillates at $\omega$ is a current $\dot{\mathbf p}=-\ii\omega\mathbf p$, and a current couples to the field with a factor $1/c$. The longitudinal line brings $c/\omega^2$: in the gauge $A_0=0$ a vector potential must grow as $c/\omega$ to carry a given electric field. The product is an instantaneous interaction between dipoles: $c$ has dropped out, and $\omega$ survives only in the electronic screening of $W^{\mathrm e}(\bq,\omega)$. At $\omega=0$ it is the electrostatic dipole–dipole energy of Section A.4.

The simplest case: a cubic crystal with two atoms

Take the simplest polar crystal, cubic with two atoms per cell, such as GaAs. Cubic symmetry makes the Born charges and the electronic tensor multiples of the unit matrix, and the charge sum rule makes the two Born charges opposite: $\mathbf Z^*_1=-\mathbf Z^*_2=Z^*\mathbf 1$ and $\boldsymbol\varepsilon_\infty=\varepsilon_\infty\mathbf 1$, with $Z^*$ a number, the Born charge of atom 1. At $\bq\to0$ the three optical modes of the bare lattice are degenerate. In each of them the two atoms move against each other along a direction $\hat\bu$ while the centre of mass stays at rest (Section A.1): $\be_{1\nu}=\sqrt{M_2/(M_1+M_2)}\,\hat\bu$ and $\be_{2\nu}=-\sqrt{M_1/(M_1+M_2)}\,\hat\bu$.

  1. The mode current is, with the reduced mass $M_{\mathrm r}$,

    $$\begin{gathered}\bj_\nu=\frac{eZ^*}{\sqrt{M_1+M_2}}\Big[\sqrt{\frac{M_2}{M_1}}+\sqrt{\frac{M_1}{M_2}}\Big]\hat\bu\\ =\frac{eZ^*}{\sqrt{M_{\mathrm r}}}\,\hat\bu .\end{gathered}$$
    The phases $\ee^{-\ii\bq\cdot\btau_\kappa}$ tend to 1. Atom 1 contributes $eZ^*\be_{1\nu}/\sqrt{M_1}$ and atom 2 contributes $(-eZ^*)\,\be_{2\nu}/\sqrt{M_2}$: the two atoms carry opposite charges and move in opposite directions, so their currents add. The bracket equals $(M_1+M_2)/\sqrt{M_1M_2}$, and $\sqrt{M_1+M_2}/\sqrt{M_1M_2}=1/\sqrt{M_{\mathrm r}}$.

  2. For the longitudinal mode, $\hat\bu=\hat\bq$: $\frac{W^{\mathrm e}}{e^2\OV}|\bq\cdot\bj_\nu|^2=\frac{4\pi e^2}{q^2\varepsilon_\infty}\,\frac{q^2Z^{*2}}{\OV M_{\mathrm r}}$, independent of $q$. For the two transverse modes, $\hat\bu\perp\hat\bq$, $\bq\cdot\bj_\nu=0$.

    Only the longitudinal current piles up charge; a transverse polarization wave has no bound charge and makes no longitudinal field.

The transverse modes are not shifted, so the bare frequency of the optical modes is the measured transverse frequency $\omega_{\mathrm{TO}}$, and gives

$$\begin{gathered}\omega^2_{\mathrm{LO}}=\omega^2_{\mathrm{TO}}+\frac{(\omega^{\mathrm{ion}}_{\mathrm p})^2}{\varepsilon_\infty},\\ (\omega^{\mathrm{ion}}_{\mathrm p})^2=\frac{4\pi e^2Z^{*2}}{\OV M_{\mathrm r}} .\end{gathered} \tag{B9.11}$$

This is the LO–TO splitting of Section A.4 , with the $\omega_{\mathrm{LO}}$, $\omega_{\mathrm{TO}}$ of that section and the ionic plasma frequency $\omega^{\mathrm{ion}}_{\mathrm p}$ of Sections A.5 and B.3. For GaAs, with $Z^*=2.07$, $\OV=a^3/4=45.2$ Å³, $M_{\mathrm r}=36.1$ amu, $\varepsilon_\infty=10.9$ and $\hbar\omega_{\mathrm{TO}}=33.3$ meV (literature values, illustrative), $\hbar\omega^{\mathrm{ion}}_{\mathrm p}=44.6$ meV and $\hbar\omega_{\mathrm{LO}}=35.9$ meV, against 36.2 meV measured. Any crystal: the dipole–dipole force constants

For a general crystal, write out the shift in atom by atom.

  1. The component of the mode current along $\bq$ is

    $$\bq\cdot\bj_\nu(\bq)=e\sum_{\kappa\alpha}\big(\bq\cdot\mathbf Z^*_\kappa\big)_\alpha\,\frac{e_{\kappa\alpha,\nu}(\bq)}{\sqrt{M_\kappa}}\,\ee^{-\ii\bq\cdot\btau_\kappa},$$
    with $(\bq\cdot\mathbf Z^*_\kappa)_\alpha=\sum_\gamma q_\gamma Z^*_{\kappa,\gamma\alpha}$. The first index of a Born charge is the direction of the dipole, which is contracted with $\bq$; the second is the direction of the displacement, which is contracted with the eigenvector.

  2. Its squared modulus is a double sum over atoms:

    $$\begin{gathered}\big|\bq\cdot\bj_\nu\big|^2=e^2\sum_{\kappa\alpha,\kappa'\beta}\frac{e^*_{\kappa\alpha,\nu}}{\sqrt{M_\kappa}}\big(\bq\cdot\mathbf Z^*_\kappa\big)_\alpha\big(\bq\cdot\mathbf Z^*_{\kappa'}\big)_\beta\\ \times\ee^{\ii\bq\cdot(\btau_\kappa-\btau_{\kappa'})}\,\frac{e_{\kappa'\beta,\nu}}{\sqrt{M_{\kappa'}}} .\end{gathered}$$
    $|z|^2=z^*z$. The Born charges are real, so the complex conjugate acts on the eigenvector and the phase only.

  3. Multiply by $W^{\mathrm e}(\bq,0)/e^2\OV=4\pi/(\OV\,\bq\cdot\boldsymbol\varepsilon_\infty\cdot\bq)$ and read the result as a matrix sandwiched between the eigenvector and its conjugate, divided by $\sqrt{M_\kappa M_{\kappa'}}$. The matrix is

    $$\begin{gathered}\Phi^{\mathrm{dd}}_{\kappa\alpha,\kappa'\beta}(\bq)=\frac{4\pi e^2}{\OV}\,\ee^{\ii\bq\cdot(\btau_\kappa-\btau_{\kappa'})}\\ \times\frac{\big(\bq\cdot\mathbf Z^*_\kappa\big)_\alpha\big(\bq\cdot\mathbf Z^*_{\kappa'}\big)_\beta}{\bq\cdot\boldsymbol\varepsilon_\infty\cdot\bq} .\end{gathered} \tag{B9.12}$$
    The factor $e^2$ of step 2 restores the $e^2$ of the Coulomb interaction. $\Phi^{\mathrm{dd}}$ has the units of a force constant, eV/Å².

  4. With the mass-scaled matrix $\tPhi^{\mathrm{dd}}_{\kappa\alpha,\kappa'\beta}=\Phi^{\mathrm{dd}}_{\kappa\alpha,\kappa'\beta}/\sqrt{M_\kappa M_{\kappa'}}$, the dressed frequency is, with every factor at the same $\bq$,

    $$\begin{gathered}\bar\omega^2_{\bq\nu}=\omega^2_{\bq\nu}\\ +\sum_{\kappa\alpha,\kappa'\beta}e^*_{\kappa\alpha,\nu}\,\tPhi^{\mathrm{dd}}_{\kappa\alpha,\kappa'\beta}\,e_{\kappa'\beta,\nu} ,\end{gathered} \tag{B9.13}$$
    The eigenvalues of the dynamical matrix $\tPhi$ are squared frequencies ; the bare $\omega^2_{\bq\nu}$ are those of $\tPhi^{\mathrm{sr}}$, the dynamical matrix of the short-range force constants. When this Hermitian matrix is changed by $\tPhi^{\mathrm{dd}}$, the first-order change of an eigenvalue is the expectation value of $\tPhi^{\mathrm{dd}}$ in the normalized eigenvector. So is first-order perturbation theory in the dipole–dipole term.

The matrix is the $\bG=0$ term of the dipole–dipole force constants of Section A.4 : the term that Gonze and Lee add to the analytic force constants of DFPT, built from the Born charges and $\boldsymbol\varepsilon_\infty$ alone. The phonon self-energy has produced it from a single line of $W^{\mathrm e}$ between two dipole currents. Its phase is the relative phase of the two atoms, and it is there because the vertex places each dipole at its own atom; with all dipoles of a cell at one point, the term would depend on how the atoms are grouped into cells (Section B.1).11

Apart from its phase, which tends to 1, the term is a ratio of two quadratic forms in $\bq$, so it does not change when $\bq$ is scaled. As $\bq\to0$ it therefore tends to a limit that depends on the direction $\hat\bq$ from which $\Gamma$ is approached:

$$\Phi^{\mathrm{dd}}_{\kappa\alpha,\kappa'\beta}(\bq)\to\frac{4\pi e^2}{\OV}\,\frac{\big(\hat\bq\cdot\mathbf Z^*_\kappa\big)_\alpha\big(\hat\bq\cdot\mathbf Z^*_{\kappa'}\big)_\beta}{\hat\bq\cdot\boldsymbol\varepsilon_\infty\cdot\hat\bq}\quad(\bq\to0) .$$

A function whose limit at a point depends on the direction of approach has no Taylor expansion there. This is why the term is called nonanalytic, and why the frequencies at $\Gamma$ depend on $\hat\bq$. The cause is the long range of the Coulomb interaction, a factor $1/q^2$, meeting the $q^2$ of two dipoles. In a cubic crystal the dependence reduces to LO against TO. In a uniaxial crystal, such as GaN in its hexagonal wurtzite structure, the LO and TO frequencies themselves change with the angle $\theta$ between $\hat\bq$ and the optic axis $z$, between their values $\omega_{\mathrm{LO},z}$, $\omega_{\mathrm{TO},z}$ for modes polarized along $z$ and $\omega_{\mathrm{LO},x}$, $\omega_{\mathrm{TO},x}$ for modes polarized across it ().

Acoustic modes are left alone at $\bq\to0$. In a rigid translation every atom moves by the same vector $\bd$, the eigenvector is $\be_{\kappa\nu}\propto\sqrt{M_\kappa}\,\bd$, and $\bq\cdot\bj_\nu\propto\sum_\kappa\bq\cdot\mathbf Z^*_\kappa\cdot\bd=0$ by the charge sum rule. At finite $\bq$ the acoustic eigenvector carries the phase $\ee^{\ii\bq\cdot\btau_\kappa}$ of each atom's position, which cancels the phase $\ee^{-\ii\bq\cdot\btau_\kappa}$ of the vertex; what remains is the dipole of the internal strain, the small relative displacement of the sublattices that the strain of the wave induces. It is a coupling of order $q$, discussed in Section B.1.

First order, or the full matrix

Equation is first-order perturbation theory on the bare modes, inherited from the diagonal approximation. A DFPT calculation instead adds $\Phi^{\mathrm{dd}}$ to the short-range force constants and diagonalizes the dynamical matrix of the sum. The two agree when the dipole–dipole term does not mix the bare modes, as in the cubic crystal above, where the LO and TO modes are already the eigenvectors of the term. They disagree when several polar modes lie close together compared with the size of the term. Wurtzite GaN is an example. Its bare polar modes along the optic axis and across it differ by 3.35 meV (27 cm⁻¹), while the dipole–dipole term shifts the frequencies by more than 20 meV. At $\theta=45^\circ$ the first-order formula puts two modes near 80 meV, where the crystal has none; the true modes are an LO-like mode at 91.5 meV and a TO-like mode at 67.6 meV, polarized along and across $\hat\bq$ rather than along the crystal axes.12

The matrix Dyson equation of Section B.7 does not need the diagonal approximation. Its poles are the frequencies where $F^{-1}=(F^0)^{-1}-\Pi^{\mathrm{ph}}$ cannot be inverted. Multiplying row $\nu$ and column $\nu'$ of $F^{-1}$ by $(2\omega_{\bq\nu}/\hbar)^{1/2}$ and $(2\omega_{\bq\nu'}/\hbar)^{1/2}$ gives the condition

$$\begin{gathered}\det\Big[\big(\omega^2-\omega^2_{\bq\nu}\big)\delta_{\nu\nu'}\\ -\frac2\hbar\sqrt{\omega_{\bq\nu}\omega_{\bq\nu'}}\;\Pi^{\mathrm{ph}}_{\nu\nu'}(\bq,\omega)\Big]=0 .\end{gathered} \tag{B9.14}$$

With at $\omega=0$, the matrix in the second term is $\sum e^*_{\kappa\alpha,\nu}\tPhi^{\mathrm{dd}}_{\kappa\alpha,\kappa'\beta}e_{\kappa'\beta,\nu'}$, the dipole–dipole term in the basis of the bare modes; the matrix $\omega^2_{\bq\nu}\delta_{\nu\nu'}$ is $\tPhi^{\mathrm{sr}}$ in the same basis. The static poles are therefore exactly the eigenvalues of $\tPhi^{\mathrm{sr}}+\tPhi^{\mathrm{dd}}$: the phonons of Part A. This also fixes what the bare lattice of Part B has to be. A dynamical matrix from DFPT already contains $\Phi^{\mathrm{dd}}$, and dressing its modes by the field would count the dipole field twice; the bare modes must come from the short-range force constants alone.

So far only the macroscopic field, $\bG=0$, has been kept, as the EPP manuscript does. A dipole at $\btau_\kappa$ also couples to the fields at $\bq+\bG$, through the phase $\ee^{-\ii(\bq+\bG)\cdot\btau_\kappa}$ of its vertex, with $\bG$ a reciprocal-lattice vector. If the electronic screening does not mix different $\bG$, each $\bG$ adds a term of the same form, and the sum is the dipole–dipole matrix of Gonze and Lee:13

$$\begin{gathered}\Phi^{\mathrm{dd}}_{\kappa\alpha,\kappa'\beta}(\bq)=\frac{4\pi e^2}{\OV}\sum_{\bG}\ee^{\ii(\bq+\bG)\cdot(\btau_\kappa-\btau_{\kappa'})}\\ \times\frac{\big[(\bq+\bG)\cdot\mathbf Z^*_\kappa\big]_\alpha\big[(\bq+\bG)\cdot\mathbf Z^*_{\kappa'}\big]_\beta}{(\bq+\bG)\cdot\boldsymbol\varepsilon_\infty\cdot(\bq+\bG)},\end{gathered} \tag{B9.15}$$

from which the field of each dipole on itself has to be subtracted. The sum converges only conditionally. Ewald's method makes it converge: a Gaussian factor in $\bq+\bG$ cuts the sum off, and a sum over lattice vectors in real space restores what the cutoff removed. Only its $\bG=0$ term is nonanalytic; the terms with $\bG\neq0$ are smooth functions of $\bq$, which could equally well be counted in $\Phi^{\mathrm{sr}}$.

In Part A the dipole–dipole term is put in by hand. Keeping $\omega$: LO-phonon–plasmon modes

The static step needs the electrons to follow the lattice. The bound electrons of an undoped semiconductor do. Free carriers bring a collective motion of their own, the plasma oscillation, which is slow when the carriers are few. In a doped semiconductor with $n_{\mathrm c}$ free carriers per unit volume and effective mass $m^*$, its frequency is the screened plasma frequency $\bar\omega_{\mathrm p}=\omega_{\mathrm p}/\sqrt{\varepsilon_\infty}$, where $\omega_{\mathrm p}$ is the plasma frequency of Section A.5 and the bound electrons screen it: $\bar\omega_{\mathrm p}^2=4\pi n_{\mathrm c}e^2/(\varepsilon_\infty m^*)$ (background box below).14 It grows as $\sqrt{n_{\mathrm c}}$ and sweeps through the phonon range: in n-type GaAs it crosses $\omega_{\mathrm{LO}}$ near $7\times10^{17}$ electrons per cm³. Near that density $W^{\mathrm e}(\bq,\omega)$ at phonon frequencies is nothing like $W^{\mathrm e}(\bq,0)$, and must be solved as it stands. Plasmon and LO phonon are then both longitudinal oscillations with a macroscopic field, and they mix.

The dielectric function of free carriers: Drude, Thomas–Fermi and Lindhard skip if familiar

Take $n_{\mathrm c}$ free electrons per unit volume in one parabolic band, $E_\bk=\hbar^2k^2/2m^*$, at zero temperature. They fill a Fermi sphere of radius $k_{\mathrm F}=(3\pi^2n_{\mathrm c})^{1/3}$, with two spin states per $\bk$, up to the Fermi energy $E_{\mathrm F}$$\,=\hbar^2k_{\mathrm F}^2/2m^*$; the electrons at the Fermi surface move with the Fermi velocity $v_{\mathrm F}=\hbar k_{\mathrm F}/m^*$. In a semiconductor they move among the bound electrons, whose response is $\varepsilon_\infty$, so the electronic dielectric function is $\varepsilon^{\mathrm e}=\varepsilon_\infty-v\chi^0$, with the independent-particle response of the carriers, Lindhard's function,

$$\chi^0(\bq,\omega)=\frac{2}{N_\ell\OV}\sum_\bk\frac{f_\bk-f_{\bk+\bq}}{\hbar\omega+E_\bk-E_{\bk+\bq}+\ii\eta} ,$$

where $f_\bk$ is the occupation of state $\bk$ and $N_\ell$$\OV$ the volume of the crystal. Two corners of this function are simple.

  • Fast fields, $\omega\gg qv_{\mathrm F}$. Expand the fraction in $(E_{\bk+\bq}-E_\bk)/\hbar\omega$. The first term cancels in the sum. In the second, shift $\bk\to\bk-\bq$ in the part with $f_{\bk+\bq}$; the energy differences then add up to $\hbar^2q^2/m^*$ for every occupied state, and $\chi^0\simeq n_{\mathrm c}q^2/m^*\omega^2$. So
    $$\varepsilon^{\mathrm e}(\omega)\simeq\varepsilon_\infty\Big(1-\frac{\bar\omega_{\mathrm p}^2}{\omega^2}\Big),\qquad \bar\omega_{\mathrm p}^2=\frac{4\pi n_{\mathrm c}e^2}{\varepsilon_\infty\,m^*},$$
    the Drude form, here without damping. It vanishes at $\bar\omega_{\mathrm p}$, where the carriers oscillate against the fixed lattice and the bound electrons: a plasmon. With $\varepsilon_\infty=1$ and $m^*=m_{\mathrm e}$ this is the plasma frequency of the electron gas of Section A.5.
  • Slow fields, $\omega\ll qv_{\mathrm F}$, with $q\ll k_{\mathrm F}$. The fraction becomes $\partial f/\partial E$, and $\chi^0\simeq-\partial n_{\mathrm c}/\partial\mu=-3n_{\mathrm c}/2E_{\mathrm F}$, with $\mu$ the chemical potential. So
    $$\varepsilon^{\mathrm e}(\bq,0)\simeq\varepsilon_\infty+\frac{k_{\mathrm{TF}}^2}{q^2},\qquad k_{\mathrm{TF}}^2=\frac{6\pi n_{\mathrm c}e^2}{E_{\mathrm F}},$$
    the Thomas–Fermi form: the carriers rearrange and screen a static charge beyond the length $\sqrt{\varepsilon_\infty}/k_{\mathrm{TF}}$.

Between the corners, and for $q$ comparable with $k_{\mathrm F}$, the full function is needed; Lindhard gave it in closed form. Its imaginary part is nonzero inside the particle–hole continuum, $\hbar\omega\le\hbar qv_{\mathrm F}+\hbar^2q^2/2m^*$, where a field can lose energy to single electron–hole pairs. A plasmon that enters this region decays, which is called Landau damping. For GaAs with $n_{\mathrm c}=10^{18}$ cm⁻³ and $m^*=0.067\,m_{\mathrm e}$: $k_{\mathrm F}=0.031$ Å⁻¹, $E_{\mathrm F}=54$ meV, $\hbar v_{\mathrm F}=3.5$ eV Å, $\hbar\bar\omega_{\mathrm p}=43.5$ meV and $k_{\mathrm{TF}}=0.071$ Å⁻¹.

  2. Multiply by $\varepsilon^{\mathrm e}/(\omega^2_{\mathrm{TO}}-\omega^2)$ and collect the terms on one side:

    $$\begin{gathered}\varepsilon(\bq,\omega)\equiv\varepsilon^{\mathrm e}(\bq,\omega)\\ +\frac{(\omega^{\mathrm{ion}}_{\mathrm p})^2}{\omega^2_{\mathrm{TO}}-\omega^2}=0 .\end{gathered} \tag{B9.16}$$
    The second term is the ionic part of the dielectric function, the Lorentz oscillator of Section B.3 , so $\varepsilon$ is the total dielectric function, electrons and ions together. The coupled longitudinal modes are its zeros, as Section B.6 found from the photon side : without external charges a longitudinal field $\bE\neq0$ can oscillate only where $\varepsilon\bE=\bE+4\pi\bP=0$.

  3. Insert the Drude form $\varepsilon^{\mathrm e}=\varepsilon_\infty(1-\bar\omega_{\mathrm p}^2/\omega^2)$ of the background box, and $(\omega^{\mathrm{ion}}_{\mathrm p})^2=\varepsilon_\infty(\omega^2_{\mathrm{LO}}-\omega^2_{\mathrm{TO}})$ from :

    $$\begin{gathered}\varepsilon(\omega)=\varepsilon_\infty\Big[1-\frac{\bar\omega_{\mathrm p}^2}{\omega^2}\\ +\frac{\omega^2_{\mathrm{LO}}-\omega^2_{\mathrm{TO}}}{\omega^2_{\mathrm{TO}}-\omega^2}\Big]=0 .\end{gathered} \tag{B9.17}$$
    $\omega_{\mathrm{LO}}$ here is the LO frequency of the undoped crystal, which fixes the strength of the ionic term.

  4. Multiply by $\omega^2(\omega^2_{\mathrm{TO}}-\omega^2)/\varepsilon_\infty$. This gives the quadratic equation $\omega^4-(\omega^2_{\mathrm{LO}}+\bar\omega_{\mathrm p}^2)\,\omega^2+\bar\omega_{\mathrm p}^2\,\omega^2_{\mathrm{TO}}=0$ for $\omega^2$, with the roots

    $$\begin{gathered}\omega^2_{\mathrm L\pm}=\tfrac12\big(\omega^2_{\mathrm{LO}}+\bar\omega_{\mathrm p}^2\big)\\ \pm\tfrac12\sqrt{\big(\omega^2_{\mathrm{LO}}+\bar\omega_{\mathrm p}^2\big)^2-4\,\bar\omega_{\mathrm p}^2\,\omega^2_{\mathrm{TO}}} .\end{gathered} \tag{B9.18}$$
    The two roots are the frequencies of the two coupled modes, called $\mathrm L_+$ and $\mathrm L_-$. Their product, $\omega^2_{\mathrm L+}\omega^2_{\mathrm L-}=\bar\omega_{\mathrm p}^2\,\omega^2_{\mathrm{TO}}$, and their sum, $\omega^2_{\mathrm L+}+\omega^2_{\mathrm L-}=\omega^2_{\mathrm{LO}}+\bar\omega_{\mathrm p}^2$, give the limits below.

At low density, $\bar\omega_{\mathrm p}\ll\omega_{\mathrm{TO}}$, the upper mode $\mathrm L_+$ is the LO phonon, at $\omega_{\mathrm{LO}}$, and the lower mode is a plasmon at $\bar\omega_{\mathrm p}\,\omega_{\mathrm{TO}}/\omega_{\mathrm{LO}}=\bar\omega_{\mathrm p}\sqrt{\varepsilon_\infty/\varepsilon_{\mathrm s}}$. It oscillates so slowly that the lattice screens it with its full static response, by the Lyddane–Sachs–Teller relation. At high density, $\bar\omega_{\mathrm p}\gg\omega_{\mathrm{LO}}$, the upper mode becomes the plasmon, at $\bar\omega_{\mathrm p}$, and the lower one tends to $\omega_{\mathrm{TO}}$: the carriers screen the macroscopic field of the lattice wave, and the LO–TO splitting disappears. In between, the two branches repel each other and exchange character where $\bar\omega_{\mathrm p}\approx\omega_{\mathrm{LO}}$, an anticrossing; for GaAs ($m^*=0.067\,m_{\mathrm e}$, $\varepsilon_\infty=10.9$) that happens at $n_{\mathrm c}\approx7\times10^{17}$ cm⁻³. At $n_{\mathrm c}=10^{18}$ cm⁻³, with $\hbar\omega_{\mathrm{TO}}=33.3$ and $\hbar\omega_{\mathrm{LO}}=36.2$ meV, the modes sit at $\hbar\omega_{\mathrm L+}=47.8$ meV and $\hbar\omega_{\mathrm L-}=30.3$ meV. The lower mode always lies below both $\omega_{\mathrm{TO}}$ and $\bar\omega_{\mathrm p}$, the upper one above both $\omega_{\mathrm{LO}}$ and $\bar\omega_{\mathrm p}$. Raman scattering on n-type GaAs observed this pair, after Varga had predicted it.15

What the adiabatic step gives instead

Now make Part A's adiabatic step in the doped crystal: set $\omega=0$ in $W^{\mathrm e}$, as in , and screen with the static response of the carriers in its Thomas–Fermi form. For the LO mode,

$$\begin{gathered}\bar\omega^2_{\bq\nu}=\omega^2_{\mathrm{TO}}+(\omega^{\mathrm{ion}}_{\mathrm p})^2\,\frac{q^2}{\varepsilon_\infty q^2+k^2_{\mathrm{TF}}}\\ (\nu\ \text{the LO mode}).\end{gathered} \tag{B9.19}$$

There is a single mode, and it slides down to $\omega_{\mathrm{TO}}$ as soon as $q$ drops below the screened Thomas–Fermi wavevector $k_{\mathrm{TF}}/\sqrt{\varepsilon_\infty}$, which is 0.021 Å⁻¹ in GaAs at $10^{18}$ cm⁻³; this happens at any density. The plasmon branch is missing altogether. In the language of Section A.6 the plasmon pole lives entirely in the nonadiabatic self-energy $\Pi^{\mathrm{ph,NA}}$, which is why no adiabatic calculation of the DFPT kind can produce the $\mathrm L_\pm$ pair. compares the two answers for GaAs and GaN.

When the static step is safe

The electrons must respond faster than the lattice, in one of two ways. Collectively: the plasmon, or in an insulator the gap, lies far above $\omega_{\mathrm{LO}}$, as in an undoped semiconductor. Or one by one: $qv_{\mathrm F}\gg\omega_{\mathrm{LO}}$, so that a carrier crosses a wavelength within a phonon period and the Thomas–Fermi corner applies. In GaAs at $10^{18}$ cm⁻³ the second condition holds for $q\gg\omega_{\mathrm{LO}}/v_{\mathrm F}=0.010$ Å⁻¹. shows both regimes: at small $q$ the two dynamic branches; at larger $q$ ridges that move onto the static curve, while the plasmon dissolves in the particle–hole continuum. The static step fails near the anticrossing. At small $q$ it also fails for dilute carriers whose plasmon lies below the phonon: then $W^{\mathrm e}(\bq,\omega_{\mathrm{LO}})$ is close to the undoped $4\pi e^2/q^2\varepsilon_\infty$, while $W^{\mathrm e}(\bq,0)$ is fully screened. Raman scattering of visible light in backscattering probes $q\approx0.01$ Å⁻¹, right at the crossover.

Giustino's review anticipated these modes. It expects new structure in the phonon spectrum whenever the electronic dielectric function has dynamical structure near the vibrational frequencies, names the coupled plasmon–phonon modes of degenerate polar semiconductors (doped so heavily that the carriers form a Fermi sea) as the example, and speculates that they could be obtained from the frequency dependence of the phonon self-energy (Section A.6). Equation does exactly this. The EPP formalism thus holds two analogous effects side by side: phonon polaritons, from the transverse photon (Section B.7), and plasmon–phonon modes, from the longitudinal $W^{\mathrm e}(\bq,\omega)$.16

Two corners of one function

The Drude form ($\omega\gg qv_{\mathrm F}$) and the Thomas–Fermi form ($\omega\ll qv_{\mathrm F}$) are opposite corners of the Lindhard function, and the limits $\omega\to0$ and $\bq\to0$ do not commute: the dielectric function of an electron gas diverges as both go to zero, in a way that depends on their ratio. uses the full function. It ignores the nonparabolicity of the conduction band, local fields and finite temperature. At room temperature the zero-temperature picture is a stretch in GaAs below about $3\times10^{17}$ cm⁻³, where $E_{\mathrm F}$ drops below $\kB T$$\,=26$ meV.

Check yourself: three short questions

  1. At fixed $\bq$ and $\omega$ the free longitudinal line grows as $c$ and the free transverse line falls as $1/c$. Why is the phonon self-energy independent of $c$, and why do the transverse sectors drop out of it?

    Answer. Each ionic vertex carries $(\hbar/2c\OV\omega_{\bq\nu})^{1/2}\,\omega$, so $\Pi^{\mathrm{ph}}$ contains $\omega^2D^{\mathrm e}/c$. For large $c$ the longitudinal sector is $-\frac{c}{e^2\omega^2}q_\alpha q_\beta W^{\mathrm e}$ , and the factors $c$ and $\omega^2$ cancel. The transverse and mixed sectors are at most of order $1/c$, so they contribute at order $c^{-2}$ and vanish.

  2. GaAs is doped to $n_{\mathrm c}=2\times10^{18}$ cm⁻³ ($m^*=0.067\,m_{\mathrm e}$, $\varepsilon_\infty=10.9$, $\hbar\omega_{\mathrm{TO}}=33.3$ meV, $\hbar\omega_{\mathrm{LO}}=36.2$ meV). Estimate $\hbar\bar\omega_{\mathrm p}$ and the two coupled modes at long wavelength. Which one carries the lattice?

    Answer. $\bar\omega_{\mathrm p}\propto\sqrt{n_{\mathrm c}}$, so $\hbar\bar\omega_{\mathrm p}=43.45\times\sqrt2=61.4$ meV. Equation gives $\hbar\omega_{\mathrm L+}=63.7$ meV and $\hbar\omega_{\mathrm L-}=32.1$ meV. The plasmon lies well above the phonon, so $\mathrm L_+$ is mostly plasmon and $\mathrm L_-$ is the lattice vibration, whose macroscopic field the carriers screen: it sits just below $\omega_{\mathrm{TO}}$.

  3. In wurtzite GaN the LO frequency at $\Gamma$ is 91.0 meV for $\hat\bq$ along the optic axis and 91.9 meV across it. How can a frequency at the single point $\bq=0$ depend on a direction, and why does the first-order formula fail at $45^\circ$?

    Answer. Apart from its phase, which tends to 1, the dipole–dipole term does not change when $\bq$ is scaled, so its limit at $\bq\to0$ depends on $\hat\bq$: it is nonanalytic, and bulk phonons with a small but finite $\bq$ feel this limit. First order fails because the dipole–dipole term couples the bare modes polarized along and across the axis more strongly than their bare frequencies differ; the modes reorganize into LO and TO with respect to $\hat\bq$, and only the matrix condition finds them.
