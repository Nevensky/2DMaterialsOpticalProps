The phonon self-energy: shifts, widths and the Kohn anomaly

The phonons of Section A.1 are computed with the electrons following the nuclei instantly. Real electrons need time to respond, and in metals and doped semiconductors that time can exceed a vibration period. This section shows how the phonon self-energy turns the delay into a shift of the phonon frequency and a finite lifetime, separates the static part that density-functional perturbation theory already contains, derives the formula that codes evaluate, and explains the Kohn anomaly, a kink in phonon dispersions drawn by the geometry of the Fermi surface.

What you need: the phonon frequencies, eigenvectors and phonon field of Section A.1; the electron–phonon matrix element of Section A.2; the static screening of Section A.3; and the phonon propagator, the screened interaction and the density responses of Section A.5, whose background boxes teach Green's functions and Dyson equations. Two background boxes in this section recall linear response and the Lindhard function of the electron gas.1

The picture: a lattice that drags its electrons

When an atom moves, the potential felt by the electrons changes, and the electrons rearrange to screen the change (Section A.3). The screening cloud pushes back on the nuclei. This is how the electrons shape the force constants $\Phi$ of Section A.1, and in a metal they change them completely. In the simplest model of a metal, positive ions in a uniform electron gas, ions without screening electrons would oscillate together at the ionic plasma frequency, about 30 meV for sodium, at every wavelength. The screening electrons soften this oscillation into sound; the last subsection works this out.

Section A.1 computed the force constants with the electrons in their ground state for each frozen position of the nuclei, so the electrons follow the nuclei instantly. This is the Born–Oppenheimer, or adiabatic, approximation, and density-functional perturbation theory (DFPT, Section A.3) computes the force constants within it. It is accurate when every electronic excitation that a vibration can produce costs much more than the phonon energy $\hbar\omega_{\bq\nu}$, typically 10 to 200 meV. Insulators with gaps of an electronvolt or more satisfy this condition; metals and doped semiconductors need not. In a metal an electron just below the Fermi level can jump to an empty state just above it, leaving a hole behind. Such an electron–hole pair with a small wavevector $\bq$ costs at most about $\hbar v_{\mathrm F}q$, where $v_{\mathrm F}$, the Fermi velocity, is the speed of the electrons at the Fermi level. When $\omega_{\bq\nu}\gtrsim v_{\mathrm F}q$ the electrons are slower than the vibration, and the screening lags behind it.

A lagging screening cloud changes the vibration in two ways. The part of its force that stays in phase with the displacement differs from the static force, so the frequency shifts. The part that lags by a quarter period does work on the electrons: the vibration loses energy by creating electron–hole pairs, so the phonon lives for a finite time and its spectral line acquires a width. One object, the phonon self-energy, collects both effects. The section proceeds in four steps:

  1. how a self-energy shifts and broadens the phonon line (the Dyson equation);
  2. how it splits into the static part that DFPT already contains and a dynamic remainder;
  3. what the remainder contains, one bare and one screened coupling, and the formula that codes evaluate;
  4. three applications: a doped two-dimensional semiconductor, the experiments that saw nonadiabatic phonons, and the electron gas, where the Fermi surface produces the Kohn anomaly.

Perturb a system in equilibrium by adding $-h(t)\,\hat O'$ to its Hamiltonian, where $\hat O'$ is an operator and $h(t)$ a weak drive. To first order in $h$, the expectation value of an operator $\hat O$ changes by $\delta\langle\hat O\rangle(t)=\int\dd t'\,\chi_{OO'}(t-t')\,h(t')$, with the retarded response function of Kubo's formula,

$$\begin{gathered}\chi_{OO'}(t)=\frac{\ii}{\hbar}\big\langle[\hat O(t),\hat O'(0)]\big\rangle\ \ (t\gt0),\\ \chi_{OO'}(t)=0\ \ (t\lt0).\end{gathered}$$

The response cannot precede the drive. In frequency space $\delta\langle\hat O\rangle(\omega)=\chi_{OO'}(\omega)\,h(\omega)$. Because $\chi_{OO'}(t)$ vanishes for $t\lt0$, the factor $\ee^{\ii\omega t}$ of the Fourier transform keeps $\chi_{OO'}(\omega)$ finite everywhere in the upper half of the complex $\omega$ plane, so its poles lie below the real axis; $\omega+\ii\eta$ enforces this. The retarded phonon propagator is minus the response of the phonon field to a drive coupled to it: with $\hat O=\hat\phi_{\bq\nu}$ and $\hat O'=\hat\phi^\dagger_{\bq\nu'}$, $F_{\nu\nu'}(\bq,\omega)=-\chi_{OO'}(\omega)$. The oscillator below checks the sign. At zero temperature the retarded functions equal the time-ordered functions of Section A.5 at positive frequency. At finite temperature they follow from the Matsubara formalism by continuation to real frequencies, and the formulas of this section keep their form with thermal occupations.

The simplest model: one oscillator and a modified spring

Take a single mass $M$ on a spring $K$, with frequency $\omega_0=(K/M)^{1/2}$ and displacement $\hat x$. In the units of Section A.1 the displacement is $\hat x=(\hbar/2M\omega_0)^{1/2}\hat\phi$: the zero-point amplitude times a dimensionless field. Drive the oscillator by adding $-h(t)\hat\phi$ to its energy. The drive $h$ is an energy; on the mass it acts as the force $(2M\omega_0/\hbar)^{1/2}h(t)$.

  1. Newton's equation for the mean displacement reads

    $$M\,\frac{\dd^2\langle\hat x\rangle}{\dd t^2}=-K\langle\hat x\rangle+\Big(\frac{2M\omega_0}{\hbar}\Big)^{1/2}h(t).$$
    For a harmonic oscillator Ehrenfest's theorem gives the classical equation of motion for the mean values exactly.

  2. Take a drive at one frequency, $h(t)\propto\ee^{-\ii\omega t}$: $M(\omega_0^2-\omega^2)\langle\hat x\rangle=(2M\omega_0/\hbar)^{1/2}h$.

    Each time derivative of $\ee^{-\ii\omega t}$ gives a factor $-\ii\omega$, and $K=M\omega_0^2$.

  3. Divide by the zero-point amplitude to obtain the field:

    $$\begin{gathered}\langle\hat\phi\rangle(\omega)=\frac{2\omega_0}{\hbar}\,\frac{h(\omega)}{\omega_0^2-(\omega+\ii\eta)^2}\\ =-F^0(\omega)\,h(\omega).\end{gathered} \tag{A6.2}$$
    $\langle\hat\phi\rangle=(2M\omega_0/\hbar)^{1/2}\langle\hat x\rangle$, and the mass cancels. The infinitesimal $\ii\eta$ selects the solution that responds after the drive, as in the box above.
$\omega_0$, $\hat x$
frequency and displacement (Å) of the oscillator of mass $M$ (amu) and spring $K$ (eV/Å²)
$h(t)$
drive coupled to the phonon field (eV)

So $F^0$ is minus the response of the phonon field to a drive, and its pole is the frequency at which the oscillator answers without being driven: its normal mode.

Now let the electrons add a correction $\delta K(\omega)$ to the spring. The correction depends on the frequency of the motion, because a slow motion is screened fully and a fast one is not. It can be complex, because part of the electrons' force lags behind the displacement. Repeating step 2 with $K\to K+\delta K(\omega)$ gives

$$\begin{gathered}F(\omega)^{-1}=F^0(\omega)^{-1}-\Pi(\omega),\\ \Pi(\omega)=\frac{\hbar}{2M\omega_0}\,\delta K(\omega).\end{gathered} \tag{A6.3}$$
$\delta K(\omega)$
frequency-dependent, complex correction to the spring (eV/Å²)
$\Pi(\omega)$
self-energy of the oscillator (eV)

With the correction, step 2 reads $[M(\omega_0^2-\omega^2)+\delta K]\langle\hat x\rangle=(2M\omega_0/\hbar)^{1/2}h$. Dividing by the zero-point amplitude as in step 3 gives $\langle\hat\phi\rangle=-h/[F^0(\omega)^{-1}-\Pi(\omega)]$, with $F^0(\omega)^{-1}=\hbar(\omega^2-\omega_0^2)/2\omega_0$ from .

The self-energy is the spring correction times the square of the zero-point amplitude, so it is an energy. A static, real correction moves the pole to $\omega^2=\omega_0^2+\delta K/M$, that is, to first order, to $\omega\simeq\omega_0+\delta K/2M\omega_0=\omega_0+\Pi/\hbar$: the frequency shifts by $\Pi/\hbar$. An imaginary correction describes friction. A friction force $-M\gamma\,\dd\hat x/\dd t$, with damping rate $\gamma$, is at frequency $\omega$ the spring correction $\delta K=-\ii M\gamma\omega$. Then $\Pi=-\ii\hbar\gamma\omega/2\omega_0$, so $\mathrm{Im}\,\Pi=-\hbar\gamma/2$ at $\omega=\omega_0$, and a free oscillation decays as $\ee^{-\gamma t/2}$. A negative imaginary part of the self-energy means that the oscillator loses energy to whatever produces the correction. shows what a self-energy of this kind, a static correction plus friction, does to the spectral line.

The Dyson equation for phonons

In a crystal the same construction applies mode by mode, with one new freedom: the electrons can also couple two modes $\nu$ and $\nu'$ of the same wavevector. They cannot couple different wavevectors, because a perfect crystal conserves crystal momentum (Section A.2). At fixed $\bq$ the propagator and the self-energy are therefore $3N_\kappa\times3N_\kappa$ matrices in the branch indices, and $F^0$ is the diagonal matrix of the $F^0_\nu$:4

$$\begin{gathered}F(\bq,\omega)=F^0(\bq,\omega)\\ +F^0(\bq,\omega)\,\Pi^{\ph}(\bq,\omega)\,F(\bq,\omega),\\ F^{-1}=(F^0)^{-1}-\Pi^{\ph}.\end{gathered} \tag{A6.4}$$
$\Pi^{\ph}_{\nu\nu'}(\bq,\omega)$
phonon self-energy (eV), retarded: the sum of all processes in which the phonon field of mode $\nu'$ disappears into the electrons and that of mode $\nu$ reappears, excluding those that fall apart into two such processes when one free phonon line is cut. Processes of this kind are called proper, or one-phonon irreducible.

  1. Insert the equation into its own right side again and again:

    $$F=F^0+F^0\Pi^{\ph}F^0+F^0\Pi^{\ph}F^0\Pi^{\ph}F^0+\dots$$
    Each term is a history: the phonon propagates freely, is scattered by the electrons, propagates freely again, and so on. Because $\Pi^{\ph}$ contains only proper processes, every history appears exactly once.

  2. Sum the series: $F=F^0\big(1-\Pi^{\ph}F^0\big)^{-1}$, so $F^{-1}=(F^0)^{-1}-\Pi^{\ph}$.

    It is a geometric series of matrices, $1+X+X^2+\dots=(1-X)^{-1}$ with $X=\Pi^{\ph}F^0$. The series converges only where $X$ is small, which fails near the free poles, exactly where the phonon is; the closed form holds at every frequency. Any finite number of terms leaves the pole where it was, and only the sum moves it ().

  3. For a mode that is not degenerate with another mode of the same $\bq$, keep only the diagonal element:

    $$\begin{gathered}F_{\nu\nu}(\bq,\omega)\\ \simeq\frac1\hbar\,\frac{2\omega_{\bq\nu}}{\omega^2-\omega^2_{\bq\nu}-2\omega_{\bq\nu}\Pi^{\ph}_{\nu\nu}/\hbar}.\end{gathered} \tag{A6.5}$$
    Here $\Pi^{\ph}_{\nu\nu}$ is taken at $(\bq,\omega)$. An off-diagonal element $\Pi^{\ph}_{\nu\nu'}$ shifts mode $\nu$ only at second order, by about $|\Pi^{\ph}_{\nu\nu'}|^2$ divided by the energy that separates modes $\nu$ and $\nu'$. It matters when the two modes are degenerate or nearly so.

The poles of are the solutions of

$$\omega^2=\omega^2_{\bq\nu}+\frac{2\omega_{\bq\nu}}{\hbar}\,\Pi^{\ph}_{\nu\nu}(\bq,\omega). \tag{A6.6}$$

Because $\Pi^{\ph}$ is complex and depends on $\omega$, the solutions can leave the real axis, and one mode can have more than one solution. The self-energy has a direct meaning in the language of Section A.1. If the electrons change the force constants by a frequency-dependent amount, the change and the self-energy are related by the crystal version of :

The review lists four ways in which $\Pi^{\ph}$ changes the phonons:5

  1. the real part of a diagonal element shifts the frequency;
  2. its imaginary part broadens the line;
  3. off-diagonal elements mix modes of the same wavevector;
  4. a strongly frequency-dependent $\Pi^{\ph}$ can give a mode more than one pole. Shift, width and lifetime

The self-energy is usually much smaller than $\hbar\omega_{\bq\nu}$, and can then be solved to first order.

  1. Write the pole as $\omega=\omega_{\bq\nu}+\Delta\omega_{\bq\nu}-\ii\gamma_{\bq\nu}$, with $|\Delta\omega_{\bq\nu}|$ and $\gamma_{\bq\nu}$ small compared with $\omega_{\bq\nu}$.

    A small self-energy moves the pole a little. Writing $-\ii\gamma_{\bq\nu}$ with $\gamma_{\bq\nu}\ge0$ keeps the pole below the real axis, as a retarded function requires.

  2. Expand the left side of to first order: $\omega^2\simeq\omega^2_{\bq\nu}+2\omega_{\bq\nu}(\Delta\omega_{\bq\nu}-\ii\gamma_{\bq\nu})$.

    The square $(\Delta\omega_{\bq\nu}-\ii\gamma_{\bq\nu})^2$ is of second order and is dropped.

  3. Evaluate the self-energy at $\omega_{\bq\nu}$ instead of at the pole.

    If $\Pi^{\ph}$ changes little over a frequency range of order $|\Delta\omega_{\bq\nu}|+\gamma_{\bq\nu}$, the error is again of second order. This fails where $\Pi^{\ph}$ changes abruptly, for instance where the phonon enters or leaves the band of electron–hole pairs ().

  4. Compare real and imaginary parts:

    $$\begin{gathered}\Delta\omega_{\bq\nu}\simeq\frac1\hbar\,\mathrm{Re}\,\Pi^{\ph}_{\nu\nu}(\bq,\omega_{\bq\nu}),\\ \gamma_{\bq\nu}\simeq-\frac1\hbar\,\mathrm{Im}\,\Pi^{\ph}_{\nu\nu}(\bq,\omega_{\bq\nu}).\end{gathered} \tag{A6.8}$$
    After step 2, reads $2\omega_{\bq\nu}(\Delta\omega_{\bq\nu}-\ii\gamma_{\bq\nu})=2\omega_{\bq\nu}\Pi^{\ph}_{\nu\nu}/\hbar$, and the factor $2\omega_{\bq\nu}$ cancels.
$\Delta\omega_{\bq\nu}$
frequency shift of mode $\bq\nu$ ($\hbar\Delta\omega_{\bq\nu}$ in meV); $\bar\omega_{\bq\nu}=\omega_{\bq\nu}+\Delta\omega_{\bq\nu}$ is the renormalized frequency
$\gamma_{\bq\nu}$
linewidth of mode $\bq\nu$, defined as the half width at half maximum (HWHM) of the line, in angular frequency; the full width at half maximum is $2\gamma_{\bq\nu}$

A passive medium can only absorb energy, so for $\omega\gt0$ the imaginary part of a retarded self-energy is negative or zero, and $\gamma_{\bq\nu}\ge0$.6 Near its peak the spectral function becomes a Lorentzian of half width $\hbar\gamma_{\bq\nu}$:

$$\mathcal B_{\bq\nu}(\omega)\simeq\frac1\pi\,\frac{\hbar\gamma_{\bq\nu}}{(\hbar\omega-\hbar\bar\omega_{\bq\nu})^2+\hbar^2\gamma^2_{\bq\nu}}. \tag{A6.9}$$

With $\Pi^{\ph}$ taken at $\omega_{\bq\nu}$, steps 2 and 4 make the denominator of equal to $\omega^2-(\bar\omega_{\bq\nu}-\ii\gamma_{\bq\nu})^2$ to first order. Near the peak, $\omega\simeq\bar\omega_{\bq\nu}$, this is $2\bar\omega_{\bq\nu}(\omega-\bar\omega_{\bq\nu}+\ii\gamma_{\bq\nu})$, and the weight of the line, $\omega_{\bq\nu}/\bar\omega_{\bq\nu}$, is 1 to first order.

In time, the pole gives an amplitude that oscillates as $\ee^{-\ii\bar\omega_{\bq\nu}t}$ and decays as $\ee^{-\gamma_{\bq\nu}t}$. The number of phonons, proportional to the squared amplitude, decays twice as fast, so the phonon lifetime is

$$\tau^{\ph}_{\bq\nu}=\frac{1}{2\gamma_{\bq\nu}}. \tag{A6.10}$$

In numbers, $\hbar\gamma_{\bq\nu}=1$ meV gives $\tau^{\ph}_{\bq\nu}=0.33$ ps and a full width of 2 meV, or 16 cm⁻¹. Experimental papers often quote the full width as "the linewidth". In this primer $\gamma_{\bq\nu}$ is always the half width, and the friction oscillator above has $\gamma_{\bq\nu}=\gamma/2$.

Static and dynamic parts: the adiabatic split

The size of $\Pi^{\ph}$ depends on what $F^0$ already contains, that is, on the reference lattice whose frequencies $\omega_{\bq\nu}$ enter $F^0$. The static part is what DFPT computes

A static self-energy is a correction to the force constants, by with a frequency-independent $\delta\Phi$. The reference force constants plus this correction are the force constants of the lattice with its electrons in the ground state: the Born–Oppenheimer force constants $\Phi$ of Section A.1. Writing them out shows where the electrons enter. The energy surface $U$ of Section A.1 is the ground-state energy of the electrons in the potential $V^{\mathrm{en}}$ of the nuclei, plus the Coulomb repulsion $U^{\mathrm{nn}}$ of the nuclei, and it depends on the displacements $u_{\ell\kappa\alpha}$.

  1. The first derivative is

    $$\begin{gathered}\frac{\partial U}{\partial u_{\ell\kappa\alpha}}=\int\dd^3r\,n_{\mathrm e}(\br)\,\frac{\partial V^{\mathrm{en}}(\br)}{\partial u_{\ell\kappa\alpha}}\\ +\frac{\partial U^{\mathrm{nn}}}{\partial u_{\ell\kappa\alpha}}.\end{gathered}$$
    By the Hellmann–Feynman theorem, the derivative of a ground-state energy with respect to a parameter of the Hamiltonian is the expectation value of the derivative of the Hamiltonian. Only $V^{\mathrm{en}}$ and $U^{\mathrm{nn}}$ depend on the positions of the nuclei, and the expectation value of $V^{\mathrm{en}}$ is its integral with the electron density.

  2. Differentiate again, with the product rule, because the density depends on the positions too:

    $$\begin{gathered}\Phi_{\ell\kappa\alpha,\ell'\kappa'\beta}=\\ \int\dd^3r\,\frac{\partial n_{\mathrm e}(\br)}{\partial u_{\ell'\kappa'\beta}}\,\frac{\partial V^{\mathrm{en}}(\br)}{\partial u_{\ell\kappa\alpha}}\\ +\int\dd^3r\,n_{\mathrm e}(\br)\,\frac{\partial^2V^{\mathrm{en}}(\br)}{\partial u_{\ell\kappa\alpha}\,\partial u_{\ell'\kappa'\beta}}\\ +\frac{\partial^2U^{\mathrm{nn}}}{\partial u_{\ell\kappa\alpha}\,\partial u_{\ell'\kappa'\beta}}.\end{gathered} \tag{A6.12}$$
    All derivatives are taken at equilibrium, as in the definition .

  3. The density change is the static response to the change of the potential:

    $$\frac{\partial n_{\mathrm e}(\br)}{\partial u_{\ell'\kappa'\beta}}=\int\dd^3r'\,\chi(\br,\br';0)\,\frac{\partial V^{\mathrm{en}}(\br')}{\partial u_{\ell'\kappa'\beta}}.$$
    $\chi$ is the density response of the interacting electrons to a change of the external potential (Section A.5). The Hartree and exchange–correlation potentials created by the induced density are inside it; DFPT computes them with the self-consistent loop of Section A.3.

The first term of is the potential change, times the static response, times the potential change. It is negative for every displacement pattern, so the induced electrons always soften the lattice. The dynamic remainder

Let the nuclei oscillate at frequency $\omega$ instead of standing still. The second and third terms of do not change: they contain only the ground-state density and the positions of the nuclei, and the Coulomb interaction of Part A acts instantly. Only the induced density changes, because it now follows the potential with the dynamic response $\chi(\omega)$. The nonadiabatic correction to the force constants is therefore the first term of with $\chi(\omega)-\chi(0)$ in place of $\chi(0)$, and turns it into the mode basis:

$N_\ell$

The mode basis collects the derivatives $\partial V^{\mathrm{en}}/\partial u_{\ell\kappa\alpha}$ of all atoms, weighted by the zero-point amplitudes, eigenvectors and phases of the mode, into $N_\ell^{-1/2}\Delta_{\bq\nu}V^{\mathrm{en}}$, with $\Delta_{\bq\nu}V$ as Section A.2 defines it. One factor $N_\ell^{-1/2}$ comes from each end, because one phonon is spread over the whole crystal . The double integral over the supercell grows as $N_\ell$, so $\Pi^{\mathrm{ph,NA}}$ does not depend on the size of the supercell.

Equation is exact within the harmonic approximation. It is Baym's result of Section A.5 in another form: the nuclei interact through the electron-screened interaction $W^{\rme}$, and with $W^{\rme}=v+v\chi v$ its dynamic part is $W^{\rme}(\omega)-W^{\rme}(0)=v[\chi(\omega)-\chi(0)]v$, while the gradient of the Coulomb interaction $v$ at a nucleus is the gradient of the nuclear potential.9 The nonadiabatic part depends only on the electrons' delay, through $\chi(\omega)-\chi(0)$.

The DFPT lattice already contains the static part. With that lattice as the reference, the self-energy in the Dyson equation is the nonadiabatic part alone:

$$F=F^0+F^0\,\Pi^{\mathrm{ph,NA}}\,F. \tag{A6.14}$$

From here on $\omega_{\bq\nu}$, $e_{\kappa\alpha,\nu}(\bq)$ and $F^0$ are those of the DFPT lattice, and the self-energy in – is $\Pi^{\mathrm{ph,NA}}$. Moving the static part into the reference changes the modes, so $\Pi^{\mathrm{ph,NA}}$ is projected on the DFPT modes, as does.

Physical picture: who is faster, the electrons or the nuclei?

The adiabatic approximation requires $|E_{m\bk+\bq}-E_{n\bk}|\gg\hbar\omega_{\bq\nu}$ for the electronic transitions $n\bk\to m\,\bk+\bq$ that the phonon couples, where $E_{n\bk}$ are the band energies. In an insulator every transition costs at least the gap, and $\chi(\omega)-\chi(0)$ is smaller than the static response by a factor of order (phonon energy/gap)², about $4\times10^{-3}$ for a 60 meV phonon and a 1 eV gap. In a metal, a transition within one band at small $\bq$ costs at most about $\hbar v_{\mathrm F}q$, so the electrons are slower than the phonon when $q\lesssim\omega_{\bq\nu}/v_{\mathrm F}$ (Maksimov and Shulga). In doped graphene, with $\hbar v_{\mathrm F}\approx6.6$ eV Å and $\hbar\omega=196$ meV for the Raman-active optical phonon, this window is $q\lesssim0.03$ Å⁻¹, 2% of the distance from $\Gamma$ to K, but it contains the $\bq=0$ phonon that Raman scattering measures. In a doped semiconductor whose Fermi energy is comparable to $\hbar\omega_{\bq\nu}$, the window extends to about twice the Fermi wavevector.

Inside the nonadiabatic self-energy: one bare and one screened vertex

Equation has the bare vertex at both ends and the full response $\chi$ between them. The response is a chain: the electrons respond to a potential with the irreducible response $\chi^{\mathrm{irr}}$, the induced density creates a Hartree potential through $v$, the electrons respond to that potential again, and so on (Section A.5). draws the chain and what follows from it.

Summing the chain into one end of the bubble takes three steps.

  1. Write the chain: $\chi=\chi^{\mathrm{irr}}+\chi^{\mathrm{irr}}v\chi^{\mathrm{irr}}+\chi^{\mathrm{irr}}v\chi^{\mathrm{irr}}v\chi^{\mathrm{irr}}+\dots$

    Products are integrals over intermediate positions. $\chi^{\mathrm{irr}}$ contains every process that cannot be cut in two by cutting one Coulomb line, so each term of the chain is distinct and none is missing.

  2. Sum it: $\chi=\chi^{\mathrm{irr}}\big(1-v\chi^{\mathrm{irr}}\big)^{-1}=\chi^{\mathrm{irr}}(\varepsilon^{\rme})^{-1}$.

    A geometric series again, with the electronic dielectric function $\varepsilon^{\rme}=1-v\chi^{\mathrm{irr}}$ of Section A.5. As for the phonon, the series converges only where $v\chi^{\mathrm{irr}}$ is small, which fails in a metal at small $q$, where $v\chi^{\mathrm{irr}}=1-\varepsilon^{\rme}\approx-k_{\mathrm{TF}}^2/q^2$; the closed form, the solution of the Dyson equation $\chi=\chi^{\mathrm{irr}}+\chi^{\mathrm{irr}}v\chi$, holds everywhere.

  3. Let $(\varepsilon^{\rme})^{-1}$ act on the vertex at the right end. This gives the screened vertex of Section A.5 ,

    $$\mathcal V_{\bq\nu}(\br,\omega)=\int\dd^3r'\,(\varepsilon^{\rme})^{-1}(\br,\br';\omega)\,\Delta_{\bq\nu}V^{\mathrm{en}}(\br').$$
    Applied to a bare potential, $(\varepsilon^{\rme})^{-1}$ gives that potential plus the Hartree potential of the electrons it induces: the screened potential of Section A.3. When the exchange–correlation potential is added as in DFT, the static value is DFPT's change of the Kohn–Sham potential, $\Delta_{\bq\nu}V^{\mathrm{KS}}$ .

The result is

$$\begin{gathered}\Pi^{\mathrm{ph,NA}}_{\nu\nu'}(\bq,\omega)=\frac{1}{N_\ell}\int\dd^3r\,\dd^3r'\\ \times\big[\Delta_{\bq\nu}V^{\mathrm{en}}(\br)\big]^*\,\chi^{\mathrm{irr}}(\br,\br';\omega)\\ \times\mathcal V_{\bq\nu'}(\br',\omega)\ -\ \big\{\omega\to0\big\}.\end{gathered} \tag{A6.15}$$
$\mathcal V_{\bq\nu}(\br,\omega)$
screened vertex: the potential change of mode $\bq\nu$ screened by the electrons at frequency $\omega$ (eV)
$\chi^{\mathrm{irr}}(\br,\br';\omega)$
irreducible density response of the electrons (eV⁻¹ Å⁻⁶)
$\{\omega\to0\}$
the same expression at zero frequency

One bare vertex, one screened vertex, the irreducible response between them, and the same at zero frequency subtracted. This is the review's central result for the phonon self-energy; Keating had obtained it before, and Scalapino for the electron gas.10

Two ways to count a process twice

The structure of avoids two kinds of double counting. The first concerns screening. Every chain with $n$ Coulomb lines appears once in $\chi$, and summing the chains into one end counts each of them once. Screening the other end as well would multiply two geometric series:

$$\begin{gathered}\sum_{a\ge0}\sum_{b\ge0}\chi^{\mathrm{irr}}\big(v\chi^{\mathrm{irr}}\big)^{a+b}\\ =\sum_{n\ge0}(n+1)\,\chi^{\mathrm{irr}}\big(v\chi^{\mathrm{irr}}\big)^n.\end{gathered} \tag{A6.16}$$

The pairs $(a,b)$ with $a+b=n$ number $n+1$, so a chain with $n$ Coulomb lines would be counted $n+1$ times.

In a uniform electron gas, where every factor is a number at each $\bq$, two screened vertices give $\chi^{\mathrm{irr}}/(\varepsilon^{\rme})^2$ instead of $\chi^{\mathrm{irr}}/\varepsilon^{\rme}$. The result is too small by the factor $\varepsilon^{\rme}$, which in a metal at small $q$ is about $k_{\mathrm{TF}}^2/q^2\gg1$, where $k_{\mathrm{TF}}$, the Thomas–Fermi wavevector, is the inverse screening length of the metal (1.5 Å⁻¹ in sodium). Two bare vertices keep only $n=0$ and are too large by the same factor. The electron self-energy of Section A.7 is different: there the phonon line is internal, each of its ends attaches to an electron line through a chain of its own, and both vertices are screened.

The second double counting concerns the static response. The DFPT reference already contains $\chi(0)$, through the first term of . A self-energy built with $\chi(\omega)$ alone would count the static softening twice; subtracting $\{\omega\to0\}$ prevents it. The EPP manuscript states the same rule: when the starting phonons come from DFT, only the dynamic part of the electronic bubbles may be added.11

The working formula

Five approximations turn into a sum over Kohn–Sham states, the form that codes evaluate. The review lists them:

  1. the vertex $\Lambda$ of Hedin's equations (Section A.5) is set to 1, as in the GW method;
  2. the interacting electron propagator $G$ is replaced by the Kohn–Sham one at the equilibrium positions, so that $\chi^{\mathrm{irr}}$ becomes the independent-particle response $\chi^0$ of Section A.3;
  3. the screening $(\varepsilon^{\rme})^{-1}$ is that of DFT, the random-phase approximation plus exchange and correlation, so that the static screened vertex is DFPT's, $\mathcal V_{\bq\nu}(\br,0)=\Delta_{\bq\nu}V^{\mathrm{KS}}(\br)$;
  4. the frequency dependence of the screened vertex is dropped, $\mathcal V_{\bq\nu}(\br,\omega)\simeq\Delta_{\bq\nu}V^{\mathrm{KS}}(\br)$, an approximation that is "ubiquitous in the literature but it is never mentioned explicitly", in the review's words;
  5. the electrons are spin degenerate and the crystal has time-reversal symmetry.

  1. Insert $\chi^0$ as a sum over Kohn–Sham states, the static form with $\hbar(\omega+\ii\eta)$ added to the energy denominator for a perturbation at frequency $\omega$:

    $$\begin{gathered}\chi^0(\br,\br';\omega)=2\sum_{n\bk,\,m\bk'}\frac{f_{n\bk}-f_{m\bk'}}{E_{n\bk}-E_{m\bk'}+\hbar(\omega+\ii\eta)}\\ \times\psi^*_{n\bk}(\br)\,\psi_{m\bk'}(\br)\\ \times\psi^*_{m\bk'}(\br')\,\psi_{n\bk}(\br').\end{gathered}$$
    Independent electrons respond by making transitions $n\bk\to m\bk'$ between Bloch states $\psi_{n\bk}$ with occupations $f_{n\bk}$ (Section A.2). The factor 2 counts the two spin directions (approximation 5).

  2. Do the integrals over $\br$ and $\br'$. They are the matrix elements of the two vertices between Bloch states, and they vanish unless $\bk'=\bk+\bq$.

    The vertex of mode $\bq\nu$ carries crystal momentum $\hbar\bq$ (Section A.2). The screened integral is the matrix element $g_{mn\nu}(\bk,\bq)$ of , because $\mathcal V_{\bq\nu}=\Delta_{\bq\nu}V^{\mathrm{KS}}$ (approximations 3 and 4). The bare integral is the same with $V^{\mathrm{KS}}$ replaced by $V^{\mathrm{en}}$, and its complex conjugate appears.

  3. Collect the terms, label the pairs by their initial state $n\bk$ and final state $m\,\bk+\bq$, and subtract the same at $\omega=0$:

    $$\begin{gathered}\Pi^{\mathrm{ph,NA}}_{\nu\nu'}(\bq,\omega)=\frac{2}{N_\ell}\sum_{\bk}\sum_{mn}\\ \times\big[g^{\mathrm b}_{mn\nu}(\bk,\bq)\big]^*g_{mn\nu'}(\bk,\bq)\\ \bigg[\frac{f_{m\bk+\bq}-f_{n\bk}}{E_{m\bk+\bq}-E_{n\bk}-\hbar(\omega+\ii\eta)}\\ -\frac{f_{m\bk+\bq}-f_{n\bk}}{E_{m\bk+\bq}-E_{n\bk}}\bigg].\end{gathered} \tag{A6.17}$$
    For the pair $n\bk\to m\,\bk+\bq$ the weight of step 1 is $(f_{n\bk}-f_{m\bk+\bq})/(E_{n\bk}-E_{m\bk+\bq}+\hbar\omega+\ii\hbar\eta)$, which becomes the first term of the bracket when numerator and denominator change sign.

$g_{mn\nu}(\bk,\bq)$

The linewidth: Fermi's golden rule

For a linewidth only the imaginary part of is needed. The static term is real, and $\mathrm{Im}\,[1/(x-\ii\eta)]=\pi\delta(x)$. With $g^{\mathrm b}\to g$ and ,

$$\begin{gathered}\gamma_{\bq\nu}=\frac{\pi}{\hbar}\,\frac{2}{N_\ell}\sum_{\bk mn}|g_{mn\nu}(\bk,\bq)|^2\\ \times\big(f_{n\bk}-f_{m\bk+\bq}\big)\\ \times\delta\big(E_{m\bk+\bq}-E_{n\bk}-\hbar\omega_{\bq\nu}\big).\end{gathered} \tag{A6.18}$$

This is Fermi's golden rule. The phonon is absorbed by an electron that jumps from $n\bk$ to $m\,\bk+\bq$, the delta function conserves energy, and the occupation factor counts absorption minus stimulated emission. By , $1/\tau^{\ph}_{\bq\nu}=2\gamma_{\bq\nu}$.13 In metals the phonon energy is small compared with the electronic energy scales. Dropping it inside the delta function, and using $f(E)-f(E+\hbar\omega)\simeq\hbar\omega\,\delta(E-E_{\mathrm F})$ at low temperature, where $f(E)$ is the occupation of a state of energy $E$ and $E_{\mathrm F}$ the Fermi energy, gives Allen's formula:

$$\begin{gathered}\frac{\gamma_{\bq\nu}}{\pi\omega_{\bq\nu}}\simeq\frac{2}{N_\ell}\sum_{\bk mn}|g_{mn\nu}(\bk,\bq)|^2\\ \times\delta\big(E_{n\bk}-E_{\mathrm F}\big)\,\delta\big(E_{m\bk+\bq}-E_{\mathrm F}\big).\end{gathered} \tag{A6.19}$$

The review calls it "a sort of adiabatic approximation to the nonadiabatic theory". It is positive by construction and easy to converge, but it loses the temperature dependence and any structure on the scale of a phonon energy. The two delta functions count pairs of points on the Fermi surface separated by $\bq$, which draws.14

Bare or screened: a factor $\varepsilon^{\rme}$ in metals

Equation pairs a bare vertex with a screened one, while uses two screened vertices. In a metal at small $q$ the two differ by the factor $\varepsilon^{\rme}(\bq,0)\approx k_{\mathrm{TF}}^2/q^2\gg1$. In the uniform electron gas the exact imaginary part of can be evaluated (derivation below): it is the irreducible response times two vertices, both screened at the phonon frequency. The common practice of is therefore right whenever $\varepsilon^{\rme}(\omega_{\bq\nu})\approx\varepsilon^{\rme}(0)$. Pairing the bare vertex with a statically screened one, which is what approximation 4 does in , overestimates the width by about $\varepsilon^{\rme}(\bq,0)$. The bare–screened bookkeeping and the frequency dependence of the screened vertex must be treated together; the review flags the choice between the two forms as something "it will be important to assess".15

Derivation: the exact width of the uniform electron gas

In a uniform gas every quantity is a number for each $\bq$. Write $\varepsilon^{\rme}(\omega)=1-v\chi^{\mathrm{irr}}(\omega)$, the bare coupling $g^{\mathrm b}$ and the coupling screened at frequency $\omega$, $g(\omega)=g^{\mathrm b}/\varepsilon^{\rme}(\omega)$. The dynamic term of is $|g^{\mathrm b}|^2\chi^{\mathrm{irr}}/\varepsilon^{\rme}$, and

$$\mathrm{Im}\,\frac{\chi^{\mathrm{irr}}}{1-v\chi^{\mathrm{irr}}}=\frac{\mathrm{Im}\big[\chi^{\mathrm{irr}}\big(1-v\chi^{\mathrm{irr}*}\big)\big]}{|\varepsilon^{\rme}|^2}=\frac{\mathrm{Im}\,\chi^{\mathrm{irr}}}{|\varepsilon^{\rme}|^2},$$

because $v\chi^{\mathrm{irr}}\chi^{\mathrm{irr}*}$ is real. Hence $\mathrm{Im}\,\Pi^{\mathrm{ph,NA}}=|g^{\mathrm b}|^2\,\mathrm{Im}\,\chi^{\mathrm{irr}}/|\varepsilon^{\rme}(\omega)|^2=|g(\omega)|^2\,\mathrm{Im}\,\chi^{\mathrm{irr}}(\omega)$; the static term is real and drops out. With approximation 4 instead, the dynamic term is $|g^{\mathrm b}|^2\chi^{\mathrm{irr}}(\omega)/\varepsilon^{\rme}(0)$, whose imaginary part is $\varepsilon^{\rme}(0)\,|g(0)|^2\,\mathrm{Im}\,\chi^{\mathrm{irr}}(\omega)$. The cancellation between $\mathrm{Re}\,\chi^{\mathrm{irr}}\,\mathrm{Im}\,(\varepsilon^{\rme})^{-1}$ and $\mathrm{Im}\,\chi^{\mathrm{irr}}\,\mathrm{Re}\,(\varepsilon^{\rme})^{-1}$ is lost.

Which electron–hole pairs a phonon can create

Every formula above is a sum over electron–hole pairs $n\bk\to m\,\bk+\bq$, and their geometry decides where the self-energy is large. Take one parabolic band, $E_\bk=\hbar^2k^2/2m^*$, filled up to the Fermi wavevector $k_{\mathrm F}$, at zero temperature; this describes both the doped semiconductor and the electron gas below. A pair needs $\bk$ occupied, $|\bk|\lt k_{\mathrm F}$, and $\bk+\bq$ empty, $|\bk+\bq|\gt k_{\mathrm F}$, and it costs

$$E_{\bk+\bq}-E_\bk=\frac{\hbar^2}{m^*}\Big(\bk\cdot\bq+\frac{q^2}{2}\Big). \tag{A6.20}$$
$m^*$
effective mass of the band (in units of the electron mass $m_{\mathrm e}$)
$k_{\mathrm F}$
Fermi wavevector (Å⁻¹); the Fermi velocity is $v_{\mathrm F}=\hbar k_{\mathrm F}/m^*$ and the Fermi energy $E_{\mathrm F}=\hbar^2k_{\mathrm F}^2/2m^*$, measured from the bottom of the band

The cost depends only on the component of $\bk$ along $\bq$, so all pairs of one energy lie on a straight line perpendicular to $\bq$. draws the construction in two dimensions. Translate the Fermi disc by $\bq$: the translated disc holds the final states $\bk+\bq$ of all occupied states $\bk$. The part of it outside the Fermi disc is allowed by the Pauli principle; the overlap is blocked.

Three results follow from the picture.

  1. The pairs fill a band of energies, the electron–hole continuum:

    $$\begin{gathered}\hbar\omega^{\max}(q)=\tfrac{\hbar^2}{2m^*}(q^2+2k_{\mathrm F}q),\\ \hbar\omega^{\min}(q)=\max\Big[0,\\ \tfrac{\hbar^2}{2m^*}(q^2-2k_{\mathrm F}q)\Big].\end{gathered} \tag{A6.21}$$
    The most expensive pair starts at the front of the Fermi disc, $\bk=k_{\mathrm F}\hat\bq$. Pairs of zero energy exist while the translated circle crosses the Fermi circle, $q\lt2k_{\mathrm F}$; for larger $q$ the cheapest pair starts at the back, $\bk=-k_{\mathrm F}\hat\bq$. For small $q$, $\omega^{\max}\simeq v_{\mathrm F}q$: a phonon with $\omega_{\bq\nu}\gt\omega^{\max}(q)$ can create no pair, so it cannot decay, and the electrons cannot follow it.

  2. In two dimensions the imaginary part of the independent-particle response is set by the length of the line of resonant pairs inside the allowed region:

    $$\mathrm{Im}\,\chi^0(q,\omega)=-\frac{N_{\mathrm F}}{\OS}\,\frac{L_{\mathrm{res}}}{q}. \tag{A6.22}$$
    $\mathrm{Im}\,\chi^0$ is $-\pi$ times the number of pairs of energy $\hbar\omega$ per unit energy and area, with a factor 2 for spin and $(2\pi)^{-2}$ per unit area of $\bk$ space. The delta function of energy, $\delta\big(\hbar^2(q\,\bk\cdot\hat\bq+q^2/2)/m^*-\hbar\omega\big)$ $=(m^*/\hbar^2q)\,\delta(\bk\cdot\hat\bq-k^\star)$, puts $\bk$ on the line, and the remaining integral along the line gives its length. The factors combine into $m^*/2\pi\hbar^2$, the density of states per spin and unit area, $N_{\mathrm F}/\OS$, with $\OS$ the cell area.

  3. For a slow phonon, $\omega\ll v_{\mathrm F}q$ with $q\lt2k_{\mathrm F}$, the resonant line hugs the two points where the circles cross, and

    $$\begin{gathered}\mathrm{Im}\,\chi^0(q,\omega)\simeq-\frac{2N_{\mathrm F}}{\OS}\\ \times\frac{\omega}{v_{\mathrm F}q\sqrt{1-(q/2k_{\mathrm F})^2}}.\end{gathered} \tag{A6.23}$$
    On the line of initial states, $\bk\cdot\hat\bq=k^\star\simeq-q/2$ ( draws their final states $\bk+\bq$, on the parallel line at $k^\star+q\simeq q/2$), the allowed states satisfy $k_{\mathrm F}^2-2m^*\omega/\hbar\lt|\bk|^2\lt k_{\mathrm F}^2$. Near each crossing point this is a segment of length $(m^*\omega/\hbar)/(k_{\mathrm F}^2-q^2/4)^{1/2}$, and the two segments make up $L_{\mathrm{res}}$.

$N_{\mathrm F}$

As $\omega\to0$ the allowed part of the resonant line shrinks to the crossing points of the two circles: the pairs of zero energy. They exist for $q\lt2k_{\mathrm F}$, merge at $q=2k_{\mathrm F}$, where the circles touch and the pair takes an electron straight across the Fermi surface, and disappear beyond. At the merger the resonant line runs along the Fermi circle, which is why the length of resonant line per unit frequency diverges there, the square root in . The Lindhard function of the electron gas skip if familiar

For a uniform gas of independent electrons the response $\chi^0$ of Section A.3 depends only on $\bq$ and $\omega$. Per unit volume, with both spins, it is the Lindhard function

$$\begin{gathered}\chi^0(q,\omega)=\frac{2}{N_\ell\OV}\\ \times\sum_\bk\frac{f_\bk-f_{\bk+\bq}}{E_\bk-E_{\bk+\bq}+\hbar(\omega+\ii\eta)},\end{gathered}$$

where $N_\ell\OV$ is the volume of the supercell; for a sheet the cell area $\OS$ replaces $\OV$. It is negative at $\omega=0$. With $x=q/2k_{\mathrm F}$ its static values are

$$\begin{gathered}\text{3D:}\quad\chi^0(q,0)=-\frac{2N_{\mathrm F}}{\OV}\Big[\frac12\\ +\frac{1-x^2}{4x}\ln\Big|\frac{1+x}{1-x}\Big|\Big],\\ \text{2D:}\quad\chi^0(q,0)=-\frac{2N_{\mathrm F}}{\OS}\\ \times\begin{cases}1,&x\le1,\\1-\sqrt{1-1/x^2},&x\gt1.\end{cases}\end{gathered}$$

Both are continuous at $q=2k_{\mathrm F}$. In three dimensions the slope diverges logarithmically there; in two dimensions $\chi^0$ is constant up to $2k_{\mathrm F}$ and rises toward zero with infinite slope just above it; in one dimension $\chi^0$ itself diverges, which drives the Peierls instability. The fewer the dimensions, the larger the part of the Fermi surface that $2k_{\mathrm F}$ connects, and the stronger the singularity. Here $2N_{\mathrm F}/\OV$ is the density of states per unit volume, both spins, $m^*k_{\mathrm F}/\pi^2\hbar^2$: the quantity that Section A.3 calls $N_{\mathrm F}$. At small $q$ the three-dimensional static value tends to $-2N_{\mathrm F}/\OV$, and $\varepsilon^{\rme}=1-v\chi^0=1+k_{\mathrm{TF}}^2/q^2$ with $k_{\mathrm{TF}}^2=8\pi e^2N_{\mathrm F}/\OV$: the Thomas–Fermi screening of Section A.3. Above the continuum the response is positive and small, $\chi^0\simeq n_{\mathrm e}q^2/m^*\omega^2$, where $n_{\mathrm e}$ is the electron density. Inside the continuum $\chi^0$ has the imaginary part .

A doped two-dimensional semiconductor: the nonadiabatic window

The simplest system in which the adiabatic approximation fails is a semiconductor sheet doped with free electrons. The model has one parabolic conduction band with effective mass $m^*=0.5\,m_{\mathrm e}$, one valley and two spin directions, holding $n_{\mathrm e}$ electrons per unit area, so that $k_{\mathrm F}=(2\pi n_{\mathrm e})^{1/2}$. One optical phonon has the same energy $\hbar\omega_0$ at every $\bq$; $\omega_0$ is the DFPT frequency, which already contains $\Pi^{\mathrm{ph,A}}$. The phonon couples to the electrons within the band through a constant matrix element $g$, following the common practice $g^{\mathrm b}\to g$. At zero temperature becomes16

$$\begin{gathered}\Pi^{\mathrm{ph,NA}}(q,\omega)\\ =|g|^2\,\OS\,\big[\chi^0(q,\omega)-\chi^0(q,0)\big].\end{gathered} \tag{A6.24}$$

With one band and a constant $g$, the sum $\frac{2}{N_\ell}\sum_\bk$ of the bracket is $\OS$ times the Lindhard function per unit area of the box above, since $\frac1{N_\ell}\sum_\bk=\OS\int\dd^2k/(2\pi)^2$.

The static value is $\chi^0(q,0)=-2N_{\mathrm F}/\OS$ for $q\le2k_{\mathrm F}$, and the behaviour of follows from the geometry of the pairs.

  • Above the continuum ($\omega_0\gt\omega^{\max}(q)$, small $q$): $\chi^0(q,\omega_0)\simeq n_{\mathrm e}q^2/m^*\omega_0^2=(v_{\mathrm F}q/\omega_0)^2\,N_{\mathrm F}/\OS$ is small, the static term survives, and
    $$\hbar\Delta\omega_{\bq}\simeq2N_{\mathrm F}|g|^2,\qquad\gamma_{\bq}=0. \tag{A6.25}$$
    The phonon is hardened and undamped. The electrons cannot follow it, so the softening by the Fermi sea that the static calculation built into $\omega_0$ is undone.
  • Inside the continuum, with $\omega_0\ll v_{\mathrm F}q$: $\chi^0(q,\omega_0)\simeq\chi^0(q,0)+\ii\,\mathrm{Im}\,\chi^0$, so the shift vanishes and gives the width
    $$\begin{gathered}\hbar\gamma_{\bq}\simeq2N_{\mathrm F}|g|^2\\ \times\frac{\omega_0}{v_{\mathrm F}q\sqrt{1-(q/2k_{\mathrm F})^2}}.\end{gathered} \tag{A6.26}$$
    The phonon decays into electron–hole pairs, a process called Landau damping, and its frequency returns to the adiabatic value. The ratio $\omega_0/v_{\mathrm F}q$, the speed of the phonon over the speed of the electrons, controls the width.
  • Below the continuum ($q\gt2k_{\mathrm F}$ and $\omega_0\lt\omega^{\min}(q)$): every pair costs more than $\hbar\omega_0$, the dynamic and static responses nearly agree, and the phonon is nearly free again.

The stiffening is the model's version of a general result used by Saitta and coworkers. At $\bq\to0$, replace $g^{\mathrm b}$ by $g$ and keep only the transitions within one band, $m=n$. At exactly $\bq=0$ the dynamic term of the bracket of vanishes, because $f_{n\bk}-f_{n\bk}=0$, while the subtracted static term, taken as $\bq\to0$, contributes $-\partial f/\partial E$, which is $\delta(E_{n\bk}-E_{\mathrm F})$ at zero temperature. Hence

$$\hbar\Delta\omega_{\bq\to0,\nu}\simeq2N_{\mathrm F}\,\big\langle|g_{nn\nu}(\bk,\mathbf 0)|^2\big\rangle_{\mathrm{FS}}, \tag{A6.27}$$
$\langle\cdots\rangle_{\mathrm{FS}}$
average over the Fermi surface, $\sum_{n\bk}(\cdots)\,\delta(E_{n\bk}-E_{\mathrm F})\big/\sum_{n\bk}\delta(E_{n\bk}-E_{\mathrm F})$

where the factor 2 counts the two spin directions, as in , and $N_{\mathrm F}$ is per spin.17 The shift is positive: at $\bq\to0$ the dynamic phonon is stiffer than the adiabatic one.

Near the upper edge of the continuum, first order and the full pole equation differ strongly. The hardened pole can sit just above the edge and stay sharp, while the first-order formula, which evaluates $\Pi^{\mathrm{ph,NA}}$ at $\omega_0$, already samples the continuum. As the doping grows and $E_{\mathrm F}\gg\hbar\omega_0$, the window in which the phonon outruns the electrons shrinks toward $q\lesssim\omega_0/v_{\mathrm F}$. Two effects of the general list are absent from the model: mode mixing, since there is one mode, and new poles, since the response $\chi^0$ contains no Coulomb chain and therefore no plasmon.

Physical picture: two limits that do not commute

At $\bq\to0$ the intraband part of depends on the order of the limits. Taking $\omega\to0$ first gives the static term: the Fermi sea rearranges to follow a slow distortion, which softens the phonon, and DFPT includes this softening. Taking $\bq\to0$ first at finite $\omega$ removes the dynamic term: a uniform distortion faster than the electrons cannot rearrange the Fermi sea. The nonadiabatic self-energy is the difference between the two orders, and it is positive. In it is the hardening by $2N_{\mathrm F}|g|^2$ at small $q$.

Where nonadiabatic phonons have been seen

The first first-principles calculations of nonadiabatic frequencies, by Lazzeri and Mauri and by Pisana and coworkers, concerned the Raman G band of graphene: the in-plane optical phonon at $\bq=0$, with $\hbar\omega=196$ meV (1585 cm⁻¹). In graphene the Dirac cones allow transitions with $\bq=0$ and energies comparable to the phonon, so the adiabatic condition fails. As a gate adds electrons or holes, the measured frequency rises by about 5 cm⁻¹ for a few $10^{12}$ cm⁻². The adiabatic DFPT frequency stays flat, while the nonadiabatic calculation follows the data. Metallic carbon nanotubes behave the same way. This is a nonadiabatic Kohn anomaly: it sits at small $\bq$, where the two limits of the callout above differ, unlike the adiabatic anomaly at $2k_{\mathrm F}$ in the last subsection.18

Corrections are typically 1% of the frequency, but they can be large. In graphite intercalation compounds Saitta and coworkers found up to about 300 cm⁻¹: in KC₈ the adiabatic G-band frequency of about 1230 cm⁻¹ moves to about 1540 cm⁻¹, close to the measured 1520 cm⁻¹ (values read from the review's figure). In Mg and Ti the correction is a few cm⁻¹. Calandra, Profeta and Mauri evaluated $\Pi^{\mathrm{ph,NA}}$ over the whole Brillouin zone of CaC₆ and found up to 7%, concentrated near $\Gamma$. In MgB₂ the calculation overshoots, 761 cm⁻¹ against a measured 600 cm⁻¹. Finite electron lifetimes, absent from the Kohn–Sham propagator of approximation 2, are thought to wash out part of the effect: electrons that scatter within a phonon period partly follow the lattice. In heavily boron-doped diamond, a superconductor, inelastic x-ray scattering found optical phonons near $\Gamma$ that DFPT could not reproduce and the nonadiabatic self-energy could.

Linewidths are routinely computed with Allen's formula . The review quotes values from about 1 meV in niobium to about 30 meV for the optical mode of MgB₂ that drives its superconductivity, in reasonable agreement with neutron and Raman data. In a semiconductor without free carriers no electron–hole pair costs as little as a phonon, so gives no width. There the decay of an optical phonon into two phonons, through the third-order terms of the energy that Section A.1 dropped, dominates.

The electron gas: adiabatic phonons, sound and the Kohn anomaly

The simplest adiabatic self-energy belongs to point ions of valence $Z$ and mass $M$ in an electron gas, with one ion per volume $\OV$ and electron density $n_{\mathrm e}=Z/\OV$. Treat the ions as a uniform fluid without a lattice, and take as the reference the ions in a rigid uniform negative background: the electron gas before it responds.

  1. In the reference, a longitudinal wave of displacements leaves a charge imbalance whose field pulls the ions back, as in a plasma. Every ion feels the same spring $K=M(\omega^{\ion}_{\mathrm p})^2$ at every $q$, with

    $$\big(\omega^{\ion}_{\mathrm p}\big)^2=\frac{4\pi Z^2e^2}{M\OV}. \tag{A6.28}$$
    Displacements $\bu$ along $\bq$ change the ion charge density by $-Ze\,\nabla\cdot\bu/\OV$. Gauss's law then gives a field $-4\pi Ze\,\bu/\OV$, and the force on an ion of charge $Ze$ is $-(4\pi Z^2e^2/\OV)\,\bu$: the ionic plasma frequency of Section A.5.

  2. The electrons screen the force between the ions, so the spring becomes $K/\varepsilon^{\rme}(q,\omega)$.

    Two ions interact through the electron-screened interaction $W^{\rme}=v/\varepsilon^{\rme}$ (Section A.5), and the force constant is proportional to that interaction.

  3. By , with the spring correction $\delta K=K\,[1/\varepsilon^{\rme}-1]$, the self-energy is

    $$\begin{gathered}\Pi^{\ph}(q,\omega)\\ =\frac{\hbar\omega^{\ion}_{\mathrm p}}{2}\Big[\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{\rme}(q,\omega)}-1\Big].\end{gathered} \tag{A6.29}$$
    $\hbar\,\delta K/2M\omega^{\ion}_{\mathrm p}$ with $K=M(\omega^{\ion}_{\mathrm p})^2$.

  4. Its static part is not small, about $-\hbar\omega^{\ion}_{\mathrm p}/2$ at small $q$, but it does not depend on frequency, so the pole equation is solved exactly:

    $$\omega_{\mathrm{LA}}(q)^2=\frac{\big(\omega^{\ion}_{\mathrm p}\big)^2}{\varepsilon^{\rme}(q,0)}. \tag{A6.30}$$
    $(\omega^{\ion}_{\mathrm p})^2+(2\omega^{\ion}_{\mathrm p}/\hbar)\,\Pi^{\mathrm{ph,A}}=(\omega^{\ion}_{\mathrm p})^2/\varepsilon^{\rme}(q,0)$.

The adiabatic phonon of the electron gas, the longitudinal acoustic phonon $\omega_{\mathrm{LA}}(q)$, is the bare ionic plasma oscillation with its spring weakened by the static dielectric function: the result of Bardeen and Pines, which Section A.5 also found as a pole of the screened interaction. With Thomas–Fermi screening, $\varepsilon^{\rme}=1+k_{\mathrm{TF}}^2/q^2$, the dispersion is $\omega_{\mathrm{LA}}(q)^2=(\omega^{\ion}_{\mathrm p})^2q^2/(q^2+k_{\mathrm{TF}}^2)$, which is sound at small $q$:

$$\begin{gathered}\omega_{\mathrm{LA}}(q)\simeq v_{\mathrm s}q,\\ v_{\mathrm s}=\frac{\omega^{\ion}_{\mathrm p}}{k_{\mathrm{TF}}}=v_{\mathrm F}\Big(\frac{Zm_{\mathrm e}}{3M}\Big)^{1/2}.\end{gathered} \tag{A6.31}$$

For free electrons in three dimensions $N_{\mathrm F}/\OV=3n_{\mathrm e}/4E_{\mathrm F}$ per spin, so $k_{\mathrm{TF}}^2=8\pi e^2N_{\mathrm F}/\OV=6\pi n_{\mathrm e}e^2/E_{\mathrm F}$. With $n_{\mathrm e}=Z/\OV$ and $E_{\mathrm F}=m_{\mathrm e}v_{\mathrm F}^2/2$, the ratio is $v_{\mathrm s}^2=2ZE_{\mathrm F}/3M$.

This is the Bohm–Staver sound velocity; $v_{\mathrm s}$ is the slope of the acoustic branch, as in Section A.1. Sodium has $Z=1$, $M=22.99$ amu and the density parameter $r_{\mathrm s}=3.93$, where $r_{\mathrm s}$ is the radius of the sphere that holds one electron, in units of the Bohr radius $a_0=0.529$ Å. Then $\hbar\omega^{\ion}_{\mathrm p}=29.6$ meV, $k_{\mathrm F}=0.92$ Å⁻¹, $k_{\mathrm{TF}}=1.49$ Å⁻¹ and $v_{\mathrm s}\approx3.0$ km/s.19

With the Lindhard function of the box above, the static dielectric function of the electron gas is the one in Bardeen's vertex :

$$\begin{gathered}\varepsilon^{\rme}(q,0)=1+\frac{k_{\mathrm{TF}}^2}{q^2}\Big[\frac12\\ +\frac{1-x^2}{4x}\ln\Big|\frac{1+x}{1-x}\Big|\Big],\quad x=\frac{q}{2k_{\mathrm F}}.\end{gathered} \tag{A6.32}$$

It is continuous at $q=2k_{\mathrm F}$, but its slope is not. Differentiating gives

$$\begin{gathered}\frac{\dd\omega_{\mathrm{LA}}}{\dd q}=-\frac{\omega_{\mathrm{LA}}}{2\varepsilon^{\rme}}\,\frac{\dd\varepsilon^{\rme}}{\dd q},\\ \frac{\dd\varepsilon^{\rme}(q,0)}{\dd q}\simeq\frac{k_{\mathrm{TF}}^2}{16k_{\mathrm F}^3}\ln\Big|1-\frac{q}{2k_{\mathrm F}}\Big|\\ (q\to2k_{\mathrm F}).\end{gathered} \tag{A6.33}$$

The derivative of the bracket of with respect to $x$ is $\frac1{2x}-\frac{1+x^2}{4x^2}\ln\big|\frac{1+x}{1-x}\big|$, which near $x=1$ is $\frac12\ln|1-x|$ plus a finite constant. With $\dd x/\dd q=1/2k_{\mathrm F}$ and $k_{\mathrm{TF}}^2/q^2=k_{\mathrm{TF}}^2/4k_{\mathrm F}^2$ at $q=2k_{\mathrm F}$ this gives the logarithm; the remaining terms are finite.

The logarithm goes to minus infinity on both sides of $2k_{\mathrm F}$, so the group velocity $\dd\omega_{\mathrm{LA}}/\dd q$ of the phonon has a logarithmic spike there, while $\omega_{\mathrm{LA}}(q)$ itself shows only a barely visible kink. This is the Kohn anomaly (Kohn, 1959). shows it for sodium, potassium and aluminium.

The geometric reason is the one of . Static screening at wavevector $q$ is built from electron–hole pairs, each weighted by the inverse of its energy, so the pairs of nearly zero energy count most. They exist only for $q\lt2k_{\mathrm F}$, where the Fermi sphere and its copy shifted by $\bq$ cross, and they end abruptly at $q=2k_{\mathrm F}$, where the two spheres touch. The abrupt end leaves a singularity in the $q$ dependence of the screening, and the phonon inherits it. In real space the same singularity appears as the Friedel oscillations, proportional to $\cos2k_{\mathrm F}r$, of the screening cloud around an ion.

The Kohn anomaly is an adiabatic feature: it comes from the static response, and DFPT reproduces it on fine enough $\bk$ grids, while smearing of the occupations blunts it. Nonadiabatic corrections are small here. For sound, the ratio of the speed of the phonon to that of the electrons is $v_{\mathrm s}/v_{\mathrm F}=(Zm_{\mathrm e}/3M)^{1/2}$, between $10^{-3}$ and $10^{-2}$; this is Migdal's small parameter. The shift is of second order in this ratio and the width of first order (derivation below):

$$\frac{\gamma_{\bq}}{\omega_{\mathrm{LA}}(q)}\simeq\frac{\pi}{4}\,\frac{v_{\mathrm s}}{v_{\mathrm F}}\qquad(q\ll k_{\mathrm{TF}}), \tag{A6.34}$$

which is $2.2\times10^{-3}$ for sodium. In a crystal the anomaly appears wherever $|\bq+\bG|=2k_{\mathrm F}$ for some reciprocal lattice vector $\bG$, so it is folded back into the Brillouin zone. In the ion fluid with $Z=1$, $2k_{\mathrm F}$ even lies outside the Debye sphere, the sphere with the volume of the Brillouin zone, whose radius is $q_{\mathrm D}=(2/Z)^{1/3}k_{\mathrm F}$. Kohn anomalies were seen by neutron scattering in lead, and graphite has them at $\Gamma$ and K in its highest optical branch.20

Derivation: Landau damping of sound in the electron gas

For $\omega\ll v_{\mathrm F}q$ and $q\ll k_{\mathrm F}$ the three-dimensional Lindhard function is $\chi^0(q,\omega)\simeq-(2N_{\mathrm F}/\OV)\big[1+\ii\pi\omega/2v_{\mathrm F}q\big]$: the static value plus the imaginary part from the pairs of the continuum. Then $\varepsilon^{\rme}(q,\omega)=1+(k_{\mathrm{TF}}^2/q^2)\big[1+\ii\pi\omega/2v_{\mathrm F}q\big]$, and for $q\ll k_{\mathrm{TF}}$, $1/\varepsilon^{\rme}\simeq(q^2/k_{\mathrm{TF}}^2)\big[1-\ii\pi\omega/2v_{\mathrm F}q\big]$. The pole equation with the full self-energy reads $\omega^2=(\omega^{\ion}_{\mathrm p})^2/\varepsilon^{\rme}(q,\omega)\simeq v_{\mathrm s}^2q^2\big[1-\ii\pi\omega/2v_{\mathrm F}q\big]$. Its root is $\omega\simeq v_{\mathrm s}q\big[1-\ii\pi v_{\mathrm s}/4v_{\mathrm F}\big]$, whose imaginary part is $-\gamma_\bq$; this is . The real part of $\chi^0(q,\omega)-\chi^0(q,0)$ is of order $(\omega/v_{\mathrm F}q)^2$, which is why the shift is of second order.

Check yourself: three short questions

  1. A Raman line has a full width at half maximum of 4 cm⁻¹. What are $\hbar\gamma_{\bq\nu}$ and the phonon lifetime?

    Answer. 4 cm⁻¹ is 0.496 meV, so the half width is $\hbar\gamma_{\bq\nu}=0.248$ meV. By , $\tau^{\ph}_{\bq\nu}=\hbar/2\hbar\gamma_{\bq\nu}=0.658\ \text{meV ps}/0.496\ \text{meV}=1.3$ ps.

  2. Why is $\Pi^{\mathrm{ph,NA}}$ negligible for the optical phonons, $\hbar\omega=60$ meV, of an insulator with a gap of 1 eV?

    Answer. Every transition in costs at least the gap $E_{\mathrm g}\gg\hbar\omega$. A transition across the gap and its reverse together contribute $2/E_{\mathrm g}-2E_{\mathrm g}/(E_{\mathrm g}^2-\hbar^2\omega^2)\simeq-2\hbar^2\omega^2/E_{\mathrm g}^3$ to the bracket, smaller than their static term $2/E_{\mathrm g}$ by $(\hbar\omega/E_{\mathrm g})^2\approx0.004$. No pair can be created with energy $\hbar\omega\lt E_{\mathrm g}$, so there is no width either.

  3. In a metal at $q=0.1\,k_{\mathrm{TF}}$, by what factor would a static self-energy built with two screened vertices be wrong? And one built with two bare vertices?

    Answer. $\varepsilon^{\rme}\approx1+k_{\mathrm{TF}}^2/q^2=101$. By , two screened vertices give a result about 100 times too small, and two bare vertices one about 100 times too large.
