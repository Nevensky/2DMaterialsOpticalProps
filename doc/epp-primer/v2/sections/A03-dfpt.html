Density-functional perturbation theory: the vertex is screened

When an atom moves, the electrons move with it, and their charge cancels most of the potential change that the atom causes. This section computes what is left: the self-consistent change of the Kohn–Sham potential that enters the electron–phonon matrix element of Section A.2. It derives density-functional perturbation theory (DFPT) step by step, shows that its answer is the bare electron–ion perturbation divided by a static dielectric function, checks the result against Bardeen's screened vertex of a simple metal, and ends with the second-order, Debye–Waller, coupling.

What you need: Section A.1 for displacements, normal modes and the displacement operator ; Section A.2 for Bloch states, the Kohn–Sham potential, the change of a potential in a displacement wave , the matrix element , and the screening of an ion in a free-electron gas (Thomas–Fermi and Lindhard screening, the random-phase approximation, Bardeen's vertex ). From quantum mechanics, first-order perturbation theory; from electrostatics, Poisson's equation. The picture: electrons follow the ions

Move one atom of a crystal by a small amount. Its nucleus, or in a pseudopotential calculation its ion (the nucleus with its core electrons), carries its potential along, so the electron–nucleus potential energy $V^{\mathrm{en}}$ changes near the atom. On its own, this bare change would scatter electrons far too strongly: the rigid-ion models of Section A.2, which used it, overestimated the coupling and, in metals, made it diverge at long wavelengths. But the electrons do not stay put. Charge flows toward the regions that the displaced ion has made more attractive and away from the others. The rearranged charge creates its own potential, which opposes the bare change and cancels most of it: the electrons screen the perturbation, in the language of Section A.2.

What an electron finally feels is the bare change plus the potential of the rearranged charge, with all electrons in their new ground state. In density-functional theory this is the change of the Kohn–Sham potential energy $V^{\mathrm{KS}}=V^{\mathrm{en}}+V^{\mathrm H}+V^{\mathrm{xc}}$, the sum of the electron–nucleus, Hartree and exchange–correlation parts, and it is the object inside the matrix element $g_{mn\nu}(\bk,\bq)$ of . The induced charge both responds to the total potential and contributes to it, so the change has to be found self-consistently, like the ground state of Section A.2: the potential that drives the electrons must equal the potential that their response produces.2

The effect is large. Measure the field of an ion by the force it exerts on an electron, in eV/Å. Along the displacement this is also the change of the electron's potential energy per ångström that the ion moves. In sodium, at the Wigner–Seitz radius $R_{\mathrm{WS}}=2.1$ Å of Section A.2, the radius of the sphere that holds one ion's share of the electrons, the field is 3.3 eV/Å without screening. With the screening of the electron gas in the random-phase approximation of Section A.2 it is 0.46 eV/Å: 86 percent is cancelled. In silicon, a perturbation that varies slowly from cell to cell is reduced about twelvefold.

The simplest model: one ion in an electron gas

Section A.2 worked out the simplest case: one fixed ion of charge $Z_\kappa e$ in jellium, the model metal of free electrons with mean density $n_0$ in a uniform positive background, screened in the Thomas–Fermi approximation. Three ingredients give the answer. Where the potential energy of an electron is lowered, electrons flow in until their occupied levels again reach the common chemical potential $\mu$, so the local density rises by $(\partial n_0/\partial\mu)$ times the lowering, with

$$(\partial n_0/\partial\mu)=\frac{\partial n_0}{\partial\mu}=\frac{m_{\mathrm e}k_{\mathrm F}}{\pi^2\hbar^2}$$

the density of states per unit volume at the Fermi level, both spins, and $k_{\mathrm F}$ the Fermi wavevector, $n_0=k_{\mathrm F}^3/3\pi^2$. The induced density $n^{\mathrm{ind}}$ adds its Hartree potential energy, which obeys Poisson's equation and in Fourier space is $(4\pi e^2/q^2)\,n^{\mathrm{ind}}(\bq)$. And the electrons respond to the total potential energy $V^{\mathrm{scr}}_\kappa$, bare plus induced, so that $n^{\mathrm{ind}}=-(\partial n_0/\partial\mu)V^{\mathrm{scr}}_\kappa$ and $V^{\mathrm{scr}}_\kappa(\bq)=V_\kappa(\bq)-(4\pi e^2/q^2)(\partial n_0/\partial\mu)V^{\mathrm{scr}}_\kappa(\bq)$. With the bare ion potential $V_\kappa$, equal to $-Z_\kappa e^2/r$, the solution is a Coulomb potential cut off exponentially:

$$\begin{gathered}V^{\mathrm{scr}}_\kappa(\br)=-\frac{Z_\kappa e^2}{r}\,\ee^{-k_{\mathrm{TF}}r},\\ V^{\mathrm{scr}}_\kappa(\bq)=\frac{V_\kappa(\bq)}{\varepsilon^{\mathrm e}(q)},\\ \varepsilon^{\mathrm e}(q)=1+\frac{k_{\mathrm{TF}}^2}{q^2},\\ k_{\mathrm{TF}}^2=4\pi e^2(\partial n_0/\partial\mu) .\end{gathered} \tag{A3.1}$$

all-space Fourier transform of the ion's potential, $-4\pi Z_\kappa e^2/q^2$ for a bare ion (eV Å³), as in Section A.2
$\varepsilon^{\mathrm e}(q)$
static dielectric function of the electron gas, here in its Thomas–Fermi form, without exchange and correlation: the factor by which the Fourier component of wavevector $q$ is reduced
$k_{\mathrm{TF}}$

The length $1/k_{\mathrm{TF}}$ sets the size of the screening cloud. In sodium ($n_0=0.0265$ Å⁻³, one valence electron per ion) $k_{\mathrm F}=0.92$ Å⁻¹, $k_{\mathrm{TF}}=1.49$ Å⁻¹ and $1/k_{\mathrm{TF}}=0.67$ Å; in aluminium ($n_0=0.181$ Å⁻³, three valence electrons per ion) $k_{\mathrm{TF}}=2.05$ Å⁻¹ and $1/k_{\mathrm{TF}}=0.49$ Å. Both lengths are less than a fifth of the distance between neighbouring ions. Section A.2 also measures the density by the parameter $r_{\mathrm s}$, the radius of a sphere that holds one electron in units of the Bohr radius $a_0=\hbar^2/m_{\mathrm e}e^2=0.529$ Å, so $\frac{4\pi}3(r_sa_0)^3=1/n_0$: $r_{\mathrm s}=3.93$ for sodium and 2.07 for aluminium.3 The induced electrons, $n^{\mathrm{ind}}=Z_\kappa k_{\mathrm{TF}}^2\,\ee^{-k_{\mathrm{TF}}r}/4\pi r$, add up to exactly $Z_\kappa$: the screening is perfect. In Fourier space says the same thing. A component with $q\ll k_{\mathrm{TF}}$ is divided by about $(k_{\mathrm{TF}}/q)^2$, a large number, so slowly varying potentials are wiped out. The cloud is spread over a few screening lengths, though. The electrons it holds within the radius $r$ number $Z_\kappa\big[1-(1+k_{\mathrm{TF}}r)\,\ee^{-k_{\mathrm{TF}}r}\big]$: 26 percent of the ion's charge at $r=1/k_{\mathrm{TF}}$, half at $1.7/k_{\mathrm{TF}}$ and 90 percent at $3.9/k_{\mathrm{TF}}$.

Now displace the ion by a small vector $\bu$. In the electron gas the cloud follows the ion, and the potential changes by $-\bu\cdot\nabla V^{\mathrm{scr}}_\kappa$, a dipolar pattern. Along the displacement, the fraction of the bare field $Z_\kappa e^2/r^2$ that survives is

$$S(r)=\frac{\partial_rV^{\mathrm{scr}}_\kappa}{\partial_rV_\kappa}=\big(1+k_{\mathrm{TF}}r\big)\,\ee^{-k_{\mathrm{TF}}r} .$$

By Gauss's theorem $S(r)$ is also the fraction of the ion's charge that is not yet neutralized within the radius $r$. draws the displaced ion, its potential and its cloud, and compares the Thomas–Fermi model with the full response of free electrons, the Lindhard function derived later in this section, without and with exchange.

The Thomas–Fermi model contains the essence of screening but misses four things:

  • Quantum nonlocality. Electrons cannot follow potentials that vary over less than about half their wavelength. The full response of free electrons, the Lindhard function of Section A.2, screens components with $q\gtrsim2k_{\mathrm F}$ less, and it leaves weak ripples of period $\pi/k_{\mathrm F}$ around the ion.
  • Exchange and correlation. The electrons that respond avoid each other, and the electron that feels the result takes part in that avoidance.
  • The crystal. Real electrons occupy bands, and an insulator has no free carriers: it reduces a slowly varying potential by a finite factor, the dielectric constant (about 12 in silicon), instead of wiping it out.
  • Motion. The ion moves, and the cloud may lag behind it.

DFPT treats the first three within Kohn–Sham theory, exactly for a given exchange–correlation functional. It neglects the fourth: the cloud is assumed to follow the ion instantly, the adiabatic approximation of Section A.2, examined near the end of this section. The plan:

  1. write the change of $V^{\mathrm{KS}}$ caused by a displacement wave as three parts, and derive each;
  2. compute the density response from first-order perturbation theory, and turn it into the Sternheimer equation, which needs only occupied states;
  3. close the self-consistency loop, and read its solution as screening by a dielectric function;
  4. check the result on the electron gas, where Bardeen's vertex of Section A.2 returns;
  5. state when static screening is allowed;
  6. add the second-order (Debye–Waller) coupling.

The self-consistent change of the potential

Kohn–Sham theory in brief skip if familiar

Hohenberg and Kohn proved that the ground-state density $n(\br)$ of interacting electrons in a given external potential determines that potential, and with it every ground-state property, and that the ground-state energy is the minimum of a functional of the density. Kohn and Sham replaced the interacting electrons by independent ones with the same density. Their orbitals obey a one-electron Schrödinger equation, $H^{\mathrm{KS}}\psi_{n\bk}=E_{n\bk}\psi_{n\bk}$ with $H^{\mathrm{KS}}=-\hbar^2\nabla^2/2m_{\mathrm e}+V^{\mathrm{KS}}$, and the density is $n(\br)=2\sum_{n\bk}f_{n\bk}|\psi_{n\bk}(\br)|^2$. The potential energy $V^{\mathrm{KS}}$ contains the electron–nucleus part $V^{\mathrm{en}}$; the Hartree part $V^{\mathrm H}(\br)=\int\dd^3r'\,e^2n(\br')/|\br-\br'|$, the classical repulsion of the density; and the exchange–correlation part $V^{\mathrm{xc}}=\delta E^{\mathrm{xc}}/\delta n$, the functional derivative of the exchange–correlation energy $E^{\mathrm{xc}}[n]$, which contains everything else. In the local-density approximation (LDA), each small volume contributes the exchange–correlation energy that a uniform electron gas of the same density would have.

$V^{\mathrm H}$ and $V^{\mathrm{xc}}$ depend on the density, which depends on the orbitals, which depend on $V^{\mathrm{KS}}$. The equations are therefore solved in a self-consistent field (SCF) cycle: guess $n$, build $V^{\mathrm{KS}}$, solve for the orbitals, compute a new $n$, mix it with the old one, and repeat until $n$ stops changing. DFPT applies the same cycle to first-order changes.

Fix an atom $\kappa$, a direction $\alpha$ and a wavevector $\bq$, and displace every atom $\kappa$ of the crystal along $\alpha$ by $u_{\ell\kappa\alpha}=\xi\,\ee^{\ii\bq\cdot\bR_\ell}$, where $\xi$ is a small complex amplitude, leaving all other coordinates unchanged. To first order in $\xi$, any quantity $X$ (a potential, the density, an orbital) changes by $\xi\,\Delta X$, with

$$\Delta X=\partial_{\kappa\alpha,\bq}X=\sum_\ell\ee^{\ii\bq\cdot\bR_\ell}\,\frac{\partial X}{\partial u_{\ell\kappa\alpha}} . \tag{A3.2}$$

For a phonon mode the same symbol means $\Delta X=\Delta_{\bq\nu}X$, the change per unit phonon field, which is a fixed combination of the $\partial_{\kappa\alpha,\bq}X$ given by , in eV for a potential; every equation below holds for it unchanged. So $\Delta V^{\mathrm{KS}}$, $\Delta n$ and $\Delta\psi_{n\bk}$ are the first-order changes of the potential, of the density and of an orbital caused by the perturbation named, always a single wave.4

One wavevector at a time

For a quantity that is lattice periodic in the undisturbed crystal, such as a potential or the density, the change caused by a wave is itself a wave with the same wavevector:

  1. Translate the observation point by a lattice vector $\bR_{\ell'}$. Because the crystal is periodic, pushing atom $\ell\kappa$ and looking at $\br+\bR_{\ell'}$ is the same as pushing the atom that sits $\bR_{\ell'}$ further back, atom $\ell''\kappa$, and looking at $\br$: $\partial X(\br+\bR_{\ell'})/\partial u_{\ell\kappa\alpha}=\partial X(\br)/\partial u_{\ell''\kappa\alpha}$, with $\bR_{\ell''}=\bR_\ell-\bR_{\ell'}$.

    Shifting the whole picture, crystal, pushed atom and observation point, by $-\bR_{\ell'}$ maps the crystal onto itself.

  2. Insert this into and sum over $\ell''$ instead of $\ell$, using $\ee^{\ii\bq\cdot\bR_\ell}=\ee^{\ii\bq\cdot\bR_{\ell''}}\ee^{\ii\bq\cdot\bR_{\ell'}}$:

    $$\begin{gathered}\Delta X(\br+\bR_{\ell'})=\ee^{\ii\bq\cdot\bR_{\ell'}}\,\Delta X(\br),\\ \Delta X(\br)=\sum_{\bG}\Delta X(\bq+\bG)\\ \times\ee^{\ii(\bq+\bG)\cdot\br} .\end{gathered} \tag{A3.3}$$
    The first line is Bloch's condition with the wavevector $\bq$. It means that $\ee^{-\ii\bq\cdot\br}\Delta X(\br)$ is lattice periodic, and a lattice-periodic function is a Fourier series over the reciprocal lattice vectors $\bG$. This gives the second line, with $\Delta X(\bq+\bG)=\OV^{-1}\int_{\mathrm{cell}}\dd^3r\,\ee^{-\ii(\bq+\bG)\cdot\br}\,\Delta X(\br)$ and $\OV$ the cell volume.

Three consequences follow. (i) A potential change of this form connects an electron in $\psi_{n\bk}$ only to states at $\bk+\bq$, the selection rule of Section A.2, so $\Delta\psi_{n\bk}$ is built from states at $\bk+\bq$. (ii) The response to a wave at $\bq$ contains only the wavevectors $\bq+\bG$: in linear response, different $\bq$ never mix. (iii) Each $\bq$ is therefore a separate calculation, and since a Bloch function is fixed by its values in one cell, each calculation lives in one unit cell.

The alternative, the frozen-phonon method of Section A.2, displaces the atoms in a supercell over which the wave repeats, computes $V^{\mathrm{KS}}$ with an ordinary ground-state code, and takes finite differences. The cost of such a code grows as the cube of the number of electrons. A wave on a $4\times4\times4$ grid of wavevectors needs a supercell of 64 cells, which costs $64^3$ times as much per electron wavevector as one cell but needs 64 times fewer electron wavevectors: the net factor, $64^2\approx4000$, is what DFPT saves.5

The three parts

The Kohn–Sham potential has three parts, so its change has three too. Only the first is known before the calculation.

The bare part. The ions carry their potentials rigidly, $V^{\mathrm{en}}(\br)=\sum_{\ell\kappa'}V_{\kappa'}(\br-\btau_{\ell\kappa'}-\bu_{\ell\kappa'})$, with $\btau_{\ell\kappa}$ the equilibrium positions.

  1. Differentiate with respect to $u_{\ell\kappa\alpha}$ at equilibrium. Only the term of atom $\ell\kappa$ depends on it, and $\partial V^{\mathrm{en}}/\partial u_{\ell\kappa\alpha}=-\partial_\alpha V_\kappa(\br-\btau_{\ell\kappa})$, with $\partial_\alpha=\partial/\partial r_\alpha$.

    Moving the ion by $\bu$ shifts its potential, $V_\kappa(\br-\bu)\simeq V_\kappa(\br)-\bu\cdot\nabla V_\kappa(\br)$.

  2. Weight with the phases of and write the ion's potential as a Fourier integral, $V_\kappa(\br)=\int\frac{\dd^3q'}{(2\pi)^3}\,V_\kappa(\bq')\,\ee^{\ii\bq'\cdot\br}$:

    $$\begin{gathered}\Delta V^{\mathrm{en}}(\br)=-\int\frac{\dd^3q'}{(2\pi)^3}\,\ii q'_\alpha V_\kappa(\bq')\\ \times\ee^{\ii\bq'\cdot(\br-\btau_\kappa)}\\ \times\sum_\ell\ee^{\ii(\bq-\bq')\cdot\bR_\ell} .\end{gathered}$$
    The gradient brings down $\ii q'_\alpha$, and $\btau_{\ell\kappa}=\bR_\ell+\btau_\kappa$ splits the phase into a cell part and an atom part.

  3. In a large crystal the lattice sum is a comb of delta functions, $\sum_\ell\ee^{\ii(\bq-\bq')\cdot\bR_\ell}=\frac{(2\pi)^3}{\OV}\sum_\bG\delta^3(\bq'-\bq-\bG)$, which selects $\bq'=\bq+\bG$. Comparing with ,

    $$\begin{gathered}\Delta V^{\mathrm{en}}(\bq+\bG)=-\frac{\ii\,(\bq+\bG)_\alpha}{\OV}\\ \times V_\kappa(\bq+\bG)\,\ee^{-\ii(\bq+\bG)\cdot\btau_\kappa} .\end{gathered} \tag{A3.4}$$
    The phases add up only when $\bq-\bq'$ is a reciprocal lattice vector. This is the lattice sum of Section A.1, $N_\ell\,\delta_{\bq,\bG}$ on the Born–von Kármán grid, in the limit of many cells.

Each component is a gradient, $-\ii(\bq+\bG)_\alpha$, times the ion's form factor $V_\kappa(\bq+\bG)/\OV$ (its Fourier transform divided by the cell volume), times the phase $\ee^{-\ii(\bq+\bG)\cdot\btau_\kappa}$, a structure factor that places the atom inside its cell. Only the component of the displacement along $\bq+\bG$ couples. For a bare ion $V_\kappa(\bq)=-4\pi Z_\kappa e^2/q^2$, so the $\bG=0$ term grows as $1/q$: a long wave of displaced charges produces a long-range field. For a silicon sublattice displaced along $\bq$ ($Z_\kappa=4$, $\OV=40.0$ Å³) it is 181 eV/Å at $q=0.1$ Å⁻¹.

The Hartree part. The Hartree potential energy is the classical repulsion of the electron density,

$$\begin{gathered}V^{\mathrm H}(\br)=\int\dd^3r'\,v(\br-\br')\,n(\br'),\\ v(\br-\br')=\frac{e^2}{|\br-\br'|} ,\end{gathered}$$

where $v$ is the bare Coulomb interaction energy of two electrons and $n(\br)=2\sum_{n\bk}f_{n\bk}|\psi_{n\bk}(\br)|^2$ is the electron density, with a factor 2 for spin. It is linear in $n$, so $\Delta V^{\mathrm H}(\br)=\int\dd^3r'\,v(\br-\br')\,\Delta n(\br')$. In plane waves:

  1. Insert the expansion of $\Delta n$. Each plane wave passes through the integral unchanged apart from a factor: $\int\dd^3r'\,v(\br-\br')\,\ee^{\ii\bq'\cdot\br'}=v(\bq')\,\ee^{\ii\bq'\cdot\br}$, with $v(\bq')=\int\dd^3s\,\ee^{-\ii\bq'\cdot\mathbf s}\,e^2/s$.

    Substitute $\mathbf s=\br-\br'$: the integral then depends on $\br$ only through the factor $\ee^{\ii\bq'\cdot\br}$.

  2. Evaluate $v(\bq)$ with Poisson's equation, $\nabla^2(e^2/r)=-4\pi e^2\delta(\br)$. In Fourier space $\nabla^2\to-q^2$ and $\delta(\br)\to1$, so

    $$\begin{gathered}\Delta V^{\mathrm H}(\bq+\bG)\\ =v(\bq+\bG)\,\Delta n(\bq+\bG),\\ v(\bq)=\frac{4\pi e^2}{q^2} .\end{gathered} \tag{A3.5}$$
    $-q^2\,v(\bq)=-4\pi e^2$.

A density wave of wavevector $\bq$ creates a potential $4\pi e^2/q^2$ times its amplitude. Long waves cost a great deal of electrostatic energy, which is why the components with $\bG=0$ and small $q$ are screened most.

The exchange–correlation part. $V^{\mathrm{xc}}(\br)$ is a functional of the density: its value at $\br$ depends on the density at every point $\br'$. To first order, the chain rule $\delta y_i=\sum_j(\partial y_i/\partial x_j)\,\delta x_j$ applies with the point $\br'$ in place of the index $j$ and an integral in place of the sum:

$$\begin{gathered}\Delta V^{\mathrm{xc}}(\br)=\int\dd^3r'\,f^{\mathrm{xc}}(\br,\br')\,\Delta n(\br'),\\ f^{\mathrm{xc}}(\br,\br')=\frac{\delta V^{\mathrm{xc}}(\br)}{\delta n(\br')} ,\end{gathered} \tag{A3.6}$$

where $f^{\mathrm{xc}}$, the second functional derivative of the exchange–correlation energy $E^{\mathrm{xc}}[n]$, is the exchange–correlation kernel. Like $v(\br-\br')$ it is measured in eV, and its Fourier transform in eV Å³. In the local-density approximation (LDA), which gives each small volume the exchange–correlation energy of a uniform electron gas of the same density, it is local, $f^{\mathrm{xc}}(\br,\br')=\delta(\br-\br')\,\dd V^{\mathrm{xc}}/\dd n$ with $\dd V^{\mathrm{xc}}/\dd n$ (eV Å³) taken at the local density, so codes apply it in real space. An example shows its size. In the electron gas with exchange only, $V^{\mathrm{xc}}=-e^2k_{\mathrm F}/\pi$ with $k_{\mathrm F}=(3\pi^2n_0)^{1/3}$, so $\dd V^{\mathrm{xc}}/\dd n=-\pi e^2/k_{\mathrm F}^2$ and the Fourier transform of the kernel is this negative constant, $f^{\mathrm{xc}}(\bq)=-\pi e^2/k_{\mathrm F}^2$ at every $\bq$: the electrons avoid each other, so extra density repels an electron less than its Hartree potential alone says. Next to $v(\bq)$,

$$v(\bq)+f^{\mathrm{xc}}(\bq)=\frac{4\pi e^2}{q^2}\Big(1-\frac{q^2}{4k_{\mathrm F}^2}\Big) ,$$

so exchange matters only at short wavelengths, for $q$ of order $k_{\mathrm F}$. This kernel is a toy. It changes sign at $q=2k_{\mathrm F}$, where a local kernel is least reliable.

Collecting the three parts:

$$\begin{gathered}\Delta V^{\mathrm{KS}}=\Delta V^{\mathrm{en}}+\Delta V^{\mathrm H}+\Delta V^{\mathrm{xc}},\\ \Delta V^{\mathrm{en}}(\br)=-\sum_\ell\ee^{\ii\bq\cdot\bR_\ell}\\ \times\partial_\alpha V_\kappa(\br-\btau_{\ell\kappa}),\\ \Delta V^{\mathrm H}(\br)=\int\dd^3r'\,v(\br-\br')\,\Delta n(\br'),\\ \Delta V^{\mathrm{xc}}(\br)=\int\dd^3r'\,f^{\mathrm{xc}}(\br,\br')\,\Delta n(\br') .\end{gathered} \tag{A3.7}$$

The bare part is fixed by the ions. The other two need the density change $\Delta n$, and $\Delta n$ depends on $\Delta V^{\mathrm{KS}}$, the potential the electrons actually feel. The next two subsections close this circle.6

How the density responds

Take the potential change as given for the moment. The Kohn–Sham electrons are independent particles in $V^{\mathrm{KS}}$, so their response to a change of it follows from first-order perturbation theory, one orbital at a time. Consider first any small real change $\delta V(\br)$, and label the Kohn–Sham states $n\bk$ and $m\bk'$.

  1. First-order perturbation theory for the orbital $\psi_{n\bk}$, with band energies $E_{n\bk}$:

    $$\delta\psi_{n\bk}=\sum_{m\bk'}{}^{'}\,\psi_{m\bk'}\,\frac{\langle\psi_{m\bk'}|\delta V|\psi_{n\bk}\rangle}{E_{n\bk}-E_{m\bk'}} .$$
    The prime on the sum excludes the states with $E_{m\bk'}=E_{n\bk}$. In an insulator their mixing does not change the density, as step 3 shows; in a metal they are handled with the occupations.

  2. The density is $n(\br)=2\sum_{n\bk}f_{n\bk}|\psi_{n\bk}(\br)|^2$, with occupations $f_{n\bk}$. With the occupations held fixed,

    $$\begin{gathered}\delta n(\br)=2\sum_{n\bk}f_{n\bk}\big[\psi^*_{n\bk}(\br)\,\delta\psi_{n\bk}(\br)\\ +\delta\psi^*_{n\bk}(\br)\,\psi_{n\bk}(\br)\big] .\end{gathered}$$
    Product rule. Fixed occupations are exact in an insulator; in a metal the occupations change too, which supplies the excluded terms.7

  3. Insert step 1. In the second term, swap the names $n\bk$ and $m\bk'$ and use $\langle\psi_{m\bk'}|\delta V|\psi_{n\bk}\rangle^*=\langle\psi_{n\bk}|\delta V|\psi_{m\bk'}\rangle$. Both terms then carry the same product of orbitals and combine:

    $$\begin{gathered}\delta n(\br)=2\sum_{n\bk,m\bk'}\frac{f_{n\bk}-f_{m\bk'}}{E_{n\bk}-E_{m\bk'}}\\ \times\psi^*_{n\bk}(\br)\,\psi_{m\bk'}(\br)\,\langle\psi_{m\bk'}|\delta V|\psi_{n\bk}\rangle .\end{gathered}$$
    $\delta V$ is real, so its matrix is Hermitian, and the renamed second term carries $-f_{m\bk'}$ over $E_{n\bk}-E_{m\bk'}$. Pairs of states with equal occupation cancel: a transition between two occupied states does not change the density.

  4. Write the matrix element as an integral, $\int\dd^3r'\,\psi^*_{m\bk'}(\br')\,\delta V(\br')\,\psi_{n\bk}(\br')$. The density change becomes a linear map of the potential change, with a kernel built from the unperturbed states only. For the complex change $\Delta V^{\mathrm{KS}}$, apply the map to its real and imaginary parts separately:

    $$\begin{gathered}\Delta n(\br)\\ =\int\dd^3r'\,\chi^0(\br,\br')\,\Delta V^{\mathrm{KS}}(\br'),\\ \chi^0(\br,\br')\\ =2\sum_{n\bk,m\bk'}\frac{f_{n\bk}-f_{m\bk'}}{E_{n\bk}-E_{m\bk'}}\\ \times\psi^*_{n\bk}(\br)\,\psi_{m\bk'}(\br)\\ \times\psi^*_{m\bk'}(\br')\,\psi_{n\bk}(\br') .\end{gathered} \tag{A3.8}$$
    $\delta V$ was arbitrary, and a linear map acts on the real and imaginary parts independently.
$\chi^0(\br,\br')$
independent-particle density response: the density change at $\br$ per unit change of the potential energy at $\br'$, with the Hartree and exchange–correlation potentials held fixed (eV⁻¹ Å⁻⁶)
$f_{n\bk}$
occupation of $\psi_{n\bk}$; 1 or 0 in an insulator at zero temperature

In many-body language $\chi^0$ is the polarizability of Adler and Wiser, the object that GW and Bethe–Salpeter codes build from a sum over empty bands, there at finite frequency. Its sign is fixed. Since the lower of two states is the more occupied, $(f_{n\bk}-f_{m\bk'})/(E_{n\bk}-E_{m\bk'})\le0$, and for any potential change

$$\begin{gathered}\int\dd^3r\int\dd^3r'\,\Delta V^*(\br)\,\chi^0(\br,\br')\,\Delta V(\br')\\ =2\sum_{n\bk,m\bk'}\frac{f_{n\bk}-f_{m\bk'}}{E_{n\bk}-E_{m\bk'}}\,\big|\langle\psi_{m\bk'}|\Delta V|\psi_{n\bk}\rangle\big|^2\le0 .\end{gathered}$$

The static response is negative: electrons move into the regions where their potential energy is lowered. The Thomas–Fermi rule $n^{\mathrm{ind}}=-(\partial n_0/\partial\mu)V^{\mathrm{scr}}_\kappa$ is the local, long-wavelength form of for the electron gas.

For the Bloch perturbation of the selection rule keeps only $\bk'=\bk+\bq$:

$$\begin{gathered}\Delta n(\br)=2\sum_{nm\bk}\frac{f_{n\bk}-f_{m\bk+\bq}}{E_{n\bk}-E_{m\bk+\bq}}\,\psi^*_{n\bk}(\br)\,\psi_{m\bk+\bq}(\br)\\ \times\langle\psi_{m\bk+\bq}|\Delta V^{\mathrm{KS}}|\psi_{n\bk}\rangle ,\end{gathered}$$

and the plane-wave components of the two sides are linked by a matrix:

$$\begin{gathered}\Delta n(\bq+\bG)\\ =\sum_{\bG'}\chi^0_{\bG\bG'}(\bq)\,\Delta V^{\mathrm{KS}}(\bq+\bG'),\\ \chi^0_{\bG\bG'}(\bq)=\frac{2}{\OVSC}\sum_{nm\bk}\frac{f_{n\bk}-f_{m\bk+\bq}}{E_{n\bk}-E_{m\bk+\bq}}\\ \times\langle\psi_{n\bk}|\ee^{-\ii(\bq+\bG)\cdot\br}|\psi_{m\bk+\bq}\rangle\\ \times\langle\psi_{m\bk+\bq}|\ee^{\ii(\bq+\bG')\cdot\br}|\psi_{n\bk}\rangle .\end{gathered} \tag{A3.9}$$

The Sternheimer equation: occupied states only

In an insulator at zero temperature $f=1$ for the occupied (valence) states and $f=0$ for the empty (conduction) states, so only occupied–empty pairs survive in . Each is a virtual transition of an electron from an occupied to an empty state, one at $\bk$ and the other at $\bk+\bq$, weighted by the inverse of its energy cost. Virtual means that the transition is a term of the perturbation sum, not a real process: it need not conserve energy. Computed this way, $\chi^0$ needs the empty states, and many of them, because the sum converges slowly with their number. DFPT never lists them. The idea, due to Sternheimer, is to obtain the whole empty-state part of each orbital's change at once, as the solution of a linear equation. shows, for a model insulator, the components of $\Delta\psi_{n\bk}$ on every state at $\bk+\bq$, which of them the density needs, and why the equation that determines them can be made well behaved.

  1. Split the Bloch form of $\Delta n$ above into the terms with $n$ occupied and $m$ empty and those with $n$ empty and $m$ occupied. Time-reversal symmetry gives $\psi_{n,-\bk}=\psi^*_{n\bk}$ and $E_{n,-\bk}=E_{n\bk}$, and relabelling $\bk\to-\bk-\bq$ turns every term of the second group into a term of the first. So

    $$\begin{gathered}\Delta n(\br)=4\sum_{n\in\mathrm{occ}}\sum_{\bk}\\ \times\psi^*_{n\bk}(\br)\,\Delta\psi^{\perp}_{n\bk}(\br),\\ \Delta\psi^{\perp}_{n\bk}=\sum_{m\in\mathrm{emp}}\psi_{m\bk+\bq}\\ \times\frac{\langle\psi_{m\bk+\bq}|\Delta V^{\mathrm{KS}}|\psi_{n\bk}\rangle}{E_{n\bk}-E_{m\bk+\bq}} .\end{gathered} \tag{A3.10}$$
    A crystal without magnetic order or magnetic field is symmetric under time reversal. The two groups describe the same virtual transitions seen from either end. The factor 4 is 2 for spin times 2 for the two groups.

  2. $\Delta\psi^{\perp}_{n\bk}$ is the part of the first-order change $\Delta\psi_{n\bk}$ that lies in the empty states at $\bk+\bq$: $\Delta\psi^{\perp}_{n\bk}=\big(1-\mathcal P^{\mathrm{occ}}_{\bk+\bq}\big)\Delta\psi_{n\bk}$, where $\mathcal P^{\mathrm{occ}}_{\bk+\bq}=\sum_{m\in\mathrm{occ}}|\psi_{m\bk+\bq}\rangle\langle\psi_{m\bk+\bq}|$ projects onto the occupied states at $\bk+\bq$.

    By step 1 of the derivation of , $\Delta\psi_{n\bk}$ has components on all states at $\bk+\bq$. Those on occupied states cancel in pairs in the density (step 3 there), so they are dropped.

  3. Apply $H^{\mathrm{KS}}-E_{n\bk}$, with $H^{\mathrm{KS}}=-\hbar^2\nabla^2/2m_{\mathrm e}+V^{\mathrm{KS}}$ the Kohn–Sham Hamiltonian. Each term of $\Delta\psi^{\perp}_{n\bk}$ is an eigenstate with eigenvalue $E_{m\bk+\bq}$, so the energy denominators cancel:

    $$\begin{gathered}\big(H^{\mathrm{KS}}-E_{n\bk}\big)\Delta\psi^{\perp}_{n\bk}\\ =-\sum_{m\in\mathrm{emp}}\psi_{m\bk+\bq}\,\langle\psi_{m\bk+\bq}|\Delta V^{\mathrm{KS}}|\psi_{n\bk}\rangle\\ =-\big(1-\mathcal P^{\mathrm{occ}}_{\bk+\bq}\big)\,\Delta V^{\mathrm{KS}}\,\psi_{n\bk} .\end{gathered}$$
    $\Delta V^{\mathrm{KS}}\psi_{n\bk}$ has the wavevector $\bk+\bq$, so it is a combination of the states at $\bk+\bq$; removing its occupied part leaves exactly the sum over empty states.

  4. The operator $H^{\mathrm{KS}}-E_{n\bk}$ is singular when an occupied state at $\bk+\bq$ has the energy $E_{n\bk}$ (at $\bq=0$, $\psi_{n\bk}$ itself), and badly conditioned near such cases: an eigenvalue close to zero makes an iterative solver slow and amplifies its errors. Its troublesome directions are occupied states, which $\Delta\psi^{\perp}_{n\bk}$ does not contain. Adding $E_{\mathrm{shift}}\,\mathcal P^{\mathrm{occ}}_{\bk+\bq}$ to the operator therefore leaves the solution unchanged, and with $E_{\mathrm{shift}}$ larger than the width of the occupied bands it makes every eigenvalue positive. The result is the Sternheimer equation of DFPT:

    $$\begin{gathered}\big(H^{\mathrm{KS}}-E_{n\bk}+E_{\mathrm{shift}}\,\mathcal P^{\mathrm{occ}}_{\bk+\bq}\big)\\ \times\Delta\psi^{\perp}_{n\bk}\\ =-\big(1-\mathcal P^{\mathrm{occ}}_{\bk+\bq}\big)\,\Delta V^{\mathrm{KS}}\,\psi_{n\bk} .\end{gathered} \tag{A3.11}$$
    On the occupied states the shifted operator has the eigenvalues $E_{m\bk+\bq}-E_{n\bk}+E_{\mathrm{shift}}\gt0$; on the empty states, $E_{m\bk+\bq}-E_{n\bk}$, which is at least the band gap.

Equation holds for every occupied band $n$ and every $\bk$, and every ingredient of it refers to occupied states only: $\psi_{n\bk}$, $E_{n\bk}$ and the projector. The unknown $\Delta\psi^{\perp}_{n\bk}$ is found by an iterative linear solver that only applies $H^{\mathrm{KS}}$ to trial functions, so the empty states are never computed, although the equation contains all of them. Derivation: force constants from the same density response

  1. Split the Born–Oppenheimer energy $U$ into the electronic ground-state energy at clamped nuclei and the repulsion of the nuclei, $U=E^{\mathrm e}+E^{\mathrm{nn}}$. The electronic energy depends on the positions only through $V^{\mathrm{en}}$, and it is minimal with respect to the density, so

    $$\frac{\partial U}{\partial u_{\ell\kappa\alpha}}=\int\dd^3r\,n(\br)\,\frac{\partial V^{\mathrm{en}}(\br)}{\partial u_{\ell\kappa\alpha}}+\frac{\partial E^{\mathrm{nn}}}{\partial u_{\ell\kappa\alpha}} .$$
    Because the energy is stationary in $n$, the first-order change of $n$ does not change it, and only the explicit dependence through $V^{\mathrm{en}}$ remains (the Hellmann–Feynman theorem). Minus this derivative is the force on the atom.

  2. Differentiate once more, with respect to $u_{\ell'\kappa'\beta}$:

    $$\begin{gathered}\Phi_{\ell\kappa\alpha,\ell'\kappa'\beta}=\int\dd^3r\,\frac{\partial n(\br)}{\partial u_{\ell'\kappa'\beta}}\,\frac{\partial V^{\mathrm{en}}(\br)}{\partial u_{\ell\kappa\alpha}}\\ +\int\dd^3r\,n(\br)\,\frac{\partial^2V^{\mathrm{en}}(\br)}{\partial u_{\ell\kappa\alpha}\,\partial u_{\ell'\kappa'\beta}}\\ +\frac{\partial^2E^{\mathrm{nn}}}{\partial u_{\ell\kappa\alpha}\,\partial u_{\ell'\kappa'\beta}} .\end{gathered}$$
    Product rule on the first term of step 1. The last two terms need only the unperturbed density and the positions of the nuclei; the middle one is nonzero only for $\ell'\kappa'=\ell\kappa$.

  3. Set $\ell=0$, multiply by $\ee^{\ii\bq\cdot\bR_{\ell'}}$ and sum over $\ell'$, as in the definition of $\Phi_{\kappa\alpha,\kappa'\beta}(\bq)$. The first term becomes $\int\dd^3r\,\big[\partial V^{\mathrm{en}}(\br)/\partial u_{0\kappa\alpha}\big]\,\partial_{\kappa'\beta,\bq}n(\br)$: the bare potential change of one atom, weighted by the density change that DFPT computes for the wave of atoms $\kappa'$ along $\beta$.

    The sum over $\ell'$ with the phase is the operation applied to $n$. A first-order density change thus gives a second-order energy. This is an instance of the 2n+1 theorem of perturbation theory, by which the wavefunctions to first order already fix the energy up to third order.

Now write the loop in a compact form in which a product means an integral over the intermediate position, as in and (in plane waves, a matrix product over $\bG$). The density change is $\Delta n=\chi^0\Delta V^{\mathrm{KS}}$ and the induced potential is $(v+f^{\mathrm{xc}})\Delta n$, so self-consistency reads

$$\begin{gathered}\Delta V^{\mathrm{KS}}=\Delta V^{\mathrm{en}}\\ +\big(v+f^{\mathrm{xc}}\big)\,\chi^0\,\Delta V^{\mathrm{KS}} .\end{gathered} \tag{A3.12}$$

  1. Move the last term to the left: $\big[1-(v+f^{\mathrm{xc}})\chi^0\big]\Delta V^{\mathrm{KS}}=\Delta V^{\mathrm{en}}$, where $1$ stands for $\delta(\br-\br')$, or $\delta_{\bG\bG'}$ in plane waves.

    The equation is linear in $\Delta V^{\mathrm{KS}}$.

  2. Invert the bracket:

    $$\begin{gathered}\Delta V^{\mathrm{KS}}=\big(\varepsilon^{\mathrm e}\big)^{-1}\Delta V^{\mathrm{en}},\\ \varepsilon^{\mathrm e}=1-\big(v+f^{\mathrm{xc}}\big)\,\chi^0 .\end{gathered} \tag{A3.13}$$
    For a stable ground state the eigenvalues of $\varepsilon^{\mathrm e}$ are positive, so the inverse exists. Without $f^{\mathrm{xc}}$ they are all at least 1, because $v$ is positive and $\chi^0$ negative.

Equation is the central result of this section. With it says that the DFPT matrix element is the matrix element of the bare electron–ion perturbation screened by the static dielectric function of the electrons. Each factor has a meaning:

  • $\Delta V^{\mathrm{en}}$: the bare perturbation, what the electrons would feel if they did not respond (the rigid-ion vertex of Section A.2);
  • $\chi^0$: how independent Kohn–Sham electrons change their density in a given potential;
  • $v+f^{\mathrm{xc}}$: the potential energy that a given density change produces;
  • $(v+f^{\mathrm{xc}})\chi^0$: one round of response, from a potential to a density to a new potential;
  • $(\varepsilon^{\mathrm e})^{-1}$: all the rounds together.10

The last statement is literal. Iterating , starting from $\Delta V^{\mathrm{en}}$, generates the geometric series of the inverse:

$$\begin{gathered}\Delta V^{\mathrm{KS}}=\Delta V^{\mathrm{en}}\\ +\big(v+f^{\mathrm{xc}}\big)\chi^0\,\Delta V^{\mathrm{en}}\\ +\big[\big(v+f^{\mathrm{xc}}\big)\chi^0\big]^2\Delta V^{\mathrm{en}}+\dots\end{gathered} \tag{A3.14}$$

Physical picture: rounds of response

Each term of is one round. The bare field induces a density; the potential of that density induces more density; and so on. Since $\chi^0$ is negative and $v$ positive, each round opposes the one before, and the terms alternate in sign. When a round is weak, $|(v+f^{\mathrm{xc}})\chi^0|\lt1$, the terms shrink and the series converges. When it is strong, each round overcorrects, the terms grow, and the series diverges, although its sum $(\varepsilon^{\mathrm e})^{-1}$ is perfectly finite. Strong rounds are the rule for slowly varying potentials: a metal has $|v\chi^0|\simeq(k_{\mathrm{TF}}/q)^2\gg1$ at long wavelengths, and even silicon, with a dielectric constant near 12, has $|v\chi^0|\approx11$.

    For $\Delta V_{\mathrm{old}}=\Delta V^{\mathrm{KS}}$ the bracket equals $\Delta V^{\mathrm{KS}}$, and so does the weighted average.

  2. Subtract the fixed point. The error obeys

    $$\begin{gathered}\Delta V_{\mathrm{new}}-\Delta V^{\mathrm{KS}}\\ =\big(1-w\,\varepsilon^{\mathrm e}\big)\big(\Delta V_{\mathrm{old}}-\Delta V^{\mathrm{KS}}\big) .\end{gathered}$$
    The bracket of minus the right side of is $(v+f^{\mathrm{xc}})\chi^0$ times the error, and $(1-w)+w(v+f^{\mathrm{xc}})\chi^0=1-w\varepsilon^{\mathrm e}$.

  3. For one Fourier component of the electron gas, where $\varepsilon^{\mathrm e}$ is a number, each cycle multiplies the error by $\zeta=1-w\varepsilon^{\mathrm e}$. The loop converges if $|\zeta|\lt1$, that is if $0\lt w\varepsilon^{\mathrm e}\lt2$.     In a crystal the same holds for each eigenvector of $\varepsilon^{\mathrm e}$, and the largest eigenvalue limits $w$.

  4. Test electron or test charge

In plane waves, is a matrix equation. Its off-diagonal elements $\varepsilon^{\mathrm e}_{\bG\bG'}(\bq)$, $\bG\neq\bG'$, are the local-field effects: a potential with one wavevector $\bq+\bG'$ induces density at every other $\bq+\bG$, because the electrons of a crystal are not uniform and the induced charge piles up where they are, near the atoms and in the bonds. In the electron gas these elements vanish.

Two dielectric functions appear in the literature. They differ in what feels the screened potential.

  • Test electron. $\Delta V^{\mathrm{KS}}$ is the potential felt by an electron. That electron takes part in exchange and correlation with the electrons that responded, so it feels their exchange–correlation potential $f^{\mathrm{xc}}\Delta n$ as well as their Hartree potential. The function $\varepsilon^{\mathrm e}$ of , which includes $f^{\mathrm{xc}}$, is therefore called the test-electron dielectric function.
  • Test charge. A classical point charge interacts with the electrons only through electrostatics. The electrons respond in the same way whatever probes them, $\Delta n=\chi^0\Delta V^{\mathrm{KS}}$, so the potential energy that a test charge $-e$ feels is
    $$\begin{gathered}\Delta V^{\mathrm{en}}+\Delta V^{\mathrm H}\\ =\Big[1+v\,\chi^0\big(\varepsilon^{\mathrm e}\big)^{-1}\Big]\Delta V^{\mathrm{en}} .\end{gathered} \tag{A3.16}$$

Without exchange and correlation the two coincide. Neglecting $f^{\mathrm{xc}}$, so that independent electrons respond to the Hartree potential alone, is the random-phase approximation (RPA) of Section A.2, due to Pines and Bohm:

$$\begin{gathered}\varepsilon^{\mathrm e}=1-\big(v+f^{\mathrm{xc}}\big)\chi^0,\\ \varepsilon^{\mathrm e}_{\mathrm{RPA}}=1-v\,\chi^0,\\ \varepsilon^{\mathrm e}_{\mathrm{RPA},\bG\bG'}(\bq)\\ =\delta_{\bG\bG'}-v(\bq+\bG)\,\chi^0_{\bG\bG'}(\bq) .\end{gathered} \tag{A3.17}$$

The last line follows from and . Which function applies depends on the probe:12

  • the electron–phonon vertex scatters an electron, so it is screened as for a test electron, which is what DFPT computes;
  • the nuclei are classical charges, so the forces between them, and the phonon frequencies, involve the induced density only through electrostatics, as the force-constant derivation above shows; DFPT gets them right because it computes $\Delta n$ itself;
  • the screened Coulomb interaction $W$ of the GW method, which Section A.5 builds, is usually taken in the RPA.

The difference is not small at short distances. In the electron gas, the exchange kernel above doubles the field of a displaced sodium ion that survives at $R_{\mathrm{WS}}=2.1$ Å, while in aluminium, with its denser gas, it changes it by a few percent (). The change comes from $q\gtrsim k_{\mathrm F}$, where a local kernel is least reliable.

Silicon. The measured electronic dielectric constant of silicon, $\varepsilon_\infty$, is about 11.7: a perturbation that varies slowly over many cells is reduced about twelvefold inside the crystal. For the bare perturbation of a displaced silicon sublattice, the $\bG=0$ term of , 181 eV/Å at $q=0.1$ Å⁻¹, this alone would leave 15.5 eV/Å, still growing as $1/q$. The local fields remove the rest. The induced charge around each displaced ion cancels its dipole exactly: silicon's two atoms are equivalent, so their Born effective charges, the dipoles per unit displacement that Section A.4 introduces, vanish. The screened vertex of silicon has no $1/q$ part. In polar crystals the cancellation is incomplete, and the $1/q$ term that remains is the Fröhlich coupling of Section A.4.13

A single sheet. A code treats a two-dimensional material as a stack of copies of the sheet, separated by vacuum and repeated periodically. The Coulomb interaction of the sheet with these periodic images must be cut off, in DFPT as in GW, and the screening changes character. For a semiconducting sheet the static screening of a potential with in-plane wavevector $\bQ_\parallel$ is close to $1+r_0Q_\parallel$, with $r_0$ a screening length of the sheet. It tends to 1 at long wavelengths instead of to $\varepsilon_\infty$. Section B.10 derives this form and its consequences.14

For GW and BSE users: the same matrix, another operand skip if familiar

The GW approximation and the Bethe–Salpeter equation (BSE) screen the Coulomb interaction between electrons with $W=(\varepsilon^{\mathrm e}_{\mathrm{RPA}})^{-1}v$, built from the same $\chi^0_{\bG\bG'}$ as , at finite frequency. The static $W$ of a BSE kernel uses the matrix $\varepsilon^{\mathrm e}_{\mathrm{RPA},\bG\bG'}(\bq)$ of itself, local fields included. Four differences remain.

  • Operand. $W$ screens the interaction between electrons; DFPT screens the electron–ion perturbation.
  • Probe. DFPT keeps $f^{\mathrm{xc}}$ (test electron); the usual GW $W$ is the RPA one.
  • Frequency. DFPT is static; GW keeps the frequency dependence.
  • Inversion. DFPT never forms or inverts $\varepsilon^{\mathrm e}$. Its loop solves iteratively, without empty states.

Everything so far is static: $\Delta V^{\mathrm{en}}$ was taken independent of time. A real phonon modulates the potential at its frequency. For a perturbation $\propto\ee^{-\ii\omega t}$, time-dependent first-order perturbation theory gives the same sum as , with $\hbar\omega$ added to each energy difference:

$$\begin{gathered}\chi^0_{\bG\bG'}(\bq,\omega)=\frac{2}{\OVSC}\sum_{nm\bk}\\ \times\frac{f_{n\bk}-f_{m\bk+\bq}}{E_{n\bk}-E_{m\bk+\bq}+\hbar(\omega+\ii\eta)}\\ \times\langle\psi_{n\bk}|\ee^{-\ii(\bq+\bG)\cdot\br}|\psi_{m\bk+\bq}\rangle\\ \times\langle\psi_{m\bk+\bq}|\ee^{\ii(\bq+\bG')\cdot\br}|\psi_{n\bk}\rangle ,\end{gathered} \tag{A3.21}$$

where $\eta\to0^+$ makes the response follow the perturbation instead of preceding it: the response is retarded, in the sense of Section A.2. DFPT sets $\omega=0$. The phonon is then treated as quasistatic: at each instantaneous position of the atoms during a vibration, the electrons have time to reach their ground state. This is the adiabatic approximation of Section A.2, the Born–Oppenheimer picture of Section A.1 applied to the response. When it holds can be read off :

  1. Pair each transition $n\bk\to m\bk+\bq$ with its time-reversed partner, as in . With $E_{m\bk+\bq}-E_{n\bk}\gt0$, together they contribute the same product of matrix elements times

    $$\begin{gathered}-\frac{1}{E_{m\bk+\bq}-E_{n\bk}-\hbar\omega}\\ -\frac{1}{E_{m\bk+\bq}-E_{n\bk}+\hbar\omega}\\ =-\frac{2}{E_{m\bk+\bq}-E_{n\bk}}\\ \times\Big[1+\frac{\hbar^2\omega^2}{(E_{m\bk+\bq}-E_{n\bk})^2}+\dots\Big] .\end{gathered}$$
    The static value is reached up to corrections of order $\big(\hbar\omega/(E_{m\bk+\bq}-E_{n\bk})\big)^2$. Static screening is accurate whenever $\hbar\omega$ is much smaller than the energies of the transitions that build $\chi^0$.

  2. In an insulator every transition costs at least the band gap. In silicon $\hbar\omega\le65$ meV and the gap is 1.1 eV, so the correction is below 0.4 percent.

    $(0.065/1.1)^2\approx0.0035$, and most of $\chi^0$ comes from transitions of several eV.

  3. In a metal, transitions within a band across the Fermi surface cost anything from zero up to about $\hbar v_{\mathrm F}q$, with $v_{\mathrm F}=\hbar k_{\mathrm F}/m_{\mathrm e}$ the Fermi velocity of Section A.2. Static screening holds for $\omega\ll v_{\mathrm F}q$. Acoustic phonons have $\omega=v_{\mathrm s}q$ with a sound velocity $v_{\mathrm s}$ about 300 times smaller than $v_{\mathrm F}$ in sodium and aluminium, so for them it holds. For optical phonons with $q\lesssim\omega/v_{\mathrm F}$ it fails: the electrons cannot follow.

    A transition that costs less than $\hbar\omega$ cannot adjust within one period of the vibration; the response is then neither static nor real.

The failure matters in metals and in doped semiconductors, whose small Fermi energies make $v_{\mathrm F}$ small. The textbook case is doped graphene. Its Raman-active optical phonon at $\bq=0$ ($\hbar\omega\approx196$ meV, with $\hbar v_{\mathrm F}\approx6.6$ eV Å, so the window is $q\lesssim0.03$ Å⁻¹) shifts with doping by about one percent in experiment, and only the dynamic response reproduces the shift. Bardeen and Pines had already screened the vertex with the frequency-dependent dielectric function (Section A.2). The review calls this frequency dependence of the screening retardation, as Section A.2 explained; in Part B the word also covers the finite speed of light (Section X.1). Section A.6 splits the phonon self-energy, the correction that the electrons add to the phonon frequencies and linewidths, into the adiabatic part that DFPT computes and a nonadiabatic remainder built from $\chi^0(\bq,\omega)-\chi^0(\bq,0)$.18

Second order: the Debye–Waller matrix element

Section A.1 expanded the energy surface to second order in the displacements; Section A.2 expanded the potential felt by an electron only to first order. Allen and Heine pointed out that this is inconsistent. To second order in the displacements, an electron's energy changes through two channels of the same size: the linear coupling acting twice, which is second order in $g$, and the quadratic term of the potential acting once. Both are of order $\hbar/2M_\kappa\omega_{\bq\nu}$, the square of a zero-point amplitude. The second channel needs the second-order term of $V^{\mathrm{KS}}$:

  1. Expand $V^{\mathrm{KS}}$ to second order in the displacement operators $\hat u_{\ell\kappa\alpha}$. The new term is

    $$\frac12\sum_{\ell\kappa\alpha}\sum_{\ell'\kappa'\beta}\frac{\partial^2V^{\mathrm{KS}}}{\partial u_{\ell\kappa\alpha}\,\partial u_{\ell'\kappa'\beta}}\,\hat u_{\ell\kappa\alpha}\,\hat u_{\ell'\kappa'\beta} .$$
    The Taylor coefficient of a second-order term is $\tfrac12$. The derivatives are those of the self-consistent potential.

  2. Insert the displacement operator for each $\hat u$. Each brings a factor $N_\ell^{-1/2}$, a zero-point amplitude, an eigenvector component, a phase and a phonon field $\hat\phi_{\bq\nu}$. The sums over cells, atoms and directions are those of $\Delta_{\bq\nu}$ in , once for each displacement:

    $$\frac1{2N_\ell}\sum_{\bq\nu}\sum_{\bq'\nu'}\Delta_{\bq\nu}\Delta_{\bq'\nu'}V^{\mathrm{KS}}\;\hat\phi_{\bq\nu}\,\hat\phi_{\bq'\nu'} .$$
    $\Delta_{\bq\nu}\Delta_{\bq'\nu'}V$ is the operation $\Delta$ applied twice, with second derivatives of $V$ in place of first ones.

  3. Turn this one-electron operator into second quantization as in Section A.2, as a sum of matrix elements times $c^\dagger_{m\bk''}c_{n\bk}$. The operator is a Bloch function with the wavevector $\bq+\bq'$, by applied twice, so only $\bk''=\bk+\bq+\bq'$ survives.

    The same selection rule as for $g$, with the two phonon wavevectors added.

The result is the second-order term $\hat H^{\mathrm{DW}}$ of the full Hamiltonian , now with its matrix element derived:

$$\begin{gathered}\hat H^{\mathrm{DW}}=\frac1{N_\ell}\sum_{\substack{\bk\bq\bq'\\ mn\nu\nu'}}g^{\mathrm{DW}}_{mn\nu\nu'}(\bk,\bq,\bq')\\ \times c^\dagger_{m\bk+\bq+\bq'}c_{n\bk}\,\hat\phi_{\bq\nu}\hat\phi_{\bq'\nu'} ,\end{gathered} \tag{A3.22}$$
$$\begin{gathered}g^{\mathrm{DW}}_{mn\nu\nu'}(\bk,\bq,\bq')=\tfrac12\\ \times\langle\psi_{m\bk+\bq+\bq'}|\,\Delta_{\bq\nu}\Delta_{\bq'\nu'}V^{\mathrm{KS}}\,|\psi_{n\bk}\rangle .\end{gathered} \tag{A3.23}$$
$g^{\mathrm{DW}}_{mn\nu\nu'}(\bk,\bq,\bq')$
second-order, Debye–Waller, matrix element for $n\bk\to m\,\bk+\bq+\bq'$ with two phonon fields (eV); a supercell integral of states normalized on the supercell, like $g$
$\hat H^{\mathrm{DW}}$
second-order electron–phonon Hamiltonian, the last term of

$N_\ell^{-1}$ stands outside $g^{\mathrm{DW}}$, as $N_\ell^{-1/2}$ stands outside $g$ in , so $g^{\mathrm{DW}}$ does not depend on the size of the supercell. Its first effect is a shift of every band energy, in first-order perturbation theory at temperature $T$:

  1. Average over the phonons. $\langle\hat\phi_{\bq\nu}\hat\phi_{\bq'\nu'}\rangle$ vanishes unless $\bq'=-\bq$ and $\nu'=\nu$, and then equals $2n_{\bq\nu}+1$, with $n_{\bq\nu}$ the Bose–Einstein occupation, as in Section A.1.

    A thermal state is diagonal in the phonon numbers.

  2. Take the expectation value in the electron state $\psi_{n\bk}$. Only $m=n$ and $\bq+\bq'=0$ contribute, so the shift is

    $$\begin{gathered}\delta E^{\mathrm{DW}}_{n\bk}=\frac1{N_\ell}\sum_{\bq\nu}\big(2n_{\bq\nu}+1\big)\\ \times g^{\mathrm{DW}}_{nn\nu\nu}(\bk,\bq,-\bq) .\end{gathered} \tag{A3.24}$$
    $c^\dagger_{m\bk''}c_{n\bk}$ has a diagonal expectation value only when it removes and restores the same electron.

The shift is nonzero at zero temperature, through the 1 of zero-point motion, and it grows with $T$. Sections A.7 and A.8 combine it with the linear coupling acting twice, the Fan–Migdal term, into the temperature dependence of band gaps.19

The rigid-ion approximation

The second derivatives of the self-consistent potential require second-order DFPT, with its own Sternheimer equations: possible, but costly and rarely done. Practical calculations use instead an approximation introduced by Allen and Heine. In this rigid-ion approximation the Kohn–Sham potential is a sum of atom-centred pieces, each carried rigidly by its atom. Section A.2 used the same name for bare ions moving without any response of the electrons; here each piece is the screened, self-consistent potential of its atom, and only its shape is frozen:

$$\begin{gathered}V^{\mathrm{KS}}(\br)\simeq\sum_{\ell\kappa}V^{\mathrm{at}}_\kappa\big(\br-\btau_{\ell\kappa}-\bu_{\ell\kappa}\big),\\ \frac{\partial^2V^{\mathrm{KS}}}{\partial u_{\ell\kappa\alpha}\,\partial u_{\ell'\kappa'\beta}}\\ \simeq\delta_{\ell\ell'}\,\delta_{\kappa\kappa'}\,\partial_\alpha\partial_\beta V^{\mathrm{at}}_\kappa(\br-\btau_{\ell\kappa}) .\end{gathered} \tag{A3.25}$$

The second line follows from the first because each piece depends on the displacement of its own atom only. What the approximation leaves out is the change of one atom's screening cloud when a different atom moves: the off-site second derivatives. The review quotes errors of about 10 meV in the zero-point shift of the band gap of diamond (its shift at zero temperature, Section A.8), and below 4 meV in a later comparison with frozen-phonon calculations.20 The Debye–Waller shift then takes a transparent form:

$$\begin{gathered}\delta E^{\mathrm{DW}}_{n\bk}\simeq\frac12\sum_{\kappa\alpha\beta}\big\langle\hat u_{\kappa\alpha}\hat u_{\kappa\beta}\big\rangle\\ \times\sum_\ell\langle\psi_{n\bk}|\partial_\alpha\partial_\beta V^{\mathrm{at}}_\kappa(\br-\btau_{\ell\kappa})|\psi_{n\bk}\rangle,\\ \big\langle\hat u_{\kappa\alpha}\hat u_{\kappa\beta}\big\rangle=\frac1{N_\ell}\sum_{\bq\nu}\frac{\hbar}{2M_\kappa\omega_{\bq\nu}}\\ \times e_{\kappa\alpha,\nu}(\bq)\,e^*_{\kappa\beta,\nu}(\bq)\,\big(2n_{\bq\nu}+1\big) .\end{gathered} \tag{A3.26}$$
$V^{\mathrm{at}}_\kappa$
piece of the Kohn–Sham potential carried by atom $\kappa$ in the rigid-ion approximation (eV)
$\langle\hat u_{\kappa\alpha}\hat u_{\kappa\beta}\rangle$
thermal average of the product of two displacement components of atom $\kappa$, the same in every cell, so the cell index is dropped (Å²); for $\alpha=\beta$ it is the mean-square displacement $\langle\hat u^2_{\ell\kappa\alpha}\rangle$ of Section A.1
Derivation: the Debye–Waller shift in the rigid-ion approximation

  1. Insert the second line of into $\Delta_{\bq\nu}\Delta_{-\bq\nu}V^{\mathrm{KS}}$. The Kronecker deltas collapse the double sums over cells and atoms:

    $$\begin{gathered}\Delta_{\bq\nu}\Delta_{-\bq\nu}V^{\mathrm{KS}}=\sum_{\kappa\alpha\beta}\frac{\hbar}{2M_\kappa\omega_{\bq\nu}}\\ \times e_{\kappa\alpha,\nu}(\bq)\,e_{\kappa\beta,\nu}(-\bq)\\ \times\sum_\ell\partial_\alpha\partial_\beta V^{\mathrm{at}}_\kappa(\br-\btau_{\ell\kappa}) .\end{gathered}$$
    Both displacements belong to the same atom $\ell\kappa$, so their phases $\ee^{\ii\bq\cdot\bR_\ell}$ and $\ee^{-\ii\bq\cdot\bR_\ell}$ cancel, and $\omega_{-\bq\nu}=\omega_{\bq\nu}$.

  2. Use $e_{\kappa\beta,\nu}(-\bq)=e^*_{\kappa\beta,\nu}(\bq)$, insert the result into and , and collect the sum over $\bq\nu$ with the factor $(2n_{\bq\nu}+1)/N_\ell$. It is $\langle\hat u_{\kappa\alpha}\hat u_{\kappa\beta}\rangle$.

    This is the average of two displacement operators that gave the mean-square displacement in Section A.1, now for two directions.

In words, the electron feels each atomic potential averaged over the cloud of positions that zero-point and thermal motion give the atom. For an atom at the origin with displacement $\hat\bu_\kappa$, the first-order term averages to zero, and

$$\begin{gathered}\langle V^{\mathrm{at}}_\kappa(\br-\hat\bu_\kappa)\rangle\simeq V^{\mathrm{at}}_\kappa(\br)\\ +\tfrac12\sum_{\alpha\beta}\langle\hat u_{\kappa\alpha}\hat u_{\kappa\beta}\rangle\,\partial_\alpha\partial_\beta V^{\mathrm{at}}_\kappa(\br) .\end{gathered}$$

This is a blurred crystal potential. The same blurring weakens x-ray reflections from a crystal by the Debye–Waller factor, which gives the coupling its name. Allen and Heine went one step further. Translation invariance, the condition that a rigid shift of the whole crystal cannot change an electron's energy, relates the matrix elements of $\partial_\alpha\partial_\beta V^{\mathrm{at}}_\kappa$ to products of first-order matrix elements $g$, so that no second derivative is ever computed. Section A.8 derives this; DFPT calculations of temperature-dependent band structures use it.

Check yourself: three short questions

  1. For the slowly varying part of a perturbation in an insulator, $\varepsilon^{\mathrm e}\approx9$. Does the plain loop ($w=1$) converge for that part? Which mixing weight converges in a single cycle?

    Answer. No: each cycle multiplies the error by $\zeta=1-\varepsilon^{\mathrm e}=-8$. The weight $w=1/\varepsilon^{\mathrm e}=1/9$ gives $\zeta=0$, and any $w\lt2/9$ converges.

  2. Why does the Sternheimer equation need no empty states, while the sum over states does?

    Answer. The density needs only the part of each orbital's change that lies in the empty states, . The projector $1-\mathcal P^{\mathrm{occ}}_{\bk+\bq}$, built from occupied states, isolates that part, and determines it as the solution of a linear equation instead of a sum over the empty states.

  3. In sodium, the bare $\bG=0$ perturbation of a long longitudinal wave grows as $1/q$. What does the screened one do as $q\to0$, and how large is it at $q=0.1$ Å⁻¹ ($\OV=37.7$ Å³)?

    Answer. Dividing by $\varepsilon^{\mathrm e}\simeq k_{\mathrm{TF}}^2/q^2$ gives $|\Delta V^{\mathrm{KS}}|\simeq4\pi Z_\kappa e^2q/(\OV k_{\mathrm{TF}}^2)=\tfrac23E_{\mathrm F}\,q$, which vanishes linearly: 0.22 eV/Å at $q=0.1$ Å⁻¹, against 48 eV/Å for the bare perturbation $4\pi e^2/(\OV q)$.
