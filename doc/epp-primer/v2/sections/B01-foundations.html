Part B

The electron–phonon–photon (EPP) formalism

Part B follows the manuscript “Theory of the Electron-Phonon-Photon coupling”, cited as the EPP manuscript. Its programme is to put the full electromagnetic field back into the electron–phonon problem of Part A. One Lagrangian describes the valence electrons, the vibrating ions and the field. Coulomb's law, the Fröhlich vertex, the electronic dielectric constant and the LO–TO splitting then follow from it, instead of being put in by hand.

The manuscript is a draft. Where it contains an error, the main text shows the correct result and one note states what the manuscript prints. Three corrections change results: the Born-charge dipoles sit at their atoms, not at the corner of their cell (Section B.1); the long-wavelength limit of a phonon dressed by photons is a polariton, not the LO–TO splitting (Section B.7); and a factor of the elementary charge $e$ is restored in the continuity equation that links charges to currents (Section B.6). The manuscript changes units between its sections. Part B keeps the primer's Gaussian units throughout and gives SI forms in the notes.

The ten sections form one chain. The phonons and the Born-charge dipoles behind the polar vertex (B.1) enter one Lagrangian (B.2), in which the field, not an electron–phonon term, couples electrons to ions; its classical solution is the polar crystal as a dielectric (B.3). Quantizing the field gives transverse photons and returns Coulomb's law as the longitudinal part of the field (B.4). Electrons and ions dress the photon through one self-energy (B.5), and charge conservation assembles the total propagator, with its plasmons and polaritons (B.6). Phonons dressed by photons acquire the LO–TO splitting (B.7) and an electrodynamic Fröhlich vertex (B.8). Letting the speed of light $c$ go to infinity recovers Part A (B.9), and a single sheet of atoms shows why two-dimensional crystals have no LO–TO splitting at $\Gamma$ (B.10).

The EPP starting point: lattice, bare vertex and polar coupling

The EPP manuscript begins by rebuilding three objects of Part A in a bookkeeping that separates what is bare from what the screening by the valence electrons adds: the phonons, the vertex of an unscreened ion, and the long-range vertex of the Born-charge dipoles. This section derives the two vertices step by step, states the rule that keeps a calculation from counting the same screening twice, and shows why the dipole of every atom must carry the phase of its own position. These are the pieces from which the electrodynamic theory of Sections B.2 to B.10 starts.

The picture: the objects of Part A, with their screening tracked

In Part A every interaction between electrons and nuclei is the instantaneous Coulomb interaction. The vertex an electron feels is computed with density-functional perturbation theory and is already screened by all the other electrons (Section A.3). In a polar crystal its long-range part comes from the dipoles of the displaced ions, treated with electrostatics (Section A.4). The EPP manuscript replaces electrostatics by the full electromagnetic field. Before it can do so, it has to say exactly what each ingredient already contains, so that the field adds nothing a second time.

Its first three sections therefore rebuild the objects of Part A in a bookkeeping that keeps track of screening. A quantity is bare when it is computed without the response of the valence electrons, which later diagrams are meant to supply; a quantity that is not bare, such as a Born charge, must say which part of that response it already contains. The bookkeeping yields three objects: the phonons, the vertex of a rigid ion that nothing screens, and the long-range vertex of the Born-charge dipoles. The plan of the section follows them:

  1. recall the phonons of Section A.1;
  2. derive the bare vertex, and see why it is far too large;
  3. state the rule that prevents double counting, for two possible starting points;
  4. derive the polar vertex from Born charges;
  5. show that each dipole must carry the phase of its own atom, and expand the vertex at long wavelength;
  6. list what carries forward into Sections B.2 to B.10.

The bare vertex: a rigid ion that nothing screens

Valence electrons move in the potential of the ions. In the EPP manuscript each ion, a nucleus with its tightly bound core electrons, acts on a valence electron through a fixed pseudopotential $V_\kappa(\br)$: the potential energy of a valence electron at $\br$ when ion $\kappa$ sits at the origin, built once for the free atom and used unchanged in the crystal. Two assumptions define the model. The pseudopotential is local: it multiplies the wavefunction, as an ordinary potential does. And the ion is rigid: when the nucleus moves, its potential moves with it without changing shape. Its core electrons stay frozen in the state they have in the free atom, which is why this description is called the frozen-core model. The vertex built from this potential is bare in the sense of the picture above: the valence electrons are not allowed to respond to the moving ion.

Fourier analysis gives the picture behind the result derived below. The potential of one ion is a superposition of plane waves $\ee^{\ii\bq'\cdot\br}$ of every wavevector $\bq'$. A lattice wave of wavevector $\bq$ shakes the cells with the phases $\ee^{\ii\bq\cdot\bR_\ell}$, and only the plane waves that share this pattern add up over the whole crystal: those with $\bq'=\bq+\bG$, one for each reciprocal lattice vector $\bG$. Each of these components couples to the electron with a weight $w(\bG)$: the amplitude of the plane wave in the ion's potential times the component of its wavevector along the atomic motion, $(\bq+\bG)\cdot\be_{\kappa\nu}(\bq)$, divided by the cell volume (energy per length, eV/Å). draws the components and their weights for sodium.

From a moving ion to the matrix element

The potential energy of a valence electron at $\br$ in the vibrating crystal is the sum over all ions, each at its instantaneous position :

$$V^{\mathrm{en}}(\br)=\sum_{\ell\kappa}V_\kappa\big(\br-\btau_{\ell\kappa}-\bu_{\ell\kappa}\big) . \tag{B1.1}$$

electron–ion potential energy of the crystal (eV), the electron–nucleus potential of Section A.2 with each nucleus and its core replaced by a pseudopotential
$V_\kappa(\br)$
potential energy of a valence electron at $\br$ due to ion $\kappa$ placed at the origin (eV)
$\btau_{\ell\kappa}$, $\bu_{\ell\kappa}$
equilibrium position and displacement of atom $\kappa$ of cell $\ell$ (Å)

  1. Expand to first order in the displacements. The first-order change of the potential, written $\Delta V^{\mathrm{en}}$, is

    $$\Delta V^{\mathrm{en}}(\br)=-\sum_{\ell\kappa\alpha}u_{\ell\kappa\alpha}\,\partial_\alpha V_\kappa(\br-\btau_{\ell\kappa}) ,$$
    Here $\partial_\alpha V_\kappa$ is the derivative of $V_\kappa$ along the Cartesian direction $\alpha$, taken at $\br-\btau_{\ell\kappa}$. Displacements of a few hundredths of an ångström are small compared with the distances over which $V_\kappa$ changes. The minus sign appears because moving the ion by $+\bu$ has the same effect as evaluating its potential at $\br-\bu$. The second-order term is the Debye–Waller coupling of Section A.3, which the EPP manuscript drops.

  2. Write each ionic potential as a superposition of plane waves, $V_\kappa(\br)=\int\frac{\dd^3q'}{(2\pi)^3}\,V_\kappa(\bq')\,\ee^{\ii\bq'\cdot\br}$, with $V_\kappa(\bq')=\int\dd^3r\,\ee^{-\ii\bq'\cdot\br}\,V_\kappa(\br)$. The gradient brings down $\ii q'_\alpha$, and the shift to the ion's position multiplies each plane wave by $\ee^{-\ii\bq'\cdot\btau_{\ell\kappa}}$:

    $$\begin{aligned}&\Delta V^{\mathrm{en}}(\br)\\&\ =-\ii\sum_{\ell\kappa}\int\frac{\dd^3q'}{(2\pi)^3}\\&\quad\times\big(\bq'\cdot\bu_{\ell\kappa}\big)\,V_\kappa(\bq')\,\ee^{\ii\bq'\cdot(\br-\btau_{\ell\kappa})} .\end{aligned} \tag{B1.2}$$
    The derivative of $\ee^{\ii\bq'\cdot\br}$ along $\alpha$ is $\ii q'_\alpha\,\ee^{\ii\bq'\cdot\br}$. The phase records where the ion sits: the corner $\bR_\ell$ of its cell plus its position $\btau_\kappa$ inside the cell. $V_\kappa(\bq')$ has units of energy times volume (eV Å³).

  3. Insert the displacement operator for $u_{\ell\kappa\alpha}$, which turns the change of potential into an operator, $\Delta\hat V^{\mathrm{en}}$. With $\ee^{-\ii\bq'\cdot\btau_{\ell\kappa}}=\ee^{-\ii\bq'\cdot\bR_\ell}\,\ee^{-\ii\bq'\cdot\btau_\kappa}$, the cell index appears only in one factor:

    $$\begin{aligned}\Delta\hat V^{\mathrm{en}}(\br)={}&-\ii\sum_{\bq\nu\kappa}\Big(\frac{\hbar}{2N_\ell M_\kappa\omega_{\bq\nu}}\Big)^{1/2}\phi_{\bq\nu}\\&\times\int\frac{\dd^3q'}{(2\pi)^3}\big(\bq'\cdot\be_{\kappa\nu}(\bq)\big)\,V_\kappa(\bq')\\&\times\ee^{-\ii\bq'\cdot\btau_\kappa}\,\ee^{\ii\bq'\cdot\br}\sum_\ell\ee^{\ii(\bq-\bq')\cdot\bR_\ell} .\end{aligned}$$
    Each mode carries the phase $\ee^{\ii\bq\cdot\bR_\ell}$ of the displaced atom's cell; each Fourier component of the ionic potential carries $\ee^{-\ii\bq'\cdot\bR_\ell}$ of the same cell.

  4. Do the sum over cells: $\sum_\ell\ee^{\ii(\bq-\bq')\cdot\bR_\ell}=N_\ell\sum_{\bG}\delta_{\bq',\bq+\bG}$. On the finite crystal the integral over $\bq'$ is a sum over the allowed wavevectors, $\int\frac{\dd^3q'}{(2\pi)^3}\to\frac{1}{\OVSC}\sum_{\bq'}$, so the Kronecker delta keeps only $\bq'=\bq+\bG$, and the factors combine as $N_\ell/\OVSC=1/\OV$.

    The phases of $N_\ell$ cells add up only when $\bq'-\bq$ is a reciprocal lattice vector; otherwise they spread evenly around the circle and cancel, as in the derivation of the normal coordinates in Section A.1 (the background box below gives the finite-crystal rules). Here $\OVSC=N_\ell\OV$ is the volume of the supercell and $\OV$ that of one cell. This step is the statement of the picture above: only the components $\bq+\bG$ survive.

The result has the general form of Section A.2, $\Delta\hat V^{\mathrm{en}}(\br)=N_\ell^{-1/2}\sum_{\bq\nu}\Delta_{\bq\nu}V^{\mathrm{en}}(\br)\,\phi_{\bq\nu}$, with an explicit formula for the change of potential per unit phonon field $\Delta_{\bq\nu}V$:

$$\begin{aligned}&\Delta_{\bq\nu}V^{\mathrm{en}}(\br)\\&\ =-\frac{\ii}{\OV}\sum_\kappa\Big(\frac{\hbar}{2M_\kappa\omega_{\bq\nu}}\Big)^{1/2}\\&\quad\times\sum_{\bG}(\bq+\bG)\cdot\be_{\kappa\nu}(\bq)\,V_\kappa(\bq+\bG)\\&\quad\times\ee^{-\ii(\bq+\bG)\cdot\btau_\kappa}\,\ee^{\ii(\bq+\bG)\cdot\br} .\end{aligned} \tag{B1.3}$$

Each term is $\ee^{\ii\bq\cdot\br}$ times a function with the period of the lattice, since $\ee^{\ii\bG\cdot\br}$ is periodic. Between Bloch states such a perturbation connects an electron wavevector $\bk$ only to $\bk+\bq$, as Section A.2 shows. Its matrix element between the Bloch states $\psi_{n\bk}$ and $\psi_{m\bk+\bq}$, defined as in with $V^{\mathrm{en}}$ in place of $V^{\mathrm{KS}}$, is the bare vertex:3

Fourier transforms on a finite crystal skip if familiar

On the Born–von Kármán crystal every function repeats with the supercell, including the potential of one ion together with its copies in the other supercells. Its Fourier components therefore exist only on the grid of allowed wavevectors $\bq'$ of Section A.1, all of them and not only those in the first Brillouin zone, and an integral over wavevectors is a sum over that grid:

$$\begin{gathered}\int\frac{\dd^3q'}{(2\pi)^3}\,f(\bq')\;\to\;\frac{1}{\OVSC}\sum_{\bq'}f(\bq') ,\\ \OVSC=N_\ell\OV .\end{gathered}$$

Each grid wavevector can be written in exactly one way as $\bq'=\bq+\bG$ with $\bq$ in the first Brillouin zone and $\bG$ a reciprocal lattice vector. The sum over cells is $\sum_\ell\ee^{\ii(\bq-\bq')\cdot\bR_\ell}=N_\ell$ when $\bq'-\bq$ is a reciprocal lattice vector and zero otherwise, which is $N_\ell\sum_{\bG}\delta_{\bq',\bq+\bG}$. The Fourier coefficients of the ion and its copies are exactly the transform of the isolated ion, $V_\kappa(\bq')$, taken at the grid points: the integral over one supercell of all the copies equals the integral of one copy over all space, because $\ee^{-\ii\bq'\cdot\br}$ has the period of the supercell for every grid wavevector.

Reading the bare vertex

Each factor of has a meaning.

  • The zero-point amplitude $(\hbar/2M_\kappa\omega_{\bq\nu})^{1/2}$ carries no factor $1/\sqrt{N_\ell}$: that factor stands in front of the Hamiltonian, as in Section A.2.
  • The projection $(\bq+\bG)\cdot\be_{\kappa\nu}(\bq)$ comes from the gradient in step 1: moving the ion changes the plane wave $\ee^{\ii(\bq+\bG)\cdot\br}$ of its potential only through the component of the motion along $\bq+\bG$. At $\bG=0$ only motion along $\bq$ couples. A longitudinal wave compresses and stretches the lattice of ions; a transverse wave only shears it, and a shear does not change the density of ions on scales longer than a cell. The umklapp terms, $\bG\neq0$, let transverse modes couple too: in a transverse wave has no weight at $\bq$ but keeps weights at the points $\bq+\bG$.
  • The form factor $V_\kappa(\bq+\bG)$ says how strongly the ion's potential varies on the length scale $2\pi/|\bq+\bG|$.
  • The basis phase $\ee^{-\ii(\bq+\bG)\cdot\btau_\kappa}$ records where the ion sits inside its cell. It appears here without any effort, because the potential is centred on the ion. Below it turns out to be indispensable for the polar vertex too.
  • The overlap decides which bands, and which $\bG$, a given transition picks up. For free electrons, whose states are single plane waves, exactly one $\bG$ contributes: the one that connects the initial plane wave $\bk$ to the final plane wave $\bk+\bq+\bG$. Codes for the random-phase approximation call this overlap the pair density.

Take one atom per cell, placed at the origin, with mass $M$, potential $V$ and eigenvector $\be_\nu(\bq)$; take free electrons, and keep only the term $\bG=0$. Then reduces to $g^0=-\frac{\ii}{\OV}\big(\hbar/2M\omega_{\bq\nu}\big)^{1/2}\,\bq\cdot\be_\nu(\bq)\,V(\bq)$. This is the matrix element that Bloch wrote in 1929 with the rigid ion that Nordheim introduced in 1931: Bloch's average potential is replaced by the Fourier component $V(\bq)/\OV$ of one ion's potential (Section A.2).4

The Coulomb tail: why the bare vertex is not small

Outside its core an ion attracts a valence electron like a point charge. If the ion gives $Z_\kappa$ valence electrons to the crystal (one for sodium), then $V_\kappa(\br)\to-Z_\kappa e^2/r$ at large $r$, where $e\gt0$ is the elementary charge. The Fourier transform of $1/r$ is $4\pi/q^2$, so at small wavevector

$$\begin{gathered}V_\kappa(\bq)\to-\frac{4\pi Z_\kappa e^2}{q^2},\\ \text{so the }\bG=0\text{ term of }g^0\\ \propto\frac{\hat\bq\cdot\be_{\kappa\nu}}{q} .\end{gathered} \tag{B1.5}$$

Here $\hat\bq=\bq/q$ is the unit vector along $\bq$. The $\bG=0$ term of diverges as $1/q$ for every mode with a longitudinal component. Acoustic modes are not spared: their frequency vanishes as $q$, so the zero-point amplitude, proportional to $\omega^{-1/2}$, makes the divergence stronger, $|g^0|\propto q^{-3/2}$. In sodium at $q=0.3$ Å⁻¹ along [100], the $\bG=0$ weight of a longitudinal wave, $|\bq\cdot\be_{\kappa\nu}\,V_\kappa(\bq)|/\OV$, is 15 eV/Å, eleven times the largest umklapp weight ().5

No such divergence is seen in a metal. The valence electrons gather around a moving ion and cancel its long-range field. With Thomas–Fermi screening the same weight drops to 0.6 eV/Å and vanishes linearly as $q\to0$: this is Bardeen's screened vertex, , in its Thomas–Fermi limit. In a nonpolar insulator screening also removes the divergence. In a polar insulator it reduces it to the dipole field of the Born charges, the subject of the polar section below.

The manuscript states two approximations, harmonic ions and frozen cores. Three more are built into the derivation: the coupling is linear in the displacements (there is no Debye–Waller term), the pseudopotentials are local, and the ions are rigid. The first three terms have the structure of the Hamiltonian of Section A.2, , but every parameter in them is bare, where Part A uses density-functional values. The fourth term, the interaction between valence electrons, is kept explicitly; Part A hides it in the bands, the phonons and the screened vertex.6

Two-body operators in second quantization skip if familiar

Section A.2 writes a one-body operator $h$, such as the potential of the ions, as $\sum\langle m|h|n\rangle\,c^\dagger_mc_n$, for a complete set of one-electron states $\psi_n$ with operators $c_n$. The same operator reads $\int\dd^3r\,\hat\psi^\dagger(\br)\,h\,\hat\psi(\br)$ with the field operator $\hat\psi(\br)=\sum_n\psi_n(\br)c_n$, because inserting the expansion gives back the matrix elements. An interaction between pairs of electrons removes two electrons at $\br$ and $\br'$ and puts them back, weighted by $v(\br-\br')$:

$$\frac12\int\dd^3r\,\dd^3r'\,\hat\psi^\dagger(\br)\hat\psi^\dagger(\br')\,v(\br-\br')\,\hat\psi(\br')\hat\psi(\br) .$$

The factor $\frac12$ corrects for counting each pair twice. Writing the annihilation operators to the right of the creation operators removes the interaction of an electron with itself: acting on a state with a single electron, the operator gives zero. In Bloch states the operator becomes $\frac12\sum v\,c^\dagger c^\dagger cc$, with crystal momentum conserved by the same lattice-sum argument as in step 4 above.

Two starting points and the double-counting rule

The bare Hamiltonian has one useful property: nothing in it is counted twice. Including the same physical process twice, for example screening a vertex that is already screened, is called double counting. The unperturbed part of , its first two terms, contains no interaction, and every interaction is explicit: between electrons through $v$, between electrons and ions through $g^0$. A perturbation expansion may then let every particle interact with every other, in any order, with no risk of including a process twice.

The price is convergence. Neither interaction is small, the bare phonons are far from the real ones, and the bare vertex is too large by the very factor that screening removes. Screening as a sum of bubbles skip if familiar

A moving ion changes the potential by $\Delta V^{\mathrm{en}}$. The valence electrons respond with a change of density $\chi^0\Delta V^{\mathrm{en}}$, where $\chi^0$ is the response of independent electrons (Section A.3). In a diagram this response is a closed loop of an electron and a hole, a bubble. The induced density creates its own potential through the Coulomb interaction, $v\chi^0\Delta V^{\mathrm{en}}$, which induces more density, and so on. In the random-phase approximation the total is a geometric series:

$$\begin{aligned}\Delta V&=\Delta V^{\mathrm{en}}+v\chi^0\Delta V^{\mathrm{en}}+v\chi^0v\chi^0\Delta V^{\mathrm{en}}+\dots\\&=\big(1-v\chi^0\big)^{-1}\Delta V^{\mathrm{en}}=\big(\varepsilon^{\mathrm e}\big)^{-1}\Delta V^{\mathrm{en}} .\end{aligned}$$

Each term adds one more bubble and one more Coulomb line. The sum is meant in closed form: it is the solution of the self-consistency condition $\Delta V=\Delta V^{\mathrm{en}}+v\chi^0\Delta V$, which holds at any strength, whereas the series converges term by term only where $|v\chi^0|\lt1$. At long wavelength that fails in a metal, where $v\chi^0\to-k_{\mathrm{TF}}^2/q^2$ with the Thomas–Fermi wavevector $k_{\mathrm{TF}}$ (1.49 Å⁻¹ in sodium), and in an insulator whose electronic dielectric constant $\varepsilon_\infty$ exceeds 2, since there $v\chi^0\to1-\varepsilon_\infty$. With exchange and correlation, every Coulomb line $v$ between the bubbles is replaced by $v+f^{\mathrm{xc}}$, and the result is the self-consistent potential change of density-functional perturbation theory, , with the electronic dielectric function $\varepsilon^{\mathrm e}$.

Starting from density-functional theory

The practical starting point takes the bands, the states and the phonons from density-functional theory and keeps the form of :

$$\begin{gathered}E^0_{n\bk}\to E_{n\bk},\qquad \psi^0_{n\bk}\to\psi_{n\bk},\\ \omega^0_{\bq\nu}\to\omega_{\bq\nu},\qquad \be^0_{\kappa\nu}\to\be_{\kappa\nu} .\end{gathered}$$

Here $E_{n\bk}$ and $\psi_{n\bk}$ are the Kohn–Sham bands and states of Section A.2 and $\omega_{\bq\nu}$ the phonons of density-functional perturbation theory. Now the unperturbed part already contains interactions. The density-functional phonons contain the static response of the valence electrons, which follow the nuclei instantly (the adiabatic response), and the Kohn–Sham states contain much of the interaction between electrons. The expansion must leave out what is already in. Three rules follow.7

  1. The vertex is screened once. The matrix element $g$ of density-functional perturbation theory, with $\Delta V^{\mathrm{KS}}=(\varepsilon^{\mathrm e})^{-1}\Delta V^{\mathrm{en}}$ from , is the bare vertex with all chains of bubbles already summed (, row (b)). A diagram that uses $g$ must not add those bubbles again. In the same way, the electron bubble of a phonon self-energy takes one bare and one screened vertex, never two screened ones (Section A.6).
  2. Only the dynamic part of phonon screening may be added. The static part of the electron bubble in the phonon self-energy $\Pi^{\mathrm{ph}}$ is already inside the density-functional frequencies. Only the remainder $\Pi^{\mathrm{ph}}(\omega)-\Pi^{\mathrm{ph}}(0)$ may be added, the nonadiabatic part that arises because the electrons do not follow the nuclei instantly. This is the split $\Pi^{\mathrm{ph}}=\Pi^{\mathrm{ph,A}}+\Pi^{\mathrm{ph,NA}}$ of Section A.6, .
  3. Kohn–Sham states inside bubbles are allowed. The bubbles of the random-phase approximation, and the screened interaction $W$ built from them, may be computed with Kohn–Sham bands and states, as in standard GW calculations. The states enter only as a basis; the screening of the explicit interaction $v$ that the bubbles produce is not contained in the one-electron energies.

The range rule: short-range force constants only

A second question is which part of the force constants the starting phonons should contain. Section A.4 splits the force constants $\Phi$ into a short-range part $\Phi^{\mathrm{sr}}$, whose Fourier transform is analytic at $\Gamma$ (it has a power series in $\bq$ there), and the dipole–dipole part $\Phi^{\mathrm{dd}}$, whose transform is not. The EPP manuscript models the density-functional force constants as strictly short-ranged:

$$\begin{gathered}\Phi_{\ell\kappa\alpha,\ell'\kappa'\beta}\simeq\Phi^{\mathrm{sr}}_{\ell\kappa\alpha,\ell'\kappa'\beta}=0\\ \text{when }\big|\btau_{\ell\kappa}-\btau_{\ell'\kappa'}\big|\\ \text{exceeds the size of a cell.}\end{gathered} \tag{B1.7}$$

In a polar crystal a displacement creates a dipole whose field reaches far beyond one cell. The EPP manuscript adds this long-range coupling explicitly: through electrostatics below, and through the photon field from Section B.2 on. The condition for doing so without double counting is the rule that governs Part B: only short-range (analytic) force constants may be combined with an explicitly mediated long-range coupling. If the phonons already contain the dipole–dipole force constants $\Phi^{\mathrm{dd}}$, , the exchange of photons counts them a second time.8

Density-functional force constants are not short-ranged

The EPP manuscript attributes the short range to density-functional theory itself, which it says neglects long-range electronic correlations. That does not hold for density-functional perturbation theory. In polar insulators its force constants carry a dipole–dipole tail that falls off as the inverse cube of the distance, whose Fourier transform is the nonanalytic LO–TO term (Gonze and Lee), and its vertex carries the $1/q$ Fröhlich singularity (Section A.4). What is true is narrower: a periodic calculation at exactly $\bq=0$ lacks the macroscopic electric field, which codes add back through the Born charges and the electronic dielectric tensor $\boldsymbol\varepsilon_\infty$ of Section A.4. In practice the starting phonons of Part B should come from the analytic part $\Phi^{\mathrm{sr}}$ of the computed force constants, with $\Phi^{\mathrm{dd}}$ subtracted; the exchange of photons then regenerates it (Section B.7, Section B.9). The van der Waals interaction, a long-range correlation in the usual sense, is a separate matter.

Polar coupling from Born charges

In a polar crystal the atoms carry charges of opposite sign. Moving an ion displaces charge, and its cell acquires a dipole. The potential of a dipole falls off as $1/r^2$, far more slowly than the screened short-range part of the change of potential, and in Fourier space it grows as $1/q$: this is the long-range, Fröhlich part of the coupling (Section A.4; draws the dipoles of a longitudinal and a transverse wave). The size of the dipole is set by the Born effective charge tensor $\mathbf Z^*_\kappa$, : $eZ^*_{\kappa,\alpha\beta}$ is the dipole along $\alpha$ created per unit displacement of atom $\kappa$ along $\beta$, with the electrons following the displacement and the macroscopic electric field held at zero. The EPP manuscript treats the dipoles with electrostatics, in a medium that the valence electrons polarize, and then maps the result onto the bare vertex.9

The potential of one dipole in a polarizable medium

  1. The displaced atom $\ell\kappa$ carries the dipole

    $$\begin{gathered}\bp_{\ell\kappa}=e\,\mathbf Z^*_\kappa\cdot\bu_{\ell\kappa},\\ p_{\ell\kappa\alpha}=e\sum_\beta Z^*_{\kappa,\alpha\beta}\,u_{\ell\kappa\beta},\end{gathered} \tag{B1.8}$$

    located at the atom's equilibrium position $\btau_{\ell\kappa}$.

    This is the definition of the Born charge, to first order in the displacement. The dipole sits where the atom is, not at the corner of its cell; the EPP manuscript misses exactly this point, and the basis phase below depends on it. $\bp$ is a dipole moment, charge times length ($e$ Å), not a momentum.

  2. A point dipole $\bp$ at the origin is the limit of two opposite point charges at $\pm\mathbf s/2$, as their separation $\mathbf s$ shrinks while the charge times $\mathbf s$ stays equal to $\bp$. Its charge density is $-\bp\cdot\nabla\delta(\br)$.

    To first order in $\mathbf s$, $\delta(\br\mp\mathbf s/2)=\delta(\br)\mp\frac12\mathbf s\cdot\nabla\delta(\br)$. The positive charge at $+\mathbf s/2$ minus the negative one at $-\mathbf s/2$ leaves the charge times $-\mathbf s\cdot\nabla\delta(\br)$, which is $-\bp\cdot\nabla\delta(\br)$.

  3. The field of the dipole polarizes the valence electrons around it, and Poisson's equation in the medium reads

    $$\nabla\cdot\big(\boldsymbol\varepsilon_\infty\cdot\nabla A_0\big)=4\pi\,\bp\cdot\nabla\delta(\br) . \tag{B1.9}$$
    Here $A_0$ is the electrostatic (scalar) potential, so that the electric field is $\bE=-\nabla A_0$; most textbooks write it $\phi$, which in this primer is the phonon field. The electrons, with the ions clamped, add the polarization $\bP$$\,=\frac{\boldsymbol\varepsilon_\infty-1}{4\pi}\cdot\bE$, whose bound charge $-\nabla\cdot\bP$ joins that of the dipole. Gauss's law, $\nabla\cdot\bE=4\pi(-\bp\cdot\nabla\delta-\nabla\cdot\bP)$, becomes $\nabla\cdot(\boldsymbol\varepsilon_\infty\cdot\bE)=-4\pi\,\bp\cdot\nabla\delta$, which is . The clamped-ion dielectric tensor $\boldsymbol\varepsilon_\infty$ may be added here because the Born charge was defined at zero macroscopic field, so it contains no screening by the macroscopic field yet.

  4. Fourier transform, $A_0(\br)=\int\frac{\dd^3q}{(2\pi)^3}\,\ee^{\ii\bq\cdot\br}A_0(\bq)$. Each gradient becomes $\ii\bq$ and $\delta(\br)$ becomes 1, so $-(\bq\cdot\boldsymbol\varepsilon_\infty\cdot\bq)\,A_0(\bq)=4\pi\ii\,\bq\cdot\bp$, and

    $$A_0(\bq)=-4\pi\ii\,\frac{\bq\cdot\bp}{\bq\cdot\boldsymbol\varepsilon_\infty\cdot\bq} . \tag{B1.10}$$
    Check: in vacuum, $\boldsymbol\varepsilon_\infty=1$, this is the transform of the familiar $\bp\cdot\br/r^3=-\bp\cdot\nabla(1/r)$, because the transform of $1/r$ is $4\pi/q^2$ and $-\bp\cdot\nabla$ brings down $-\ii\bq\cdot\bp$.

Two properties of drive everything that follows.10

  • It is of order $1/q$, and $q$ times it tends to a limit that depends on the direction $\hat\bq=\bq/q$. The $1/q$ is the Fourier image of a potential that decays as $1/r^2$. A function whose limit at $\bq=0$ depends on the direction of approach has no power series there: it is nonanalytic, as in Section A.4.
  • It vanishes when $\bp\perp\bq$. Only the longitudinal component of a wave of dipoles produces a scalar potential. A transverse wave of dipoles produces a transverse electric field, which is a photon field and invisible to electrostatics (Section B.4, Section B.6).

Derivation: the potential of a dipole in an anisotropic medium, in real space

  1. For a unit point charge at the origin, $\nabla\cdot(\boldsymbol\varepsilon_\infty\cdot\nabla A_0)=-4\pi\,\delta(\br)$. Change variables to $\br=\boldsymbol\varepsilon_\infty^{1/2}\cdot\mathbf x$.

    Then $\nabla_{\br}=\boldsymbol\varepsilon_\infty^{-1/2}\cdot\nabla_{\mathbf x}$, so the operator becomes the ordinary Laplacian in $\mathbf x$, and $\delta(\br)=\delta(\mathbf x)/\sqrt{\det\boldsymbol\varepsilon_\infty}$.

Physical picture: a fictitious charge on every atom

The map says that, for the electron, a polar crystal acts as if every atom carried a point charge $-e$, like the electron's own, displaced by $-\mathbf Z^*_\kappa\cdot\bu_{\ell\kappa}$. Such a displaced charge is exactly the dipole $e\mathbf Z^*_\kappa\cdot\bu_{\ell\kappa}$, and it repels the electron through the Coulomb interaction screened by the electrons of the medium, $4\pi e^2/(\bq\cdot\boldsymbol\varepsilon_\infty\cdot\bq)$. The polar vertex is the bare vertex of this fictitious lattice.

Two remarks place in the manuscript's plan. First, $\boldsymbol\varepsilon_\infty$ was put in by hand. Second, the frequency. The manuscript's phonons come from short-range force constants, so for a polar mode $\omega_{\bq\nu}$ in is close to the transverse optical frequency. Why the basis phase is needed

Which atoms make up one cell is a choice of bookkeeping. In GaAs the Ga atom at the corner of a cell can be grouped with any of its four As neighbours, or with an As atom several cells away; the crystal is the same. Nothing physical may depend on that choice. The phase convention of Section A.1, however, attaches the phase of a wave to the cell, so assigning an atom to a different cell changes the phase of its eigenvector component. draws a chain of GaAs planes along [111] booked in two ways and computes the polar vertex with and without the basis phase.

  1. Assign atom $\kappa$ to a neighbouring cell. Its position in the cell changes by a lattice vector, and the atom that was labelled as belonging to cell $\ell$ is now labelled as belonging to the cell at $\bR_\ell-\bR_{\ell_0}$:

    $$\begin{gathered}\btau_\kappa\to\btau_\kappa+\bR_{\ell_0},\\ \bR_\ell\to\bR_\ell-\bR_{\ell_0}\\ \text{(for atom }\kappa\text{ only)} .\end{gathered} \tag{B1.14}$$
    The physical position $\bR_\ell+\btau_\kappa$ of every atom stays the same; only the labels change. $\bR_{\ell_0}$ is a fixed lattice vector, and the other atoms of the basis keep their labels.

  2. The eigenvector component of atom $\kappa$ picks up a phase: $e_{\kappa\alpha,\nu}(\bq)\to\ee^{\ii\bq\cdot\bR_{\ell_0}}\,e_{\kappa\alpha,\nu}(\bq)$.

    The displacement operator gives atom $\kappa$ the phase $\ee^{\ii\bq\cdot\bR_\ell}$ of its cell. Its physical displacement does not change, so the new phase $\ee^{\ii\bq\cdot(\bR_\ell-\bR_{\ell_0})}$ must be compensated by the factor $\ee^{\ii\bq\cdot\bR_{\ell_0}}$ in the eigenvector. In the dynamical matrix , each force constant now carries the phase of the partner's new cell: row $\kappa$ is multiplied by $\ee^{\ii\bq\cdot\bR_{\ell_0}}$ and column $\kappa$ by $\ee^{-\ii\bq\cdot\bR_{\ell_0}}$. The frequencies do not change, and the eigenvectors change as stated.

  3. The product that enters and does not change:

    $$\begin{gathered}e_{\kappa\alpha,\nu}(\bq)\,\ee^{-\ii(\bq+\bG)\cdot\btau_\kappa}\\ \to\ \ee^{\ii\bq\cdot\bR_{\ell_0}}\,e_{\kappa\alpha,\nu}(\bq)\\ \times\ee^{-\ii(\bq+\bG)\cdot(\btau_\kappa+\bR_{\ell_0})}\\ =e_{\kappa\alpha,\nu}(\bq)\,\ee^{-\ii(\bq+\bG)\cdot\btau_\kappa} .\end{gathered} \tag{B1.15}$$
    The two new phases leave $\ee^{-\ii\bG\cdot\bR_{\ell_0}}$, which equals 1 for every reciprocal lattice vector.

  4. Without the basis phase, the term of atom $\kappa$ in the sum over atoms turns by $\ee^{\ii\bq\cdot\bR_{\ell_0}}$, while the terms of the other atoms do not. The sum, and with it $|g^{\mathrm{lr}}|$, then changes with the bookkeeping.

    A phase common to all terms would only change the overall phase of $g$, which is not observable. A phase on one term changes how the terms interfere.

The basis phase therefore cannot be absorbed into the eigenvector: the eigenvector's phase is tied to the assignment of atoms to cells, and only the product is physical. The phase-less form would be correct only for eigenvectors defined with the phase at the atom, $u_{\ell\kappa\alpha}\propto\ee^{\ii\bq\cdot\btau_{\ell\kappa}}$, and even then only for $\bG=0$, because the factor $\ee^{-\ii\bG\cdot\btau_\kappa}$ would remain. The derivation of produced the phase without any extra assumption, because each dipole was placed at its atom.13

Where the phase matters

At $\bG=0$ only the relative phases $\ee^{-\ii\bq\cdot(\btau_\kappa-\btau_{\kappa'})}$ between atoms are observable, and they tend to 1 as $q\to0$. For optical modes nothing then changes in the limit: neither Fröhlich's vertex, nor the direction dependence of , nor the LO–TO splitting, nor the polariton region of Sections B.6 and B.7, where $q\,|\btau_\kappa-\btau_{\kappa'}|$ is of order $10^{-4}$. The phase matters in three places.

  1. Finite $q$ in cells with more than one atom. In GaAs the As atom sits 2.448 Å from the Ga atom along [111], so the phase difference is 0.24 rad at $q=0.1$ Å⁻¹ and 1.22 rad at 0.5 Å⁻¹. In the chain of the phase-less $|g^{\mathrm{lr}}|^2$ of the LO mode is off by 0.5–0.8% at 0.1 Å⁻¹ and by about 20% at 0.5 Å⁻¹, up for one choice of cell and down for the other.
  2. Every term with $\bG\neq0$, where $\ee^{-\ii\bG\cdot\btau_\kappa}$ survives even at $\bq=0$.
  3. Acoustic modes, where the phase removes a spurious coupling (next subsection).

The long-wave expansion of the vertex

Near the zone centre the vertex simplifies, and the simplified form shows its structure. Take $q$ small compared with the size of the Brillouin zone and with $1/|\btau_\kappa-\btau_{\kappa'}|$. Keep only the $\bG=0$ term of , the only one that grows as $q\to0$, and take transitions within one band, for which the overlap tends to $\delta_{mn}$.

Optical modes: a function of direction divided by $q$

  1. Set the basis phases to 1 and the eigenvectors to their values at $\bq=0$.

    Both corrections are of relative order $q$. The starting phonons come from analytic force constants, so their eigenvectors at $\bq=0$ are well defined.

  2. Write $\bq=q\hat\bq$ in the $\bG=0$ term. One power of $q$ cancels between numerator and denominator:

    $$\begin{gathered}g^{\mathrm{lr}}_{mn\nu}(\bk,\bq)\\ \simeq\frac{4\pi\ii e}{\OV\,q}\,\frac{\hat\bq\cdot\bp_\nu}{\hat\bq\cdot\boldsymbol\varepsilon_\infty\cdot\hat\bq}\,\delta_{mn},\\ \bp_\nu=e\sum_\kappa\Big(\frac{\hbar}{2M_\kappa\omega_{\mathbf 0\nu}}\Big)^{1/2}\\ \times\mathbf Z^*_\kappa\cdot\be_{\kappa\nu}(\mathbf 0) .\end{gathered} \tag{B1.16}$$
    $\bp_\nu$ collects the atom sum: it is the dipole of one cell when every atom is displaced by its zero-point amplitude in the optical mode $\nu$ at $\Gamma$ ($e$ Å).

The vertex is $1/q$ times a function of the direction $\hat\bq$ alone, nonanalytic at $\Gamma$, as the dipole potential predicted; plots $q\,|g^{\mathrm{lr}}|$ against the direction. Physically, is the potential energy of an electron in the longitudinal macroscopic field of the wave, , created by the polarization $\bp_\nu/\OV$.

A cubic crystal with two atoms per cell gives the classic check. Take $\mathbf Z^*_1=-\mathbf Z^*_2=Z^*$ times the unit tensor, a scalar $\varepsilon_\infty$, and the LO eigenvector $\be_1=(M_2/M_{\mathrm c})^{1/2}\hat\bq$, $\be_2=-(M_1/M_{\mathrm c})^{1/2}\hat\bq$, where $M_{\mathrm c}$$\,=M_1+M_2$ is the mass of the cell. The dipole is then $\bp_\nu=eZ^*(\hbar/2M_{\mathrm r}\omega_{\mathrm{LO}})^{1/2}\hat\bq$, with the reduced mass $M_{\mathrm r}$ and the longitudinal optical frequency $\omega_{\mathrm{LO}}$, and gives

$$\big|g^{\mathrm{lr}}\big|\simeq\frac{4\pi e^2}{\OV\,q}\,\frac{Z^*}{\varepsilon_\infty}\Big(\frac{\hbar}{2M_{\mathrm r}\omega_{\mathrm{LO}}}\Big)^{1/2} .$$

Acoustic modes: the phase removes a spurious coupling

For an acoustic mode the leading term, , vanishes, and the first correction is where the basis phase matters most. In a long acoustic wave every atom moves with the local displacement of the wave at its own position, proportional to $\ee^{\ii\bq\cdot(\bR_\ell+\btau_\kappa)}$, plus a small relative displacement of the sublattices. With the phase on the cell, the eigenvector therefore carries the basis phase itself:

$$\begin{aligned}\be_{\kappa\nu}(\bq)={}&\ee^{\ii\bq\cdot\btau_\kappa}\Big[\Big(\frac{M_\kappa}{M_{\mathrm c}}\Big)^{1/2}\hat\bd\\&+\ii\,\mathbf w_{\kappa\nu}(\bq)+O(q^2)\Big] .\end{aligned} \tag{B1.17}$$

unit vector along the motion of the acoustic wave at $\bq\to0$, the direction of the rigid translation $\bd$ of Section A.1
$\mathbf w_{\kappa\nu}(\bq)$
internal strain: the mass-weighted relative displacement of sublattice $\kappa$ that the strain of the wave induces; real, of order $q$, and independent of the choice of cell (dimensionless)

  1. Insert into the atom sum of at $\bG=0$, with the basis phase:

    $$\begin{aligned}&\sum_\kappa\frac{\mathbf Z^*_\kappa\cdot\be_{\kappa\nu}(\bq)}{\sqrt{M_\kappa}}\,\ee^{-\ii\bq\cdot\btau_\kappa}\\&\ =\ii\sum_\kappa\frac{\mathbf Z^*_\kappa\cdot\mathbf w_{\kappa\nu}(\bq)}{\sqrt{M_\kappa}}\\&\ \ +O(q^2) .\end{aligned} \tag{B1.18}$$
    The basis phase cancels the eigenvector's. The leading term is $\big(\sum_\kappa\mathbf Z^*_\kappa\big)\cdot\hat\bd/\sqrt{M_{\mathrm c}}$, which vanishes by the sum rule of the Born charges, : a rigid translation of the crystal creates no dipole.   2. Without the basis phase, $\ee^{\ii\bq\cdot\btau_\kappa}=1+\ii\bq\cdot\btau_\kappa+\dots$ survives in , and the same sum acquires an extra term:

    $$\begin{aligned}&\sum_\kappa\frac{\mathbf Z^*_\kappa\cdot\be_{\kappa\nu}(\bq)}{\sqrt{M_\kappa}}\\&\ =\ii\sum_\kappa\frac{\mathbf Z^*_\kappa\cdot\mathbf w_{\kappa\nu}(\bq)}{\sqrt{M_\kappa}}\\&\ \ +\frac{\ii}{\sqrt{M_{\mathrm c}}}\sum_\kappa(\bq\cdot\btau_\kappa)\,\mathbf Z^*_\kappa\cdot\hat\bd\\&\ \ +O(q^2) .\end{aligned} \tag{B1.19}$$
    The product of $\ii\bq\cdot\btau_\kappa$ with the leading term of the bracket no longer sums to zero, because each Born charge is weighted by its own position.

The extra term is spurious. It changes when an atom is assigned to another cell, since $\btau_\kappa\to\btau_\kappa+\bR_{\ell_0}$ adds $\ii(\bq\cdot\bR_{\ell_0})\,\mathbf Z^*_\kappa\cdot\hat\bd/\sqrt{M_{\mathrm c}}$. It also survives in crystals with a centre of inversion, where the physical term vanishes: an atom on an inversion centre has no internal strain, and two atoms related by inversion have equal Born charges and opposite internal strains.

Both terms are of order $q$. With $\omega_{\bq\nu}\propto q$, then gives $|g^{\mathrm{lr}}|^2\propto1/q$, the scaling of a piezoelectric coupling, in which a strain creates a polarization. In GaAs the spurious term alone corresponds to a piezoelectric constant of 0.6 to 1.8 C/m² along [111], depending on the cell, and of 1.04 C/m² along [100], where the true coupling vanishes by symmetry. The corrected vertex keeps only the internal-strain dipole, about 1.2 C/m² along [111] in a central-force model. Neither is the measured value, about 0.19 C/m²: in the real crystal the response of the electrons at fixed ions largely cancels the internal-strain term. That response enters the long-range potential through the dynamical quadrupoles, the quadrupole moments that the displacement of an atom induces, the next term after the Born dipole in the multipole expansion of its charge. A consistent long-range vertex at order $q$ needs those quadrupoles, which neither source includes.15

This is electrostatics

The dipole potential is the instantaneous Coulomb field, valid when the finite speed of light can be ignored. That requires $q\gg\sqrt{\varepsilon_\infty}\,\omega/c$, far outside the light cone: for optical phonons the bound is of order $10^{-4}$ Å⁻¹ ($6\times10^{-5}$ Å⁻¹ for GaAs, $2\times10^{-4}$ Å⁻¹ for the in-plane mode of hexagonal boron nitride), against a Brillouin zone of about 1 Å⁻¹ (Section X.1).16 A transverse wave of dipoles, invisible to , still produces a transverse electric field. That field matters only near the light cone, where transverse optical phonons and photons mix into polaritons (Section B.6). Section B.8 derives the electrodynamic vertex; its limit $c\to\infty$, , has the form of the $\bG=0$ term of with $\boldsymbol\varepsilon_\infty$ replaced by the electronic dielectric function at the phonon frequency. Where that function is real, it is exactly the complex conjugate of that term: the same coupling, written for the reverse transition.

The rule of this section, short-range phonons plus an explicit long-range coupling, is what makes that construction free of double counting.

Check yourself: three short questions

  1. Why does a transverse acoustic wave not couple through the $\bG=0$ term of the bare vertex , and how can it couple at all?

    Answer. At $\bG=0$ the projection is $\bq\cdot\be_{\kappa\nu}(\bq)$, which vanishes when the atoms move perpendicular to $\bq$: a shear does not change the density of ions at long wavelength. The umklapp terms have the projection $(\bq+\bG)\cdot\be_{\kappa\nu}$, which is not zero in general, so a transverse wave couples through them.

  2. A calculation takes its phonons from density-functional perturbation theory and adds the full electron bubble to the phonon self-energy, at all frequencies. What is counted twice, and what should be added instead?

    Answer. The static part of the bubble, the adiabatic screening of the ions by the electrons, is already inside the density-functional frequencies. Only the dynamic remainder $\Pi^{\mathrm{ph}}(\omega)-\Pi^{\mathrm{ph}}(0)$ may be added, with one bare and one screened vertex in the bubble.

  3. In a crystal with two atoms per cell, atom 2 is assigned to a neighbouring cell so that its position in the cell becomes $\btau_2+\ba_1$, the relabelling with $\bR_{\ell_0}=\ba_1$. By what factor does $e_{2\alpha,\nu}(\bq)$ change, and why does not change?

    Answer. By $\ee^{\ii\bq\cdot\ba_1}$, step 2 of the derivation of : the atom's cell corner moves back by $\ba_1$, and the eigenvector must restore the phase $\ee^{\ii\bq\cdot\ba_1}$ that the cell lost. At the same time $\btau_2\to\btau_2+\ba_1$, so the basis phase gains $\ee^{-\ii(\bq+\bG)\cdot\ba_1}$. The product gains $\ee^{-\ii\bG\cdot\ba_1}=1$, so each term of the sum, and the vertex, is unchanged.
