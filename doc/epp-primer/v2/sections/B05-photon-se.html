Free propagators and the photon self-energy

Section B.4 wrote the crystal as free electrons, phonons and transverse photons plus the couplings between them. This section builds the free lines from which every diagram of Part B is made, and then dresses the photon with the response of matter, the photon self-energy. Electrons and vibrating ions each contribute a current–current term and a contact term, and gauge invariance ties the two together. The ionic part can be computed exactly; at long wavelengths, together with the electronic part, it gives the infrared dielectric tensor of a polar crystal, which Section B.3 obtained from a classical model.

The picture: light dressed by matter

In vacuum a photon of wavevector $\bq$ is a free oscillator of frequency $\omega_q=cq$, where $c$ is the speed of light. In a crystal it does not stay free. An electron absorbs it and is lifted across the gap, then falls back and gives it up again. Or the photon sets a polar lattice vibration ringing, and the vibration returns the energy. Between such events the photon travels as in vacuum. The sum over all these histories is light that travels more slowly than $c$ and is absorbed at the frequencies where the crystal has excitations of its own.

Many-body theory describes this with two kinds of objects. A propagator, or Green's function, gives the amplitude for a disturbance created at one point and time to arrive at another; for a system without interactions it is called a free line, after the lines of a Feynman diagram. A self-energy collects every process by which the disturbance turns into something else and back, each process counted once. For the photon, the propagator is $D$ and the self-energy is $\Pi$, both met in Section X.1. The self-energy has a direct physical meaning: it is the current that matter produces per unit vector potential acting on it. The dressed propagator $D$, with all these processes included, follows from the free one by a Dyson equation, the same algebra by which Section A.5 built the screened interaction $W$ from the bare Coulomb interaction $v$.

The free phonon line

Section A.1 showed that each phonon mode $\bq\nu$ is an independent harmonic oscillator of frequency $\omega_{\bq\nu}$, and that every displacement the mode produces is proportional to the dimensionless phonon field $\phi_{\bq\nu}=a_{\bq\nu}+a^\dagger_{-\bq\nu}$, where $a_{\bq\nu}$ and $a^\dagger_{\bq\nu}$ remove and add a phonon . In the free Hamiltonian $H_0$ of Section B.4 these phonons come from short-range force constants only. They are bare phonons: the long-range dipole–dipole forces of a polar crystal, which split the longitudinal and transverse optical frequencies (Section A.4), are left out of $H_0$, because the field restores them (Section B.7).2

The phonon propagator describes how a disturbance of one mode evolves in time. For an oscillator the answer is a ringing at the frequencies $\pm\omega_{\bq\nu}$. Two points need care: the two signs of the frequency, which an infinitesimal imaginary part keeps apart, and the thermal occupation of the mode.

The calculation uses the interaction picture, in which every operator evolves with $H_0$ alone, $O(t)=\ee^{\ii H_0t/\hbar}\,O\,\ee^{-\ii H_0t/\hbar}$. For the phonon operators this is the motion of a free oscillator, $a_{\bq\nu}(t)=a_{\bq\nu}\,\ee^{-\ii\omega_{\bq\nu}t}$ and $a^\dagger_{\bq\nu}(t)=a^\dagger_{\bq\nu}\,\ee^{\ii\omega_{\bq\nu}t}$. Section A.5 defined the phonon propagator $F$ . For free phonons at temperature $T$, written as a time-ordered function, it reads

$$F^{\mathcal T}_\nu(\bq,t)=-\frac{\ii}{\hbar}\,\big\langle\mathcal T\,\phi_{\bq\nu}(t)\,\phi_{-\bq\nu}(0)\big\rangle . \tag{B5.1}$$

Time-ordered and retarded functions; linear response skip if familiar

Two operators $\hat X$ and $\hat Y$ can be correlated in time in two standard ways. The time-ordered function $-\frac{\ii}{\hbar}\langle\mathcal T\hat X(t)\hat Y(0)\rangle$ puts the operator with the later time on the left: it is $-\frac{\ii}{\hbar}\langle\hat X(t)\hat Y(0)\rangle$ for $t\gt0$ and $-\frac{\ii}{\hbar}\langle\hat Y(0)\hat X(t)\rangle$ for $t\lt0$. Perturbation theory produces time-ordered functions, because the time-evolution operator orders the interactions in time and Wick's theorem then breaks the averages into pairs. The retarded function $-\frac{\ii}{\hbar}\theta(t)\langle[\hat X(t),\hat Y(0)]\rangle$, with the unit step $\theta(t)=1$ for $t\gt0$ and 0 otherwise, vanishes before $t=0$: it is causal.

Retarded functions describe response. Add a weak perturbation $-f(t)\,\hat Y$ to the Hamiltonian. To first order in $f$ the average of $\hat X$ changes by (the Kubo formula)

$$\begin{gathered}\delta\langle\hat X\rangle(t)\\ =\frac{\ii}{\hbar}\int\dd t'\,\theta(t-t')\,\big\langle[\hat X(t),\hat Y(t')]\big\rangle\,f(t') .\end{gathered}$$

In the frequency domain the integral $\int_0^\infty\dd t\,\ee^{\ii\omega t}$ converges once $\omega$ is replaced by $\omega+\ii\eta$ with $\eta\to0^+$. A retarded function is therefore analytic in the upper half of the complex $\omega$ plane, and all its poles lie just below the real axis. At zero temperature a time-ordered function has its poles at negative frequencies mirrored above the axis, and for $\hat Y=\hat X^\dagger$ the two functions coincide at positive frequencies. At $T\gt0$ a time-ordered function is analytic in neither half plane: for the free phonon line below, each pole gains a thermal partner on the other side of the axis, weighted by the Bose–Einstein occupation of the mode (). The identity $\frac{1}{x\pm\ii\eta}=\mathrm P\frac1x\mp\ii\pi\delta(x)$, where P takes the principal value, separates real and imaginary parts, as in Section A.6.

At finite temperature the systematic method, the Matsubara formalism of Section A.7, works on an interval of imaginary time of length $\hbar/\kB T$, on which boson functions are periodic. Their Fourier components live at the bosonic Matsubara frequencies $\omega^{\mathrm B}_p=2p\pi\kB T/\hbar$, with $p$ an integer, and the replacement $\ii\omega^{\mathrm B}_p\to\omega+\ii\eta$ turns them into the retarded function. For the free phonon line the Matsubara function is $-\frac{1}{\hbar}\,2\omega_{\bq\nu}/[(\omega^{\mathrm B}_p)^2+\omega^2_{\bq\nu}]$, and the replacement gives below. The temperature enters only through which frequencies $\omega^{\mathrm B}_p$ are sampled, not through the function.

Evaluating takes three steps.

  1. For $t\gt0$ the order is $\phi_{\bq\nu}(t)\,\phi_{-\bq\nu}(0)$. Insert $\phi_{\bq\nu}(t)=a_{\bq\nu}\ee^{-\ii\omega_{\bq\nu}t}+a^\dagger_{-\bq\nu}\ee^{\ii\omega_{\bq\nu}t}$ and $\phi_{-\bq\nu}(0)=a_{-\bq\nu}+a^\dagger_{\bq\nu}$. Only the products that remove and restore the same phonon survive the average:

    $$\begin{gathered}\big\langle\phi_{\bq\nu}(t)\,\phi_{-\bq\nu}(0)\big\rangle\\ =\big(1+n_{\bq\nu}\big)\,\ee^{-\ii\omega_{\bq\nu}t}+n_{\bq\nu}\,\ee^{\ii\omega_{\bq\nu}t}\qquad(t\gt0).\end{gathered}$$
    A thermal state is diagonal in the phonon numbers, so only $\langle a_{\bq\nu}a^\dagger_{\bq\nu}\rangle=1+n_{\bq\nu}$ and $\langle a^\dagger_{-\bq\nu}a_{-\bq\nu}\rangle=n_{\bq\nu}$ remain, with the Bose–Einstein occupation $n_{\bq\nu}$. The mode at $-\bq$ has the same frequency and occupation (Section A.1).

  2. For $t\lt0$ the order is reversed, and the same pairs give $\langle\phi_{-\bq\nu}(0)\,\phi_{\bq\nu}(t)\rangle$ $=(1+n_{\bq\nu})\,\ee^{\ii\omega_{\bq\nu}t}+n_{\bq\nu}\,\ee^{-\ii\omega_{\bq\nu}t}$.

    Now $a_{-\bq\nu}$ stands to the left of $a^\dagger_{-\bq\nu}(t)$, which gives $1+n_{\bq\nu}$ with the phase $\ee^{\ii\omega_{\bq\nu}t}$, and $a^\dagger_{\bq\nu}$ stands to the left of $a_{\bq\nu}(t)$, which gives $n_{\bq\nu}$.

  3. Transform to frequency with $\int\dd t\,\ee^{\ii\omega t}$. The integral over each half of the time axis converges once a factor $\ee^{-\eta|t|}$ is included, with $\eta\to0^+$ the positive infinitesimal of retarded functions (Section A.6):

    $$\begin{gathered}\int_0^\infty\!\dd t\,\ee^{\ii(\omega\mp\omega_{\bq\nu})t-\eta t}=\frac{\ii}{\omega\mp\omega_{\bq\nu}+\ii\eta},\\ \int_{-\infty}^0\!\dd t\,\ee^{\ii(\omega\pm\omega_{\bq\nu})t+\eta t}=\frac{-\ii}{\omega\pm\omega_{\bq\nu}-\ii\eta}.\end{gathered}$$
    The sign of the infinitesimal records which half of the time axis a term came from. The four terms combine in pairs, for example $\frac{1}{\omega-\omega_{\bq\nu}+\ii\eta}-\frac{1}{\omega+\omega_{\bq\nu}-\ii\eta}=\frac{2\omega_{\bq\nu}}{\omega^2-\omega^2_{\bq\nu}+\ii\eta}$, where the new positive infinitesimal $2\omega_{\bq\nu}\eta$ is again called $\eta$.

The result is

$$\begin{aligned}\hbar F^{\mathcal T}_\nu(\bq,\omega)={}&\frac{2\omega_{\bq\nu}\,\big(1+n_{\bq\nu}\big)}{\omega^2-\omega^2_{\bq\nu}+\ii\eta}\\&-\frac{2\omega_{\bq\nu}\,n_{\bq\nu}}{\omega^2-\omega^2_{\bq\nu}-\ii\eta} .\end{aligned} \tag{B5.2}$$

The first term carries the factor $1+n_{\bq\nu}$ of adding a phonon to the mode, the second the factor $n_{\bq\nu}$ of removing one. Split each denominator with $\frac{1}{x\pm\ii\eta}=\mathrm P\frac1x\mp\ii\pi\delta(x)$, where P takes the principal value. The real parts combine with the weight $(1+n_{\bq\nu})-n_{\bq\nu}=1$, so they do not depend on temperature. The imaginary parts add instead, $\mathrm{Im}\,\hbar F^{\mathcal T}_\nu=-\pi\,(1+2n_{\bq\nu})\,2\omega_{\bq\nu}\,\delta(\omega^2-\omega^2_{\bq\nu})$. The time-ordered line knows the temperature, but only through its imaginary part, and through the factor $1+2n_{\bq\nu}$ that also sets the thermal spread of the displacements (Section A.1).

A response, the quantity that an experiment or a classical equation of motion gives, is a retarded function: it is nonzero only after the disturbance. For the mode it is built from a commutator instead of a time-ordered product:

$$\begin{aligned}&F^0_\nu(\bq,t)\\&\quad=-\frac{\ii}{\hbar}\,\theta(t)\,\big\langle\big[\phi_{\bq\nu}(t),\phi_{-\bq\nu}(0)\big]\big\rangle\\&\quad=-\frac{\ii}{\hbar}\,\theta(t)\,\big(\ee^{-\ii\omega_{\bq\nu}t}-\ee^{\ii\omega_{\bq\nu}t}\big) .\end{aligned} \tag{B5.3}$$

Here $\theta(t)$ is the unit step. The second line holds because the commutator, $[a_{\bq\nu},a^\dagger_{\bq\nu}]\,\ee^{-\ii\omega_{\bq\nu}t}+[a^\dagger_{-\bq\nu},a_{-\bq\nu}]\,\ee^{\ii\omega_{\bq\nu}t}$, is a number: its average is the same in every state of the mode, at every temperature. Transforming with the first integral of step 3 gives the free phonon line used in the rest of Part B:

$$F^0_\nu(\bq,\omega)=\frac{1}{\hbar}\,\frac{2\omega_{\bq\nu}}{(\omega+\ii\eta)^2-\omega^2_{\bq\nu}} . \tag{B5.4}$$

Temperature: the rule $n_{\mathrm B}=0$

The EPP manuscript does all its perturbation theory with zero-temperature, time-ordered functions, which are simpler, and states a rule that brings the temperature in:4

  1. every boson line, phonon or photon, is used at $T=0$: the occupations $n_{\bq\nu}$ in are set to zero;
  2. every electron bubble, a free electron–hole pair that a boson line creates and destroys again (the polarizability diagram of Section A.5), keeps the Fermi–Dirac occupations $f_{n\bk}$ of the actual temperature;
  3. at the end the infinitesimal is placed as in a retarded function, $\omega\to\omega+\ii\eta$.

The rule works for the quantities of Part B because a measurement, or a classical response calculation, needs the retarded function. For a harmonic boson that function does not depend on temperature, since it is built from a commutator that is a number, and the zero-temperature time-ordered line equals it at all positive frequencies; the third item of the rule repairs the negative ones. The electron bubble depends on temperature only through its occupations: its retarded form is a sum over pairs of states with $f_{n\bk}$ at temperature $T$, below. The Dyson equations built from these retarded pieces have the same form as the time-ordered ones, as the self-consistent-field derivation below shows.

Where the rule holds, and where it does not

It holds for the free lines, for the photon self-energies of this section (an electron bubble, or one harmonic phonon line between two vertices) and for Dyson series built from them. It fails when a boson line runs inside a fermion self-energy. In the Fan–Migdal self-energy of Section A.7 the phonon occupations $n_{\bq\nu}$ appear in the numerators, next to the $f_{n\bk}$, and produce the temperature dependence of band gaps (Section A.8); setting them to zero would remove it. It also fails for anharmonic phonons, whose commutators are no longer numbers. Following the third item of the rule, the spectra in the later figures of this section use retarded denominators with a damping rate $\gamma$, $\omega^2-\omega_0^2+\ii\gamma\omega$ for an oscillator of frequency $\omega_0$; a time-ordered $+\ii\eta$ would give the wrong sign of the imaginary part at negative frequencies, as shows.

The free transverse photon line

  • It is classical electrodynamics.   • Static currents. At $\omega=0$, $D^{0\mathrm T}=\frac{4\pi}{cq^2}(\delta_{\alpha\beta}-\hat q_\alpha\hat q_\beta)$. This is the Biot–Savart law, $\bA=4\pi\bj/(cq^2)$, the magnetostatic counterpart of Coulomb's $4\pi\rho/q^2$.

The longitudinal line: zero as a photon, Coulomb as a response

The third polarization, $\lambda=3$ with $\be_3=\hat\bq$, behaves differently. Section B.4 showed that the longitudinal field has no restoring force: its energy is the Coulomb energy of the charges, fixed by Gauss's law, and the split $H=H_0+V'$ moved that energy into $V'$ as the instantaneous Coulomb interaction . The free Hamiltonian $H_0$ therefore contains neither the longitudinal part $\hat\bA^{\mathrm L}$ of the field, its component along $\hat\bq$, nor its conjugate momentum. Three steps follow.

  1. In the interaction picture $\hat\bA^{\mathrm L}$ does not move: $\hat\bA^{\mathrm L}(t)=\hat\bA^{\mathrm L}(0)$.

    An operator that commutes with $H_0$ does not evolve in the interaction picture.

  2. Its retarded propagator contains the commutator $[\hat A^{\mathrm L}_\alpha(\br,t),\hat A^{\mathrm L}_\beta(\br',0)]$, which equals $[\hat A^{\mathrm L}_\alpha(\br),\hat A^{\mathrm L}_\beta(\br')]=0$, so it vanishes identically.

    Components of the vector potential commute with each other at equal times. Only the field and its conjugate momentum, which is $-\bE/4\pi c$ with $\bE$ the electric field, fail to commute (Section B.4).

  3. The time-ordered propagator is constant in time, so its Fourier transform is proportional to $\delta(\omega)$.

    A function that does not change in time has only a zero-frequency component.
$$\begin{gathered}\frac{\ii}{\hbar c}\int\dd t\,\ee^{\ii\omega t}\big\langle\mathcal T\hat A^{\mathrm L}_\alpha(\br,t)\,\hat A^{\mathrm L}_\beta(\br',0)\big\rangle_0\\ =0\qquad(\omega\neq0) .\end{gathered} \tag{B5.7}$$

The longitudinal photon of the interaction picture is frozen and carries nothing; the subscript 0 marks the free ground state. This does not remove the longitudinal field from the physics. Its effect is fixed by the Maxwell equations, and it can be written as a line between currents, in three steps.

  1. Take the component along $\hat\bq$ of the Ampère–Maxwell equation of , $\nabla\times\bB=\frac1c\partial_t\bE+\frac{4\pi}{c}\bj$, with $\bB$ the magnetic field: $0=\frac1c\partial_t\bE^{\mathrm L}+\frac{4\pi}{c}\bj^{\mathrm L}$, where $\bE^{\mathrm L}$ and $\bj^{\mathrm L}$ are the longitudinal parts, along $\hat\bq$.

    In Fourier space the curl is $\ii\bq\times\bB$, which has no component along $\bq$.

  2. Insert $\bE^{\mathrm L}=-\frac1c\partial_t\bA^{\mathrm L}$ and $\partial_t\to-\ii\omega$: $\frac{\omega^2}{c^2}\,\bA^{\mathrm L}=-\frac{4\pi}{c}\,\bj^{\mathrm L}$.

    In the temporal gauge the electric field is minus the time derivative of $\bA$, over $c$ (Section B.2).

  3. Solve for $\bA^{\mathrm L}=D^{0\mathrm L}\bj$, with $\bj^{\mathrm L}=\hat\bq(\hat\bq\cdot\bj)$:

    The longitudinal part of any current is its projection on $\hat\bq$, and $\hat q_\alpha\hat q_\beta$ is that projector.
$$D^{0\mathrm L}_{\alpha\beta}(\bq,\omega)=-\frac{4\pi c\,\hat q_\alpha\hat q_\beta}{\omega^2} . \tag{B5.8}$$

The line is the Coulomb interaction in disguise. For a charge density $\rho$ and a charge current $\bj$ that vary as $\ee^{\ii(\bq\cdot\br-\omega t)}$, charge conservation reads $\omega\rho=\bq\cdot\bj$. The energy of a current $\bj_2$ in the field of a current $\bj_1$ through the line, $-\frac1c\,\bj_2^*\cdot D^{0\mathrm L}\bj_1=\frac{4\pi}{\omega^2}(\hat\bq\cdot\bj_2)^*(\hat\bq\cdot\bj_1)$, is then exactly the Coulomb energy $\rho_2^*\,\frac{4\pi}{q^2}\,\rho_1$ of the charge densities $\rho_1$ and $\rho_2$ that the two currents carry.

In the bookkeeping $H=H_0+V'$ the Coulomb term of $V'$ carries this interaction, and says that no second copy travels as an interaction-picture photon; keeping both would count it twice. Section B.6 shows that the Coulomb term acts on the correlations of matter exactly as the line does, and adds the two lines into $D^0=D^{0\mathrm L}+D^{0\mathrm T}$, the free propagator of the Maxwell equations in the temporal gauge.7

Like the EPP manuscript, the rest of this section sets the Coulomb term aside, so that only transverse photons propagate: the free line is $D^0=D^{0\mathrm T}$. Section B.6 puts the Coulomb term back.

Each kind of charge couples to the field twice. The paramagnetic term, current times field, absorbs or emits one photon. The diamagnetic or contact term, quadratic in the field, scatters two photons at one point. Both come from minimal coupling. For an electron, $\big(-\ii\hbar\nabla+\frac ec\hat\bA\big)^2/2m_{\mathrm e}$ expands into the kinetic energy, the paramagnetic term and $\frac{e^2}{2m_{\mathrm e}c^2}\hat\bA^2$. For an ion the charge is replaced by its Born charge, a tensor, at the site of the atom: $\big(\hat\bp_{\ell\kappa}-\frac ec\,\mathbf Z^{*\mathsf T}_\kappa\cdot\hat\bA(\btau_{\ell\kappa})\big)^2/2M_\kappa$ gives the ionic terms of . The weight of each contact term is charge squared over mass: $e^2/m_{\mathrm e}$ per electron, $e^2\mathbf Z^*_\kappa\mathbf Z^{*\mathsf T}_\kappa/M_\kappa$ per ion.

The ionic current has a direct meaning. The Born dipoles $e\mathbf Z^*_\kappa\cdot\bu_{\ell\kappa}$ at the atoms form the polarization of the lattice , and its time derivative is a current, $e\mathbf Z^*_\kappa\cdot\dot\bu_{\ell\kappa}$ per atom. The velocity is $\dot\bu_{\ell\kappa}=\big(\hat\bp_{\ell\kappa}-\frac ec\mathbf Z^{*\mathsf T}_\kappa\cdot\hat\bA\big)/M_\kappa$. Its first part gives the paramagnetic current ; its second part, proportional to $\hat\bA$, is the ionic diamagnetic current, exactly as the field term in the velocity of an electron gives the electronic one.8

The Dyson equation

The Dyson equation follows from a self-consistent-field argument, the classical counterpart of summing the diagrams. Place an external current $\bj^{\mathrm{ext}}$ in the crystal, varying as $\ee^{\ii(\bq\cdot\br-\omega t)}$, and follow the field it produces.

  1. The field is produced by all currents, external and induced, through the free line: $\bA=D^0\big(\bj^{\mathrm{ext}}+\bj^{\mathrm{ind}}\big)$.

    In vacuum $\bA=D^0\bj$ for any current (the first reading of ). Matter only adds its own current $\bj^{\mathrm{ind}}$ to the source.

  2. Matter answers the field that acts on it, which is the total field: $\bj^{\mathrm{ind}}=\Pi\,\bA$.

    This defines the photon self-energy $\Pi$ as the current response of matter to the total vector potential. Because it responds to the total field, $\Pi$ contains no process that falls apart into two when a single photon line is cut: it is irreducible.

  3. Insert the second relation into the first, $\bA=D^0\bj^{\mathrm{ext}}+D^0\Pi\bA$, and define the dressed propagator by $\bA=D\,\bj^{\mathrm{ext}}$. Then $D\bj^{\mathrm{ext}}=D^0\bj^{\mathrm{ext}}+D^0\Pi D\bj^{\mathrm{ext}}$ for every external current, so

    $D$ plays for the crystal the role that $D^0$ plays for vacuum: the field per unit external current.
$$\begin{gathered}D=D^0+D^0\,\Pi\,D,\\ \Pi=\Pi^{\mathrm e}+\Pi^{\ion} .\end{gathered} \tag{B5.11}$$

Dyson equations and the random-phase approximation skip if familiar

A propagator in an interacting system is a sum over histories, in each of which the free propagator is interrupted by interactions. Group the interruptions into irreducible pieces: pieces that do not fall apart when one free line is cut. Every history is then a chain, free line, piece, free line, piece, …, free line. If $\Sigma$ denotes the sum of all irreducible pieces, the chains form a geometric series,

$$\begin{gathered}G=G^0+G^0\Sigma G^0+G^0\Sigma G^0\Sigma G^0+\dots\\ =G^0+G^0\Sigma G ,\end{gathered}$$

the Dyson equation, written here for the electron propagator $G$ and its self-energy $\Sigma$. Products are matrix products over all labels; for a homogeneous system at fixed $(\bq,\omega)$ they are ordinary products, and $G=G^0/(1-G^0\Sigma)$. Poles of $G$ where $G^0$ had none are new, dressed excitations.

Section A.5 used the same structure for the Coulomb interaction, $W=v+v\chi^{\mathrm{irr}}W$, where the irreducible polarizability $\chi^{\mathrm{irr}}$ plays the role of $\Sigma$. The random-phase approximation (RPA) keeps only the simplest irreducible piece, the bubble of one free electron–hole pair, so that $\chi^{\mathrm{irr}}\simeq\chi^0$. It neglects the interaction between the excited electron and the hole it leaves behind. The name comes from the original derivation by Bohm and Pines, in which terms with random phases were dropped.

Physical picture: inertia and restoring force

Drive a charge with a field that oscillates at $\omega$. A free charge responds only through its inertia: its current follows $\bA$ with the fixed weight charge squared over mass, which is the contact term. A bound charge also feels its restoring force, the gap of an electron or the springs of an ion, and the bubble supplies it. In a slowly varying field a bound charge only polarizes; it cannot carry a steady current. So in an insulator the two terms must cancel at zero frequency. The next subsection shows that gauge invariance guarantees this.

Gauge invariance fixes the contact term

What $\Pi$ means in optics

The self-energy is the conductivity in disguise. In the temporal gauge the electric field is $\bE=-\frac1c\partial_t\bA$, which for a time dependence $\ee^{-\ii\omega t}$ reads $\bE=\frac{\ii\omega}{c}\bA$. The induced current, $\bj=\Pi\bA=\frac{c\,\Pi}{\ii\omega}\bE$, then gives the conductivity $\sigma_{\alpha\beta}$ of Section X.1, and the Gaussian relation $\varepsilon=1+4\pi\ii\sigma/\omega$ gives the dielectric tensor:

$$\begin{gathered}\sigma_{\alpha\beta}(\bq,\omega)=-\frac{\ii c}{\omega}\,\Pi_{\alpha\beta}(\bq,\omega),\\ \varepsilon_{\alpha\beta}(\bq,\omega)=\delta_{\alpha\beta}+\frac{4\pi c}{\omega^2}\,\Pi_{\alpha\beta}(\bq,\omega) .\end{gathered} \tag{B5.14}$$
$\varepsilon_{\alpha\beta}(\bq,\omega)$
dielectric tensor, electrons and ions together (dimensionless); in a crystal both relations hold for the cell-averaged (macroscopic) elements $\bG=\bG'=0$ of below, with the variation of the fields inside a cell, the local fields, neglected

So $\Pi$ has units of 1/(length × time), and $4\pi c\,\Pi$ is a squared frequency; $D$ has units of length × time, and $D\Pi$ is dimensionless.

The free electron gas gives a first check. At $\bq=0$ its paramagnetic current is $-e/m_{\mathrm e}$ times the total momentum of the electrons, which is conserved; so the bubble vanishes at every $\omega\neq0$, and only the contact term remains, $4\pi c\,\Pi=-4\pi e^2\bar n_{\mathrm e}/m_{\mathrm e}\equiv-\omega_{\mathrm p}^2$, with $\bar n_{\mathrm e}$ the mean electron density. Then $\varepsilon=1-\omega_{\mathrm p}^2/\omega^2$, the Drude dielectric function with the plasma frequency $\omega_{\mathrm p}$. For the valence electrons of silicon, $\hbar\omega_{\mathrm p}=16.6$ eV.12

also says what the dressed photon is. In an isotropic medium, with the transverse free line , the Dyson equation is a scalar equation for the transverse part, solved by $D=D^0/(1-D^0\Pi)$:

$$D_{\alpha\beta}=-\frac{4\pi c\,\big(\delta_{\alpha\beta}-\hat q_\alpha\hat q_\beta\big)}{\omega^2\,\varepsilon(\omega)-c^2q^2} .$$

Its poles, $c^2q^2=\omega^2\varepsilon(\omega)$, are light with the refractive index $\sqrt{\varepsilon}$. Where $\varepsilon$ itself has resonances they become mixed light–matter modes, the polaritons of Section B.6 .

The static response to a pure gauge vanishes

The contact term alone describes free charges. In an insulator something must cancel it at low frequency; otherwise $\varepsilon\to-\infty$ as $\omega\to0$ and the insulator would conduct. Gauge invariance enforces the cancellation, in three steps.

  1. A static longitudinal vector potential, one whose Fourier components all point along $\hat\bq$, is the gradient of a static function, a pure gauge: it carries no electric field, $\bE=-\frac1c\partial_t\bA=0$, and no magnetic field, $\ii\bq\times\bA=0$.

    The temporal gauge leaves time-independent gauge transformations free (Section B.2): adding the gradient of a static function to $\bA$ changes neither field.

  2. A pure gauge changes no observable, so it drives no current: the static response to a longitudinal $\bA$ vanishes at every $\bq$. A static transverse $\bA$ of wavevector $\bq$ carries the magnetic field $\ii\bq\times\bA$, which vanishes as $\bq\to0$; in a normal (not superconducting) material it then drives no current either.

    The only static response to a transverse field is orbital magnetism, of order $q^2$. A superconductor would respond even at $\bq\to0$: that is the Meissner effect.

  3. So $\Pi(\bq\to0,\omega=0)=0$. The contact term does not depend on frequency, so it must equal minus the static bubble:

    Write $\Pi=\Pi^{\mathrm{dia}}+\Pi^{\mathrm{para}}(\omega)$ and set $\omega=0$.
$$\begin{aligned}\Pi^{\mathrm{dia}}&=-\Pi^{\mathrm{para}}(\bq\to0,\omega=0),\\ \Pi(\bq,\omega)&=\Pi^{\mathrm{para}}(\bq,\omega)\\&\quad-\Pi^{\mathrm{para}}(\bq,0) .\end{aligned} \tag{B5.15}$$

The identity holds for each kind of charge separately, because each couples to the field through its own minimal coupling, and for every Cartesian pair. Its second line is how the EPP manuscript writes the self-energy of the electrons in a crystal: the contact term never has to be computed, and the subtracted static bubble takes its place.

In a crystal $\Pi$ is a matrix between the plane waves $\bq+\bG$ and $\bq+\bG'$, with $\bG,\bG'$ reciprocal lattice vectors, as $\chi^0$ was in Section A.3; below sets this up, and its element $\bG=\bG'=0$ is the macroscopic response. The second line of is exact for the longitudinal response, because a static longitudinal $\bA$ is a pure gauge at every wavevector (steps 1 and 2), and for the macroscopic element as $\bq\to0$. For the transverse response at a finite wavevector $\bq+\bG$, which includes the elements with $\bG\neq\bG'$, it drops the static orbital magnetism of the electrons, which matters little in optics (see the caveat on truncated band sums below). For the ions the completeness of the phonon eigenvectors proves the identity exactly, for every element of the matrix, as shown below.13

$$\begin{gathered}4\pi c\,\Pi^{\mathrm{e,para}}=-\frac{8\pi}{\OVSC}\sum_\bk\big|j^{\,\mathrm v\bk,\mathrm c\bk}_\alpha\big|^2\\ \times\Big[\frac{1}{\hbar\omega-\hbar\omega_s}-\frac{1}{\hbar\omega+\hbar\omega_s}\Big]\\ =-W_s\,\frac{\omega_s^2}{\omega^2-\omega_s^2},\\ W_s=\frac{16\pi}{\hbar\omega_s\,\OVSC}\sum_\bk\big|j^{\,\mathrm v\bk,\mathrm c\bk}_\alpha\big|^2 .\end{gathered}$$

At $\omega=0$ this is $+W_s$. Subtracting it, as does, gives $4\pi c\,\Pi^{\mathrm e}=-W_s\frac{\omega_s^2}{\omega^2-\omega_s^2}-W_s=W_s\frac{\omega^2}{\omega_s^2-\omega^2}$, and gives $\varepsilon=1+W_s/(\omega_s^2-\omega^2)$, with $\omega\to\omega+\ii\eta$. The units check: $|j|^2$ in ($e$ Å/s)², divided by an energy and a volume, is a squared frequency, because $e^2$ is an energy times a length.

Truncated band sums: why codes subtract the static bubble

What the subtracted form buys in practice

Exactly, “contact term plus bubble” and the subtracted form are the same. In a calculation they differ. Band sums are cut off, and with nonlocal pseudopotentials the velocity operator is $\frac{\ii}{\hbar}[H,\br]$ rather than the momentum over $m_{\mathrm e}$, so the computed static bubble no longer cancels $e^2\bar n_{\mathrm e}/m_{\mathrm e}c$. Suppose the $N_{\mathrm t}$ transitions kept capture only the fraction $S=\sum_{s\le N_{\mathrm t}}W_s/\omega_{\mathrm p}^2$ of the f-sum. The unsubtracted sum, contact term plus truncated bubble, then leaves $-(1-S)\,\omega_{\mathrm p}^2/\omega^2$ in $\varepsilon$: a Drude term, a fake metal. The subtracted form gives $\Pi(0)=0$ by construction and misses the same weight at high frequency instead, where it does little harm.

Two costs remain. The subtracted form sets the static transverse response at finite $\bq+\bG$, the orbital magnetism of the electrons, to zero; in the macroscopic element it is of order $q^2$ and negligible in optics. And in a metal the static bubble must be taken at finite $\bq$ before $\bq\to0$: its intraband terms, from states at the Fermi level, then survive and supply the real Drude term of the free carriers.

The ionic self-energy, exactly

A polar lattice vibration is an oscillating array of dipoles, and an oscillating dipole is a current. Because the phonons are harmonic and the current is linear in the phonon operators, the current–current correlation of the ions is exactly one phonon line between two vertices. In the simplest crystal, two atoms per cell with opposite Born charges, only the optical mode carries a dipole, and the result reduces to one resonance at its frequency (end of this subsection). The general calculation shows where the phonon line comes from, and where the position of each atom inside the cell enters.

  1. In the interaction picture the momentum of an atom is its mass times its velocity, $\hat p_{\ell\kappa\beta}(t)=M_\kappa\,\dot{\hat u}_{\ell\kappa\beta}(t)$. Differentiating the displacement operator gives

    $$\begin{gathered}\hat p_{\ell\kappa\beta}(t)\\ =-\ii\sum_{\bq\nu}\Big(\frac{\hbar M_\kappa\omega_{\bq\nu}}{2N_\ell}\Big)^{1/2}\\ \times e_{\kappa\beta,\nu}(\bq)\,\ee^{\ii\bq\cdot\bR_\ell}\\ \times\big(a_{\bq\nu}\ee^{-\ii\omega_{\bq\nu}t}-a^\dagger_{-\bq\nu}\ee^{\ii\omega_{\bq\nu}t}\big) .\end{gathered} \tag{B5.21}$$
    With $H_0$ alone there is no field in the velocity, so the momentum is $M_\kappa\dot{\hat u}$. The time derivative of $a_{\bq\nu}\ee^{-\ii\omega_{\bq\nu}t}+a^\dagger_{-\bq\nu}\ee^{\ii\omega_{\bq\nu}t}$ is $-\ii\omega_{\bq\nu}$ times the difference, and $M_\kappa\omega_{\bq\nu}(\hbar/2N_\ell M_\kappa\omega_{\bq\nu})^{1/2}$ equals $(\hbar M_\kappa\omega_{\bq\nu}/2N_\ell)^{1/2}$, with $N_\ell$ the number of cells.

  2. Correlate two momenta in the phonon vacuum. As for the phonon line, only $a_{\bq\nu}$ followed by $a^\dagger_{\bq\nu}$ survives, which forces the second wavevector to be $-\bq$ and the branches to be equal:

    $$\begin{gathered}\big\langle\mathcal T\hat p_{\ell\kappa\gamma}(t)\,\hat p_{\ell'\kappa'\delta}(0)\big\rangle=\frac{\ii\hbar}{N_\ell}\sum_{\bq\nu}\frac{\hbar\omega_{\bq\nu}\sqrt{M_\kappa M_{\kappa'}}}{2}\\ \times e_{\kappa\gamma,\nu}(\bq)\,e^*_{\kappa'\delta,\nu}(\bq)\,\ee^{\ii\bq\cdot(\bR_\ell-\bR_{\ell'})}\,F^{\mathcal T}_\nu(\bq,t) .\end{gathered}$$
    The factor $(-\ii)^2$ and the minus sign in front of $a^\dagger$ combine to $+1$. The mode at $-\bq$ supplies $e_{\kappa'\delta,\nu}(-\bq)=e^*_{\kappa'\delta,\nu}(\bq)$ and the same frequency (Section A.1), with the eigenvector components $e_{\kappa\gamma,\nu}$. The time dependence $\theta(t)\ee^{-\ii\omega_{\bq\nu}t}+\theta(-t)\ee^{\ii\omega_{\bq\nu}t}$ is $\ii\hbar$ times the zero-temperature line .

  3. Insert this into the paramagnetic part of , with the current , and transform in time; by the rule the line becomes the retarded $F^0_\nu$:

    $$\begin{aligned}&\Pi^{\mathrm{ion,para}}_{\alpha\beta}(\br,\br';\omega)\\&\ =-\frac{\hbar}{c\,N_\ell}\sum_{\bq\nu}\frac{\omega_{\bq\nu}}{2}\,F^0_\nu(\bq,\omega)\\&\quad\times\sum_{\ell\kappa\gamma}\frac{eZ^*_{\kappa,\alpha\gamma}\,e_{\kappa\gamma,\nu}(\bq)}{\sqrt{M_\kappa}}\\&\qquad\times\ee^{\ii\bq\cdot\bR_\ell}\,\delta(\br-\btau_{\ell\kappa})\\&\quad\times\sum_{\ell'\kappa'\delta}\frac{eZ^*_{\kappa',\beta\delta}\,e^*_{\kappa'\delta,\nu}(\bq)}{\sqrt{M_{\kappa'}}}\\&\qquad\times\ee^{-\ii\bq\cdot\bR_{\ell'}}\,\delta(\br'-\btau_{\ell'\kappa'}) .\end{aligned}$$
    The constants are $\frac{\ii}{\hbar c}\cdot\ii\hbar=-\frac1c$, and the masses combine as $\sqrt{M_\kappa M_{\kappa'}}/(M_\kappa M_{\kappa'})=1/\sqrt{M_\kappa M_{\kappa'}}$. Each atom contributes its Born dipole, weighted by its share of the mode and placed at its own site.

  4. Do the lattice sums. The atoms $\kappa$ of all cells form a comb of points $\bR_\ell+\btau_\kappa$, whose Fourier series is $\sum_\ell\delta(\br-\btau_{\ell\kappa})=\frac{1}{\OV}\sum_\bG\ee^{\ii\bG\cdot(\br-\btau_\kappa)}$. On its support $\bR_\ell=\br-\btau_\kappa$, so

    $$\begin{gathered}\sum_\ell\ee^{\ii\bq\cdot\bR_\ell}\,\delta(\br-\btau_{\ell\kappa})\\ =\frac{1}{\OV}\sum_\bG\ee^{\ii(\bq+\bG)\cdot\br}\,\ee^{-\ii(\bq+\bG)\cdot\btau_\kappa} .\end{gathered}$$
    The comb repeats with the lattice, so it is a Fourier series in the $\bG$; its coefficients are $\frac{1}{\OV}\int_{\mathrm{cell}}\dd^3r\,\delta(\br-\btau_\kappa)\,\ee^{-\ii\bG\cdot\br}=\ee^{-\ii\bG\cdot\btau_\kappa}/\OV$, with $\OV$ the cell volume. The phase $\ee^{-\ii(\bq+\bG)\cdot\btau_\kappa}$ appears because the dipole sits at the atom, not at the corner of its cell.

  5. The sum over $\ell'$ gives the complex conjugate, with $\bG'$. The prefactor becomes $\frac{1}{N_\ell\OV^2}=\frac{1}{\OVSC\OV}$, and comparison with reads off the matrix elements.

    $\OVSC=N_\ell\OV$. The factor $1/\OVSC$ belongs to the convention , and $1/\OV$ stays in the result.

The result is compact in terms of the ionic current vertex,

$$\begin{gathered}j_{\alpha\nu}(\bq+\bG)=\sum_{\kappa\beta}\frac{eZ^*_{\kappa,\alpha\beta}}{\sqrt{M_\kappa}}\\ \times e_{\kappa\beta,\nu}(\bq)\,\ee^{-\ii(\bq+\bG)\cdot\btau_\kappa} ,\end{gathered} \tag{B5.22}$$
$j_{\alpha\nu}(\bq+\bG)$
ionic current vertex of mode $\nu$: the Born-charge dipole along $\alpha$ that the mode carries per unit mass-weighted amplitude, seen by a field of wavevector $\bq+\bG$ (charge/√mass, $e/\sqrt{\mathrm{amu}}$); $j^*_{\beta\nu}(\bq+\bG')$ is its complex conjugate, and $j_{\alpha\nu}(\bq)$ denotes the element with $\bG=0$

and the paramagnetic ionic self-energy has the structure “vertex, phonon line, vertex”:

$$\begin{aligned}&\Pi^{\mathrm{ion,para}}_{\alpha\beta,\bG\bG'}(\bq,\omega)\\&\quad=-\frac{\hbar}{c\,\OV}\sum_\nu\frac{\omega_{\bq\nu}}{2}\,F^0_\nu(\bq,\omega)\\&\quad\quad\times j_{\alpha\nu}(\bq+\bG)\,j^*_{\beta\nu}(\bq+\bG') .\end{aligned} \tag{B5.23}$$

With , $\frac{\hbar\omega_{\bq\nu}}{2}F^0_\nu=\omega^2_{\bq\nu}/[(\omega+\ii\eta)^2-\omega^2_{\bq\nu}]$, so each mode is a resonance at $\omega_{\bq\nu}$ with the strength $j_{\alpha\nu}j^*_{\beta\nu}$. A photon turns into a phonon at one vertex, the phonon propagates, and it turns back into a photon at the other. Four properties follow.

  • Polar modes only. The vertex vanishes for a mode whose Born dipoles cancel, as for the optical mode of silicon or diamond, whose two equivalent atoms carry no Born charge.
  • No acoustic coupling at long wavelength. For an acoustic mode at $\bq\to0$ and $\bG=0$ every atom moves by the same vector $\bd$, so $e_{\kappa\beta,\nu}\propto\sqrt{M_\kappa}\,d_\beta$ (Section A.1), and $j_{\alpha\nu}\propto\sum_\kappa(\mathbf Z^*_\kappa\cdot\bd)_\alpha=0$ by the Born-charge sum rule : a neutral crystal translated rigidly carries no current.
  • The basis phase. The phase $\ee^{-\ii(\bq+\bG)\cdot\btau_\kappa}$ makes the product $e_{\kappa\beta,\nu}(\bq)\,\ee^{-\ii(\bq+\bG)\cdot\btau_\kappa}$ unchanged when an atom is assigned to a neighbouring cell ; without it the vertex of an acoustic mode at small $\bq$ keeps a spurious term that depends on that choice. At $\bq\to0$ and $\bG=0$ the phase drops out.16
  • A grating of point dipoles. The $\bG\bG'$ dependence sits only in the phases: the response is that of $N_\kappa$ interpenetrating gratings of point dipoles, one per sublattice. Real atoms are not point dipoles, so the elements with large $|\bq+\bG|$ are not reliable; from Section B.6 on only $\bG=\bG'=0$ is used.

For a two-atom cubic crystal with Born charges $\pm Z^*$ the optical mode at $\bq\to0$ has $e_\kappa\propto(\sqrt{M_2},-\sqrt{M_1})$ (Section A.1), and gives a vertex of magnitude $eZ^*/\sqrt{M_{\mathrm r}}$ along the direction of motion, with the reduced mass $M_{\mathrm r}$. With the occupations kept, the derivation produces the finite-temperature line in place of its zero-temperature form; its retarded form is again , so the ionic self-energy does not depend on temperature in the harmonic approximation.17

The ionic contact term

The contact part of is local, so its lattice sum is simpler.

  1. Expand the comb as in step 4, $\sum_\ell\delta(\br-\btau_{\ell\kappa})=\frac{1}{\OV}\sum_{\bG''}\ee^{\ii\bG''\cdot(\br-\btau_\kappa)}$, and the remaining delta function in plane waves, $\delta(\br-\br')=\frac{1}{\OVSC}\sum_\bq\sum_{\bG'}\ee^{\ii(\bq+\bG')\cdot(\br-\br')}$.

    Every wavevector of the supercell is written once as $\bq+\bG'$, with $\bq$ in the first Brillouin zone.

  2. Set $\bG=\bG'+\bG''$ and compare with .

    The product of the two expansions then has the form , with a coefficient that depends only on $\bG-\bG'$.
$$\begin{gathered}\Pi^{\mathrm{ion,dia}}_{\alpha\beta,\bG\bG'}=-\frac{e^2}{c\,\OV}\\ \times\sum_{\kappa\gamma}\frac{Z^*_{\kappa,\alpha\gamma}Z^*_{\kappa,\beta\gamma}}{M_\kappa}\,\ee^{-\ii(\bG-\bG')\cdot\btau_\kappa} .\end{gathered} \tag{B5.24}$$

It depends neither on $\bq$ nor on $\omega$: ions without springs respond only through their inertia, at once and at one point. On the diagonal, $\bG=\bG'$, the phase is 1. The macroscopic element defines the ionic plasma frequency tensor, the tensor form of the ionic plasma frequency $\omega^{\ion}_{\mathrm p}$ of Section A.5: the plasma frequency that the ions would have if their springs were cut:

$$\begin{gathered}\big(\omega^{\ion}_{\mathrm p}\big)^2_{\alpha\beta}=-4\pi c\,\Pi^{\mathrm{ion,dia}}_{\alpha\beta,\mathbf0\mathbf0}\\ =\frac{4\pi e^2}{\OV}\sum_{\kappa\gamma}\frac{Z^*_{\kappa,\alpha\gamma}Z^*_{\kappa,\beta\gamma}}{M_\kappa} .\end{gathered}$$

For a two-atom cubic crystal with Born charges $\pm Z^*$ it is the scalar $(\omega^{\ion}_{\mathrm p})^2=4\pi e^2Z^{*2}/(\OV M_{\mathrm r})$ of Section B.3, the ionic counterpart of the electronic plasma frequency. In practical units, $(\hbar\omega^{\ion}_{\mathrm p})^2$ is $0.7564\ \text{eV}^2$ times $Z^{*2}/(\OV M_{\mathrm r})$, with $\OV$ in Å³ and $M_{\mathrm r}$ in amu. GaAs, with $Z^*=2.07$, $M_{\mathrm r}=36.1$ amu and $\OV=45.2$ Å³, has $\hbar\omega^{\ion}_{\mathrm p}=44.6$ meV, against about 16 eV for its valence electrons. The ratio, about $3\times10^{-3}$, is of the order of $\sqrt{m_{\mathrm e}/M_{\mathrm r}}\approx0.004$.18

Completeness: the contact term is minus the static bubble

For the ions the identity needs no symmetry argument: the algebra of the phonon eigenvectors proves it, in three steps.

  1. Set $\omega=0$ in . Each mode contributes $-\omega^2_{\bq\nu}/(0-\omega^2_{\bq\nu})=+1$ times its strength:

    $$\begin{gathered}\Pi^{\mathrm{ion,para}}_{\alpha\beta,\bG\bG'}(\bq,0)\\ =\frac{1}{c\,\OV}\sum_\nu j_{\alpha\nu}(\bq+\bG)\,j^*_{\beta\nu}(\bq+\bG') .\end{gathered}$$
    There are no real excitations at zero frequency, so the infinitesimal can be dropped. For $\bq\neq0$ every $\omega_{\bq\nu}$ is positive; at $\bq\to0$ the acoustic terms have the form $0/0$ and are defined by taking $\omega\to0$ first.

  2. Insert the vertices :

    $$\begin{gathered}\Pi^{\mathrm{ion,para}}_{\alpha\beta,\bG\bG'}(\bq,0)\\ =\frac{e^2}{c\,\OV}\sum_{\kappa\gamma}\sum_{\kappa'\delta}\frac{Z^*_{\kappa,\alpha\gamma}Z^*_{\kappa',\beta\delta}}{\sqrt{M_\kappa M_{\kappa'}}}\\ \times\ee^{-\ii(\bq+\bG)\cdot\btau_\kappa}\,\ee^{\ii(\bq+\bG')\cdot\btau_{\kappa'}}\\ \times\sum_\nu e_{\kappa\gamma,\nu}(\bq)\,e^*_{\kappa'\delta,\nu}(\bq) .\end{gathered}$$
    The only dependence on the mode is the product of eigenvector components, now summed over all branches.

  3. Use completeness, $\sum_\nu e_{\kappa\gamma,\nu}(\bq)\,e^*_{\kappa'\delta,\nu}(\bq)=\delta_{\kappa\kappa'}\delta_{\gamma\delta}$ (Section A.1). Only $\kappa'=\kappa$ survives, and the phases in $\bq$ cancel:

    At fixed $\bq$ the eigenvectors are the columns of a unitary matrix, so its rows are orthonormal too.
$$\begin{gathered}\Pi^{\mathrm{ion,para}}_{\alpha\beta,\bG\bG'}(\bq,0)\\ =\frac{e^2}{c\,\OV}\sum_{\kappa\gamma}\frac{Z^*_{\kappa,\alpha\gamma}Z^*_{\kappa,\beta\gamma}}{M_\kappa}\,\ee^{-\ii(\bG-\bG')\cdot\btau_\kappa}\\ =-\Pi^{\mathrm{ion,dia}}_{\alpha\beta,\bG\bG'} .\end{gathered} \tag{B5.25}$$

This is for the ions, exact for every $\bq$, every Cartesian pair and every $\bG\bG'$ element; neither the force constants nor $\bq$ survive. Read at $\bG=\bG'=0$ it is a sum rule for the strengths of the modes,

$$\sum_\nu j_{\alpha\nu}(\bq)\,j^*_{\beta\nu}(\bq)=e^2\sum_{\kappa\gamma}\frac{Z^*_{\kappa,\alpha\gamma}Z^*_{\kappa,\beta\gamma}}{M_\kappa} ,$$

the ionic version of the f-sum rule. The springs move infrared strength between the modes, differently at each $\bq$, but they cannot change the total. shows this for a chain with three atoms per cell.

The total ionic self-energy now follows from , with the same structure as the electronic . The identity $\frac{\omega^2_{\bq\nu}}{\omega^2-\omega^2_{\bq\nu}}+1=\frac{\omega^2}{\omega^2-\omega^2_{\bq\nu}}$ combines the two parts:

$$\begin{aligned}&\Pi^{\ion}_{\alpha\beta,\bG\bG'}(\bq,\omega)\\&\quad=\Pi^{\mathrm{ion,para}}(\bq,\omega)-\Pi^{\mathrm{ion,para}}(\bq,0)\\&\quad=-\frac{\omega^2}{c\,\OV}\sum_\nu\frac{1}{(\omega+\ii\eta)^2-\omega^2_{\bq\nu}}\\&\qquad\times j_{\alpha\nu}(\bq+\bG)\,j^*_{\beta\nu}(\bq+\bG') .\end{aligned} \tag{B5.26}$$

The factor $\omega^2/c$ has a simple origin. The ions respond to the electric field $\bE=\ii\omega\bA/c$, and their current is the rate of change $-\ii\omega\bP$ of their polarization, so two powers of $\omega$ multiply a polarizability. The units are those of every $\Pi$, 1/(length × time). Three limits show the physics. At $\omega=0$ the self-energy vanishes, as gauge invariance requires. Far above all phonon frequencies it tends to the contact term : the lattice responds as free ions. In between, each polar mode is a resonance at its bare frequency.19

The infrared dielectric tensor

At long wavelengths, $\bq\to0$, the macroscopic element $\bG=\bG'=0$ of the self-energy gives the dielectric tensor that infrared spectroscopy measures. Electrons and ions answer on very different scales: the electrons have their resonances at several eV, the ions at tens of meV. At phonon frequencies the electrons are therefore far below their transitions, while the ions are at resonance.

  1. Electrons: by the low-frequency limit of , $4\pi c\,\Pi^{\mathrm e}_{\alpha\beta}\simeq(\varepsilon_{\infty,\alpha\beta}-\delta_{\alpha\beta})\,\omega^2$.

    Every electronic transition lies far above $\hbar\omega$, so each oscillator responds with its static strength; together they give the electronic dielectric tensor $\boldsymbol\varepsilon_\infty$ of Section A.4.

  2. Ions: from at $\bq\to0$, $\frac{4\pi c}{\omega^2}\Pi^{\ion}_{\alpha\beta}=\frac{4\pi}{\OV}\sum_\nu\frac{j_{\alpha\nu}(\mathbf0)\,j^*_{\beta\nu}(\mathbf0)}{\omega^2_{\mathbf0\nu}-(\omega+\ii\eta)^2}$.

This is the infrared dielectric tensor of Gonze and Lee, the standard result of density-functional perturbation theory, which the review cites for the long-range force constants of polar crystals. Its ingredients, Born charges, eigenvectors, frequencies and $\boldsymbol\varepsilon_\infty$, are standard outputs. In the EPP construction the formula is derived rather than assumed: its poles are the bare phonons of $H_0$, and $\boldsymbol\varepsilon_\infty$ comes out of the electronic self-energy. The poles are the transverse optical frequencies; the zeros, where a longitudinal field can exist without a source, are the longitudinal optical frequencies (Section B.3).20

For a two-atom cubic crystal the three degenerate optical modes at $\Gamma$ carry the whole sum rule, $\sum_\nu j_{\alpha\nu}j^*_{\beta\nu}=(e^2Z^{*2}/M_{\mathrm r})\,\delta_{\alpha\beta}$, and becomes

$$\varepsilon(\omega)=\varepsilon_\infty+\frac{\big(\omega^{\ion}_{\mathrm p}\big)^2}{\omega^2_{\mathrm{TO}}-\omega^2-\ii\gamma\omega} ,$$

Check yourself: three short questions

  1. The thermal spread of a phonon mode, $\langle\phi_{\bq\nu}\phi_{-\bq\nu}\rangle=2n_{\bq\nu}+1$, grows with temperature. Why does the retarded propagator not change?

    Answer. The retarded function is built from the commutator of $\phi_{\bq\nu}(t)$ with $\phi_{-\bq\nu}(0)$, which for a harmonic mode is the number $\ee^{-\ii\omega_{\bq\nu}t}-\ee^{\ii\omega_{\bq\nu}t}$, the same in every state. The spread is the average of a product, not of a commutator, and that average depends on the occupation. Physically, the response of an oscillator to a force does not depend on how strongly it already oscillates, because its restoring force is linear.

  2. In a two-atom crystal with Born charges $\pm Z^*$, what share of the ionic sum rule does the acoustic mode carry at $\bq\to0$? What would it carry if both atoms had the Born charge $+Z^*$, so that each cell is charged?

    Answer. With $\pm Z^*$, none: the acoustic eigenvector moves both atoms alike, $e_\kappa=\sqrt{M_\kappa/(M_1+M_2)}$, so $j\propto Z^*-Z^*=0$, and the optical mode carries the whole sum $e^2Z^{*2}/M_{\mathrm r}$. With $+Z^*$ on both atoms the acoustic mode carries $|j|^2=4e^2Z^{*2}/(M_1+M_2)$ and the optical mode $e^2Z^{*2}(M_2-M_1)^2$ divided by $M_1M_2(M_1+M_2)$; their sum is again $e^2Z^{*2}(1/M_1+1/M_2)$. A mode of zero frequency with nonzero strength responds like free charge: it adds a Drude term, the charged crystal accelerating as a whole in the field.

  3. Use the GaAs values $Z^*=2.07$, $M_{\mathrm r}=36.1$ amu, $\OV=45.2$ Å³, $\varepsilon_\infty=10.9$ and $\hbar\omega_{\mathrm{TO}}=33.3$ meV. Find $\hbar\omega^{\ion}_{\mathrm p}$, the static constant $\varepsilon_{\mathrm s}$ and $\hbar\omega_{\mathrm{LO}}$.

    Answer. $(\hbar\omega^{\ion}_{\mathrm p})^2$ equals $0.7564\times2.07^2/(45.2\times36.1)$ eV², which is $1.99\times10^{-3}$ eV², so $\hbar\omega^{\ion}_{\mathrm p}=44.6$ meV. Then $\varepsilon_{\mathrm s}=\varepsilon(0)=10.9+(44.6/33.3)^2$, which is 12.7, and the zero of $\varepsilon$ lies at $\hbar\omega_{\mathrm{LO}}=\sqrt{33.3^2+44.6^2/10.9}$ meV $=35.9$ meV, against the measured 36.2 meV.

Symbols defined in this section
$F^{\mathcal T}_\nu$
time-ordered free phonon propagator of mode $\bq\nu$ at temperature $T$, and (1/energy); at $T=0$ it equals the retarded line $F^0_\nu$ at positive frequencies. EPP: $F_\sigma(\bq;t-t')$ (Eqs. 251–256), $F^T_\sigma$ (Eq. 249). Giustino: $D^{\rm A}_{\bq\nu\nu}=\hbar F^{\mathcal T}_\nu$ at $T=0$ (Eqs. 133–134).
$\theta$, $\mathrm P$
unit step $\theta(t)$, 1 for $t\gt0$ and 0 otherwise; principal value, with $\frac{1}{x\pm\ii\eta}=\mathrm P\frac1x\mp\ii\pi\delta(x)$, as in Section A.6. The time ordering $\mathcal T$ is that of Section A.5.
$\hat\bj^{\mathrm{ion,para}}$
paramagnetic current density of the ions, : Born charge times canonical momentum over mass, at each atom; the polarization current $\partial_t\bP$ without its diamagnetic part. EPP: $j^{\rm ion}_\alpha(\br)$ (Eq. 272), with every dipole at the cell corner.
$\Pi^{\mathrm e}$, $\Pi^{\ion}$;
$\Pi^{\mathrm{para}}$, $\Pi^{\mathrm{dia}}$
electronic and ionic parts of the photon self-energy, with $\bj^{\mathrm{ind}}=\Pi\bA$, ; each is a paramagnetic (current–current) part plus a diamagnetic (contact) part, , as in $\Pi^{\mathrm{e,para}}$ or $\Pi^{\mathrm{ion,dia}}$. Units 1/(length × time): $4\pi c\,\Pi$ is a squared frequency, and $\varepsilon_{\alpha\beta}=\delta_{\alpha\beta}+4\pi c\,\Pi_{\alpha\beta}/\omega^2$, . EPP: $\Pi^{\rm RPA}$, $\Pi^{\rm e,dia}$, $\Pi^{\rm e,para}$, $\Pi^{\rm ion,dia}$, $\Pi^{\rm ion,para}$ (Eqs. 278–280); $\Pi_{jj}$ from its §6.
$D^{\mathrm e}$
photon propagator dressed by the electrons only, the solution of $D^{\mathrm e}=D^0+D^0\Pi^{\mathrm e}D^{\mathrm e}$, : light in the crystal with the ions clamped (length × time). EPP: $D^{\rm e}$ (Eq. 388).
$\bar n_{\mathrm e}$
mean electron number density, the average of $n_{\mathrm e}(\br)$ over the crystal (Å⁻³). It sets the electronic contact term: $4\pi c\,\Pi^{\mathrm{e,dia}}=-\omega_{\mathrm p}^2$ on the diagonal, with $\omega_{\mathrm p}^2=4\pi e^2\bar n_{\mathrm e}/m_{\mathrm e}$.
$f_{\bG\bG'}(\bq)$
matrix element of a lattice-periodic two-point function between the plane waves $\bq+\bG$ and $\bq+\bG'$, ; $\bG=\bG'=0$ is the macroscopic response, $\bG\neq\bG'$ the local fields. EPP: $f_{\bG\bG'}(\bq,\omega)$ (Eq. 294), with an integral over the zone in place of the sum over $\bq$.
$j^{\,n\bk,m\bk+\bq}_\alpha(\bG)$
electronic current vertex: Fourier component at $\bq+\bG$ of the transition current density from $m\bk+\bq$ to $n\bk$, (charge × velocity); at $\bq\to0$, $\bG=0$ it is $-e/m_{\mathrm e}$ times the momentum matrix element. EPP: $j^\alpha_{n\bk,m\bk+\bq}(\bG)$ (Eqs. 283–284), with its signed $e$; $j^\mu_{n\bk,m\bk+\bq}$ at $\bG=0$ (Eq. 384).
$\omega_s$, $W_s$; $N_{\mathrm t}$, $S$
frequency and strength (a squared frequency) of the electronic transition $s$ in the oscillator form ; the number of transitions kept in a truncated band sum, and the fraction $S$ of the f-sum that they capture.
$j_{\alpha\nu}(\bq+\bG)$
ionic current vertex of mode $\nu$, , with the basis phase of each atom: the Born-charge dipole of the mode per unit mass-weighted amplitude (charge/√mass). $j_{\alpha\nu}(\bq)$ is its element with $\bG=0$. EPP: $j_{\alpha\sigma}(\bq)$ (Eqs. 295–296) and $j_{\mu\sigma}(\bq)$ after Eq. (385), without the basis phase and with $Z^*$ carrying the charge.
$\big(\omega^{\ion}_{\mathrm p}\big)^2_{\alpha\beta}$
ionic plasma frequency tensor, $-4\pi c$ times the macroscopic element of the contact term : the plasma frequency of the ions with their springs cut. For a two-atom cubic crystal it is the scalar $4\pi e^2Z^{*2}/(\OV M_{\mathrm r})$, with $\hbar\omega^{\ion}_{\mathrm p}=44.6$ meV in GaAs.
