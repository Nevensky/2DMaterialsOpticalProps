A.4Born charges · long-range fields · Fröhlich vertex · LO–TO splitting

Polar crystals: Born charges, the Fröhlich vertex and LO–TO splitting

In a polar crystal the atoms carry electric charge, so a lattice wave that moves them against each other is a wave of electric dipoles, and its field reaches across many cells. This section builds that field from two properties of the crystal, the Born effective charges and the electronic dielectric constant, and follows its two consequences: an electron–phonon coupling that grows as $1/q$ at long wavelengths, the coupling introduced by Fröhlich, and a longitudinal optical frequency that stays above the transverse one even as $\bq\to0$, the LO–TO splitting. Both are the electrostatic, long-wavelength face of physics that Part B rebuilds with the full electromagnetic field.

The picture: a lattice of charged ions

In sodium chloride, magnesium oxide, gallium arsenide or hexagonal boron nitride (hBN) the cell holds atoms of two different elements. Electrons gather around one of them (Cl, O, As, N), which becomes negative, and leave the other positive. Section A.1 showed that in an optical mode of long wavelength the two sublattices move against each other while the centre of mass of each cell stays at rest. In such a crystal this motion pushes positive charge one way and negative charge the other, so every cell acquires an oscillating electric dipole. A crystal whose optical vibrations carry dipoles is called polar.

The dipoles act at long range. A single displaced ion is a small dipole, and the potential of a dipole falls off only as $1/r^2$ with the distance $r$. In a lattice wave the dipoles of all the cells add up, and in a longitudinal wave they produce an electric field that runs through the whole crystal. Nonpolar crystals have no such field. In an insulator such as silicon the electrons screen a displaced atom so well that what remains of its potential falls off at least as fast as $1/r^3$, the potential of a quadrupole; in a metal the free electrons screen it within a few ångström.2

The field acts on everything that carries charge. It pulls the ions back toward equilibrium, which stiffens the longitudinal optical vibrations: their frequency stays above that of the transverse ones even at the longest wavelengths, the LO–TO splitting. It also acts on the electrons and gives an electron–phonon coupling that grows without bound as the wavelength grows, the coupling that Fröhlich introduced around 1950. Both effects are fixed by three ingredients, all computed by density-functional perturbation theory (Section A.3): the Born effective charges of the atoms, the dielectric constant of the electrons and the phonon eigenvectors. The section proceeds in six steps:

  1. define the Born effective charges and find their sum rule;
  2. compute the field of a wave of dipoles from Gauss's law;
  3. let the field act on the ions: long-range force constants and the LO–TO splitting;
  4. let the field act on the electrons: the long-range vertex, its Fröhlich limit and Fröhlich's coupling constant;
  5. see why this vertex breaks the interpolation used in practical calculations, and how it is repaired;
  6. find where electrostatics stops being exact.

Born effective charges

Displace every atom $\kappa$ of the crystal by the same small vector $\bu_\kappa$, a displacement pattern with wavevector $\bq=0$, and hold all other atoms fixed. Each cell then carries an extra dipole, and the crystal an extra polarization $\bP$, the electric dipole moment per unit volume. For small displacements the change is linear in $\bu_\kappa$. One condition makes it a property of the bulk: the crystal must be kept at zero macroscopic electric field $\bE$, the electric field averaged over a cell. In a finite sample the displaced sublattice leaves charge on two opposite surfaces, whose field acts back on the electrons and the ions, and the result would depend on the shape of the sample. Joining the two surfaces by a wire, as in a short-circuited capacitor, removes that field; a density-functional calculation with periodic boundary conditions removes it automatically. The Born effective charge tensor of atom $\kappa$ is then defined by3

$$Z^*_{\kappa,\alpha\beta}=\frac{\OV}{e}\,\frac{\partial P_\alpha}{\partial u_{\kappa\beta}}\bigg|_{\bE=0} . \tag{A4.1}$$

Multiplied by the cell volume, says that a displaced atom adds to its cell the dipole $\mathbf p=e\,\mathbf Z^*_\kappa\cdot\bu_\kappa$, with components $p_\alpha=e\sum_\beta Z^*_{\kappa,\alpha\beta}u_{\kappa\beta}$: the dipole that a point charge $eZ^*$ would create if the tensor were a single number $Z^*$. The same tensor gives the force that a uniform field exerts on the atom:

  1. In a uniform field the energy per cell contains the term $-\OV\,\bE\cdot\bP$.

    $\OV\bP$ is the dipole of one cell, and a dipole $\mathbf p$ in a uniform field has the energy $-\mathbf p\cdot\bE$.

  2. The force on atom $\kappa$ is minus the derivative of the energy per cell with respect to $u_{\kappa\beta}$:

    $$\begin{gathered}F_{\kappa\beta}=\OV\sum_\alpha E_\alpha\,\frac{\partial P_\alpha}{\partial u_{\kappa\beta}}\\ =e\sum_\alpha Z^*_{\kappa,\alpha\beta}\,E_\alpha .\end{gathered} \tag{A4.2}$$
    In the displacement pattern of each cell holds one atom $\kappa$, so the force on each of them is the derivative of the energy per cell. At equilibrium the springs exert no force, so only the field term contributes; the second form uses the definition .

So $\mathbf F_\kappa=e\,\mathbf Z^{*\mathsf T}_\kappa\bE$, with the transposed tensor because now the dipole index $\alpha$ is summed with the field.4 The two readings, dipole per displacement and force per field, are one mixed second derivative of the energy, and density-functional perturbation theory computes $\mathbf Z^*_\kappa$ in exactly this way, as a response to a displacement or to a uniform field (Section A.3). It computes the electronic dielectric constant, introduced below, as the response to the field alone.

Why the Born charge is not the charge of the ion

Each atom consists of a core, the nucleus with its tightly bound inner electrons, of charge $Z_\kappa e$, and of the valence electrons it contributes to the crystal; $Z_\kappa$ is its ionic valence.5 If the valence electrons stayed where they were when a core moves, the dipole per unit displacement would be $Z_\kappa e$, and $Z^*_{\kappa,\alpha\beta}$ would be $Z_\kappa\delta_{\alpha\beta}$. They do not stay. For every position of the cores they settle into their ground state (the Born–Oppenheimer picture of Section A.1), so a moving core drags valence charge with it and shifts charge between neighbouring bonds. Their contribution adds to that of the bare core:

$$Z^*_{\kappa,\alpha\beta}=Z_\kappa\,\delta_{\alpha\beta}+\frac{\OV}{e}\,\frac{\partial P^{\mathrm e}_\alpha}{\partial u_{\kappa\beta}}\bigg|_{\bE=0} , \tag{A4.3}$$

where $\bP^{\mathrm e}$ is the polarization of the valence electrons. In gallium arsenide, Ga contributes three valence electrons and As five, yet $Z^*=+2.07$ for Ga and $-2.07$ for As: the electrons that follow an As core more than cancel its charge. A second reference is the nominal ionic charge, the charge each ion would carry if the valence electrons were handed over completely and each ion then moved rigidly with its closed shells: $\pm1$ in NaCl, $\pm3$ in GaAs. In the strongly ionic NaCl, LiF and MgO the Born charge is close to the nominal one: the ions move almost rigidly. In the partly covalent GaAs and hBN it is smaller. In BaTiO₃ it is much larger: when a titanium atom moves toward an oxygen, the overlap of their orbitals along that bond changes, and electronic charge flows along the bond in the direction opposite to the motion, which adds to the dipole. The Born charge is a property of the bonding, not of the isolated ion. In the two-atom crystals of the table the second atom carries the opposite charge, for a reason given below.

A tensor, not a number

Column $\beta$ of $\mathbf Z^*_\kappa$, the three numbers $Z^*_{\kappa,x\beta}$, $Z^*_{\kappa,y\beta}$, $Z^*_{\kappa,z\beta}$, is the dipole, in units of $e$, created per unit displacement along $\beta$. A general displacement creates the sum of the columns weighted by its components, $\mathbf p/e=u_{\kappa x}(\text{column }x)+u_{\kappa y}(\text{column }y)+u_{\kappa z}(\text{column }z)$, and this dipole need not be parallel to the displacement. If the atom sits at a site of cubic symmetry, a rotation by 90° about any of the three axes through the site maps the crystal onto itself, either alone (NaCl, LiF, MgO) or combined with an inversion through the site (Ga and As in GaAs). The inversion reverses both the displacement and the dipole and so leaves $\mathbf Z^*_\kappa$ unchanged; either way the dipole must turn with the displacement. Each column then points along its own axis, all three have the same length, and $Z^*_{\kappa,\alpha\beta}=Z^*_\kappa\delta_{\alpha\beta}$: the tensor is a single number, as in NaCl, LiF, MgO and GaAs. At a site of lower symmetry the columns differ. The oxygen of cubic BaTiO₃ sits between two titanium atoms, and only its bond axis is special: its tensor is diagonal, with $-5.71$ along the bond and $-2.15$ across it. Displaced at an angle to the bond, it creates a dipole turned toward the bond direction (). At sites of still lower symmetry the tensor need not even be symmetric.

Charge neutrality: a sum rule

The Born charges of a cell add up to zero. The argument is the one that gave the acoustic sum rule :

  1. Displace every atom of the crystal by the same small vector $\bd$, that is $\bu_\kappa=\bd$ for every $\kappa$. By the polarization changes by

    $$\delta P_\alpha=\frac{e}{\OV}\sum_\beta\Big(\sum_\kappa Z^*_{\kappa,\alpha\beta}\Big)d_\beta .$$
    For small displacements the contributions of the sublattices add, and $d_\beta$ is the same for all of them.

  2. This is a rigid translation of the whole crystal, so $\delta\bP=0$.

    The electrons, whose ground state follows the cores, are carried along unchanged, so every cell moves as a whole with its charge distribution intact. Moving a set of charges by $\bd$ changes their dipole by their total charge times $\bd$, which vanishes for a neutral cell. In the terms of : the cores contribute $\sum_\kappa Z_\kappa$, the number of valence electrons per cell, and the valence electrons, moving rigidly, exactly minus that number.

  3. Take $\bd$ along $x$, then $y$, then $z$. Each choice keeps a single $\beta$, so each bracket vanishes:

    $$\sum_\kappa Z^*_{\kappa,\alpha\beta}=0\quad\text{for all }\alpha,\beta . \tag{A4.4}$$
    The polarization must stay unchanged whatever the direction of the translation.

The force gives the same rule from the other side: a uniform field exerts no net force on a neutral cell. In a two-atom crystal the rule means $\mathbf Z^*_2=-\mathbf Z^*_1$, which is why one number describes GaAs or NaCl. For BaTiO₃, with one Ba, one Ti and three O whose bonds point along $x$, $y$ and $z$, the $xx$ components add to $2.77+7.25-5.71-2.15-2.15=0.01$, zero within the precision of the values. Computed Born charges break slightly through numerical noise, and codes restore it before using them, as they restore . In silicon or diamond an inversion maps one atom of the cell onto the other, and this forces $\mathbf Z^*_\kappa=0$ for both (question 1 of the Check yourself box): these crystals are nonpolar. Equivalence by symmetry alone is not enough; the atoms of trigonal selenium and tellurium, related by a screw rotation, have nonzero Born charges.

A wave of dipoles and its field

The polarization of a lattice wave

Take a lattice wave as in Section A.1, $\bu_{\ell\kappa}=\bu_\kappa\,\ee^{\ii\bq\cdot\bR_\ell}$, where $\bu_\kappa$ is the complex displacement of atom $\kappa$ in the cell at the origin; the physical displacement is the real part. Let its wavelength $2\pi/q$ be much longer than the cell, $qa\ll1$. Over a region of many cells that is still small compared with the wavelength, the displacement is then uniform, and applies locally: cell $\ell$ carries the dipole $e\sum_\kappa\mathbf Z^*_\kappa\cdot\bu_\kappa\,\ee^{\ii\bq\cdot\bR_\ell}$. Spread over the volume of the cell, this is a polarization wave $\bP(\br)=\bP\,\ee^{\ii\bq\cdot\br}$ with the amplitude

$$\bP=\frac{e}{\OV}\sum_\kappa\mathbf Z^*_\kappa\cdot\bu_\kappa . \tag{A4.5}$$

If the wave also produces a field, the electrons respond to that field in addition; that response is not in and is counted separately below, through the dielectric constant. Two cases show what contains. In a long acoustic wave all atoms of a cell move together, $\bu_\kappa\to\bd$, and the sum rule makes $\bP$ vanish: acoustic waves carry no dipole at long wavelengths. In an optical wave of a two-atom crystal, $\mathbf Z^*_2=-\mathbf Z^*_1$ gives $\bP=e\,\mathbf Z^*_1\cdot(\bu_1-\bu_2)/\OV$, set by the relative displacement of the two sublattices. In a cubic crystal $\bP$ is then parallel to $\bu_1-\bu_2$: along $\bq$ in a longitudinal optical (LO) wave, perpendicular to it in a transverse optical (TO) wave.

Polarization, bound charge and Gauss's law skip if familiar

Bound charge. A polarization $\bP$ is a density of dipoles. Where it is uniform, the positive end of each dipole sits next to the negative end of its neighbour and the charges cancel. Where it changes, they do not. A slab polarized uniformly along its normal carries the charge $+|\bP|$ per unit area on the face that $\bP$ points to and $-|\bP|$ on the other, and a polarization that varies in space leaves the charge density $-\nabla\cdot\bP$ inside the material. These charges are called bound, because they belong to charges that are displaced but still attached; charges that can travel through the material, absent in an insulator, are called free.

Gauss's law and the dielectric constant. In Gaussian units $\nabla\cdot\bE=4\pi\times$(density of all charges, bound and free). The electrons of an insulator polarize in a field; for a small field their polarization is linear, $(\boldsymbol\varepsilon-1)\bE/4\pi$, which defines the dielectric tensor $\boldsymbol\varepsilon$, a single number $\varepsilon$ in a cubic crystal. If the material also carries a polarization $\bP$ that the field does not cause, here the dipoles of displaced ions, the bound charge is $-\nabla\cdot[\bP+(\boldsymbol\varepsilon-1)\bE/4\pi]$, and Gauss's law without free charges becomes $\nabla\cdot(\boldsymbol\varepsilon\bE+4\pi\bP)=0$.

Potentials. In electrostatics the field has no curl, so $\bE=-\nabla A_0$ with a scalar potential $A_0$; this primer writes $A_0$ because $\phi$ is the phonon field. A charge $Q$ at the origin has $A_0=Q/r$ in vacuum and $Q/(\varepsilon r)$ in a medium. Two opposite charges a small distance apart form a dipole $\mathbf p$ (charge times separation, pointing from the negative to the positive charge), whose potential $\mathbf p\cdot\br/(\varepsilon r^3)$ falls off as $1/r^2$ because the two charges nearly cancel.

Plane waves. On a plane wave $f\,\ee^{\ii\bq\cdot\br}$ the gradient acts as multiplication by $\ii\bq$, so the differential equations become algebraic. The Fourier transform of $1/r$ is $\int\dd^3r\,\ee^{-\ii\bq\cdot\br}/r=4\pi/q^2$, which gives the Coulomb interaction $v(\bq)=4\pi e^2/q^2$ of Section A.3. The potential of a point dipole $\mathbf p$ at the origin has the Fourier component $-4\pi\ii\,\bq\cdot\mathbf p/(\bq\cdot\boldsymbol\varepsilon\cdot\bq)$: the gradient that turns a charge into a dipole becomes the factor $\ii\bq$, and in an anisotropic medium $\varepsilon q^2$ becomes $\bq\cdot\boldsymbol\varepsilon\cdot\bq$.

Gauss's law in a wave

The two kinds of optical wave look very different to electrostatics. In an LO wave the dipoles point along $\bq$. Their heads converge on some planes perpendicular to $\bq$ and their tails on others, so sheets of positive and negative bound charge appear half a wavelength apart, like the plates of a stack of capacitors, with a field between them. In a TO wave the dipoles lie side by side within each plane, the head of one next to the tail of its neighbour, and no net charge appears anywhere. shows both.

Gauss's law turns this picture into a formula. It needs the electronic dielectric constant $\boldsymbol\varepsilon_\infty$: the dielectric tensor of the electrons alone, with the ions held fixed, at frequencies well above the phonon frequencies and well below the band gap (the subscript $\infty$ means high compared with phonon frequencies). It is the right one here because the electrons respond to a change of the field within about $\hbar$ divided by the gap, far faster than a vibration period, while the response of the ions is already contained in $\bP$.

  1. The displaced ions carry the polarization wave $\bP\,\ee^{\ii\bq\cdot\br}$, and the electrons, polarized by the field $\bE\,\ee^{\ii\bq\cdot\br}$ of the wave, add $(\boldsymbol\varepsilon_\infty-1)\bE\,\ee^{\ii\bq\cdot\br}/4\pi$.

    The first is ; the second is the definition of the electronic dielectric tensor (background box).

  2. Gauss's law with bound charges only reads $\nabla\cdot\big[(\boldsymbol\varepsilon_\infty\bE+4\pi\bP)\,\ee^{\ii\bq\cdot\br}\big]=0$, and on the plane wave

    $$\ii\bq\cdot(\boldsymbol\varepsilon_\infty\bE+4\pi\bP)=0 .$$
    An insulator has no free charges, and the gradient of $\ee^{\ii\bq\cdot\br}$ is $\ii\bq\,\ee^{\ii\bq\cdot\br}$.

  3. In electrostatics $\bE=-\nabla A_0$, which on the wave reads $\bE=-\ii\bq A_0$: the field is parallel to $\bq$. A field along the wavevector is called longitudinal, a field perpendicular to it transverse.

    The pattern of the wave moves at its phase velocity $\omega/q$, with $\omega$ the frequency of the wave, which for all but the smallest $q$ is far below the speed of light in the crystal, so magnetic effects and the delay of the field are negligible. The last subsection finds where this stops being true.

  4. Write $\bE=E\,\hat\bq$, with $\hat\bq=\bq/q$, and insert it into step 2: $E\;\hat\bq\cdot\boldsymbol\varepsilon_\infty\cdot\hat\bq=-4\pi\,\hat\bq\cdot\bP$, so7

    $$\bE=-4\pi\,\frac{\hat\bq\,(\hat\bq\cdot\bP)}{\hat\bq\cdot\boldsymbol\varepsilon_\infty\cdot\hat\bq} . \tag{A4.6}$$
    The equation of step 2 is a single scalar condition; dividing it by $\ii q$ leaves an equation for the one unknown $E$.

Equation contains the three facts that the rest of the section uses. First, only the component of $\bP$ along $\bq$ creates a field: the LO wave has one, and the TO wave, with $\hat\bq\cdot\bP=0$, has none. Second, the field does not depend on the wavelength. Longer waves put the sheets of charge further apart, but each sheet carries the same charge per unit area, so the field between them is the same, as between the plates of a capacitor. Third, the potential, which is field times distance, grows with the wavelength: from step 3, $A_0=\ii\,\hat\bq\cdot\bE/q$, which grows as $1/q$. That is the origin of the $1/q$ in Fröhlich's coupling.

The numbers are large. Take an LO wave of GaAs ($Z^*=2.07$, $\OV=45.16$ Å³, $\varepsilon_\infty=10.9$; literature values, illustrative) in which the two sublattices are displaced relative to each other by only 0.01 Å. Then $|\bP|=4.6\times10^{-4}$ e/Å², and the force on an electron, $e|\bE|=4\pi e^2Z^*|\bu_1-\bu_2|/(\OV\varepsilon_\infty)$, is $7.6\times10^{-3}$ eV/Å: a field of 0.76 MV/cm. For a wavelength of 100 Å the potential energy of an electron, $-eA_0$, swings by $\pm0.12$ eV; for 20 Å, by $\pm24$ meV. A single displaced ion, for comparison, is a dipole $\mathbf p=e\,\mathbf Z^*_\kappa\cdot\bu_\kappa$ with the potential $\mathbf p\cdot\br/(\varepsilon_\infty r^3)$ in a cubic crystal, and the field of the wave is the sum of the fields of all such dipoles.

The field acts on the ions: the LO–TO splitting

In an LO wave the field points against $\bP$, and so against the relative displacement that created it: it pulls the ions back toward equilibrium. This extra restoring force acts only on longitudinal waves, and, since the field does not depend on the wavelength, it survives as $q\to0$. The LO frequency is therefore raised above the TO frequency even at the longest wavelengths. To find by how much, the force of the field is written as a contribution to the force constants of Section A.1.

Force constants of the field

  1. Atom $\kappa$ of cell $\ell$ feels the force of the field at its position:

    $$F_{\ell\kappa\alpha}=e\sum_\gamma Z^*_{\kappa,\gamma\alpha}\,E_\gamma\,\ee^{\ii\bq\cdot\bR_\ell} .$$
    For $qa\ll1$ the phase of the field changes negligibly within a cell, so the field at the atom is the field at the corner of its cell. The summed Cartesian index is written $\gamma$.

  2. Insert the field and the polarization :

    $$\begin{gathered}F_{\ell\kappa\alpha}=-\ee^{\ii\bq\cdot\bR_\ell}\,\frac{4\pi e^2}{\OV}\,\frac{(\hat\bq\cdot\mathbf Z^*_\kappa)_\alpha}{\hat\bq\cdot\boldsymbol\varepsilon_\infty\cdot\hat\bq}\\ \times\sum_{\kappa'\beta}(\hat\bq\cdot\mathbf Z^*_{\kappa'})_\beta\,u_{\kappa'\beta} .\end{gathered}$$
    Here $(\hat\bq\cdot\mathbf Z^*_\kappa)_\alpha=\sum_\gamma\hat q_\gamma Z^*_{\kappa,\gamma\alpha}$, and $\hat\bq\cdot\bP=(e/\OV)\sum_{\kappa'\beta}(\hat\bq\cdot\mathbf Z^*_{\kappa'})_\beta\,u_{\kappa'\beta}$.

  3. Compare with the force constants. In a wave, Newton's equations of Section A.1 give $F_{\ell\kappa\alpha}=-\ee^{\ii\bq\cdot\bR_\ell}\sum_{\kappa'\beta}\Phi_{\kappa\alpha,\kappa'\beta}(\bq)\,u_{\kappa'\beta}$, so as $\bq\to0$ the field adds to $\Phi(\bq)$ the term8

    $$\begin{gathered}\Phi^{\mathrm{dd}}_{\kappa\alpha,\kappa'\beta}(\bq)=\frac{4\pi e^2}{\OV}\\ \times\frac{(\bq\cdot\mathbf Z^*_\kappa)_\alpha\,(\bq\cdot\mathbf Z^*_{\kappa'})_\beta}{\bq\cdot\boldsymbol\varepsilon_\infty\cdot\bq} .\end{gathered} \tag{A4.7}$$
    This is the relabelling step in the derivation of : summing the force constants over the partner's cell, each weighted by the phase of that cell, gives $\ee^{\ii\bq\cdot\bR_\ell}\,\Phi(\bq)$. Writing $\bq$ instead of $\hat\bq$ changes nothing, because $q^2$ cancels between numerator and denominator.

The full force constants of a polar crystal are $\Phi(\bq)=\Phi^{\mathrm{sr}}(\bq)+\Phi^{\mathrm{dd}}(\bq)$. The short-range part $\Phi^{\mathrm{sr}}$ holds everything else: the springs of Section A.1, which fall off quickly in real space and give smooth functions of $\bq$. The dipole–dipole part is different in kind. It depends on the direction of $\bq$ but not on its length, so its value at $\bq=0$ depends on the direction from which $\bq$ approaches zero. A function with this property cannot be expanded in a power series about $\bq=0$; it is called nonanalytic there. In real space the same property is the $1/R^3$ tail that the spring models of Section A.1 lack. The acoustic sum rule survives: by , $\sum_{\kappa'}(\bq\cdot\mathbf Z^*_{\kappa'})_\beta=0$, so $\Phi^{\mathrm{dd}}$ exerts no force in a rigid translation, and the acoustic frequencies still vanish at $\Gamma$.

In practice a density-functional calculation at $\bq=0$ with periodic boundary conditions has no macroscopic field. It yields $\Phi^{\mathrm{sr}}(0)$, together with $\mathbf Z^*_\kappa$ and $\boldsymbol\varepsilon_\infty$, and codes add afterwards, as described by Gonze and Lee. To interpolate phonon dispersions between computed wavevectors they subtract the dipole–dipole interaction summed over all cells, interpolate the smooth remainder and add the dipole part back. The same strategy reappears below for the electron–phonon vertex.

Two atoms in a cubic crystal

For two atoms per cell in a cubic crystal, such as NaCl, MgO or GaAs, the splitting follows in four steps.

  1. Cubic symmetry and give $Z^*_{1,\alpha\beta}=Z^*\delta_{\alpha\beta}$, $Z^*_{2,\alpha\beta}=-Z^*\delta_{\alpha\beta}$ and $(\boldsymbol\varepsilon_\infty)_{\alpha\beta}=\varepsilon_\infty\delta_{\alpha\beta}$. Without the field, the three optical modes at $\Gamma$ share one frequency, $\omega_{\mathrm{TO}}$, and have the eigenvectors

    $$\be_{1}=\sqrt{\frac{M_2}{M_{\mathrm c}}}\;\bn,\qquad \be_{2}=-\sqrt{\frac{M_1}{M_{\mathrm c}}}\;\bn .$$
    This is the optical eigenvector $(\sqrt{M_2},-\sqrt{M_1})$ of the diatomic chain in Section A.1, normalized with the cell mass $M_{\mathrm c}=M_1+M_2$, for motion along a unit vector $\bn$. Cubic symmetry makes all directions $\bn$ equivalent, so all three optical modes have the same frequency.

  2. The field adds $\Phi^{\mathrm{dd}}_{\kappa\alpha,\kappa'\beta}/\sqrt{M_\kappa M_{\kappa'}}$ to the dynamical matrix. In an optical eigenvector its expectation value is

    $$\begin{gathered}\sum_{\kappa\alpha,\kappa'\beta}\frac{e^*_{\kappa\alpha}\,\Phi^{\mathrm{dd}}_{\kappa\alpha,\kappa'\beta}\,e_{\kappa'\beta}}{\sqrt{M_\kappa M_{\kappa'}}}\\ =\frac{4\pi e^2}{\OV\varepsilon_\infty}\,\Big|\sum_\kappa\frac{\hat\bq\cdot\mathbf Z^*_\kappa\cdot\be_\kappa}{\sqrt{M_\kappa}}\Big|^2 .\end{gathered}$$
    is a product of a factor that depends on $\kappa\alpha$ and the same factor for $\kappa'\beta$, so the double sum is the squared modulus of a single sum.

  3. Evaluate the sum:

    $$\begin{gathered}\sum_\kappa\frac{\hat\bq\cdot\mathbf Z^*_\kappa\cdot\be_\kappa}{\sqrt{M_\kappa}}\\ =Z^*(\hat\bq\cdot\bn)\Big(\sqrt{\frac{M_2}{M_{\mathrm c}M_1}}+\sqrt{\frac{M_1}{M_{\mathrm c}M_2}}\Big)\\ =\frac{Z^*\,(\hat\bq\cdot\bn)}{\sqrt{M_{\mathrm r}}} .\end{gathered}$$
    Over the common denominator $\sqrt{M_{\mathrm c}M_1M_2}$ the bracket has the numerator $M_1+M_2=M_{\mathrm c}$, and $M_1M_2/M_{\mathrm c}$ is the reduced mass $M_{\mathrm r}$.

  4. The acoustic modes, with $\be_\kappa\propto\sqrt{M_\kappa}\,\bd$, get nothing by ; the TO modes, with $\bn$ perpendicular to $\hat\bq$, get nothing; the LO mode, with $\bn=\hat\bq$, is pushed up:9

    $$\omega^2_{\mathrm{LO}}-\omega^2_{\mathrm{TO}}=\frac{4\pi e^2Z^{*2}}{\OV M_{\mathrm r}\,\varepsilon_\infty} . \tag{A4.8}$$
    Within the three degenerate optical modes the field acts only on the component of $\bn$ along $\hat\bq$, so the LO and TO patterns remain eigenvectors, and the expectation value of step 2 is the exact shift of $\omega^2$, not a first-order estimate.

The static dielectric constant

The same ingredients fix how strongly the crystal screens a static field once the ions are allowed to respond. This is measured by the static dielectric constant $\varepsilon_{\mathrm s}$.

  1. Apply a static uniform field $\bE$ to the two-atom cubic crystal. The ions shift until the restoring force of the optical mode balances the force of the field: $M_{\mathrm r}\omega_{\mathrm{TO}}^2\,(\bu_1-\bu_2)=eZ^*\bE$.

    The field pulls the two sublattices apart with the forces $\pm eZ^*\bE$. A uniform relative displacement feels only the short-range springs, whose stiffness for the relative coordinate is $M_{\mathrm r}\omega^2_{\mathrm{TO}}$. The macroscopic field is the applied $\bE$, already on the right side, so it must not be counted again through $\Phi^{\mathrm{dd}}$.

  2. The ions then carry the polarization , $\bP=eZ^*(\bu_1-\bu_2)/\OV=e^2Z^{*2}\bE/(\OV M_{\mathrm r}\omega^2_{\mathrm{TO}})$.

    Insert the displacement of step 1.

  3. Electrons and ions together give $\varepsilon_\infty\bE+4\pi\bP=\varepsilon_{\mathrm s}\bE$, so10

    $$\varepsilon_{\mathrm s}=\varepsilon_\infty+\frac{4\pi e^2Z^{*2}}{\OV M_{\mathrm r}\,\omega^2_{\mathrm{TO}}} . \tag{A4.9}$$
    The combination that enters Gauss's law, $\varepsilon_\infty\bE+4\pi\bP$, defines the dielectric constant when both the electrons and the ions follow the field.

  4. Divide by $\omega^2_{\mathrm{TO}}$ and compare with . This gives the Lyddane–Sachs–Teller relation:

    $$\frac{\omega^2_{\mathrm{LO}}}{\omega^2_{\mathrm{TO}}}=\frac{\varepsilon_{\mathrm s}}{\varepsilon_\infty} . \tag{A4.10}$$
    $\omega^2_{\mathrm{LO}}/\omega^2_{\mathrm{TO}}-1=4\pi e^2Z^{*2}/(\OV M_{\mathrm r}\varepsilon_\infty\omega^2_{\mathrm{TO}})=(\varepsilon_{\mathrm s}-\varepsilon_\infty)/\varepsilon_\infty$.

For GaAs, $(36.2/33.3)^2=1.18$ and $\varepsilon_{\mathrm s}/\varepsilon_\infty=12.9/10.9=1.18$; for LiF, $(82/38)^2=4.7$ and $9.0/1.93=4.7$. The combination $1/\varepsilon_\infty-1/\varepsilon_{\mathrm s}$, the part of $1/\varepsilon$ that the ions remove, is 15% of $1/\varepsilon_\infty$ in GaAs and 79% in LiF; it returns in Fröhlich's coupling. The relation says that the optical frequencies and the two dielectric constants carry the same information. Section B.3 derives it from the full frequency-dependent dielectric function, whose pole lies at $\omega_{\mathrm{TO}}$ and whose zero lies at $\omega_{\mathrm{LO}}$.

The direction from which $\bq$ approaches zero

In a cubic crystal the nonanalytic term hides in the eigenvectors. Whatever the direction $\hat\bq$, the LO mode is the one that moves along $\hat\bq$, at the same frequency $\omega_{\mathrm{LO}}$; but which pattern of motion is LO changes with the direction, so the dynamical matrix at $\Gamma$ has no single value. In a crystal with one special axis, such as bulk hBN, the Born charges and $\boldsymbol\varepsilon_\infty$ differ along and across the axis, and the frequencies themselves change with the direction of approach ().

Approaching $\Gamma$ along the axis of bulk hBN, perpendicular to the layers, the mode that moves along the axis is longitudinal and lies at 103 meV, while the modes that move within the layers are transverse and lie at 170 meV. Approaching within the layers, the in-plane mode along $\hat\bq$ becomes longitudinal and rises to 200 meV, while the mode along the axis is now transverse and drops to 97 meV. In between, the two modes that move in the plane containing $\hat\bq$ and the axis are mixtures, neither purely longitudinal nor purely transverse. The eigenvalue problem behind the figure is the optical block of $\tPhi^{\mathrm{sr}}(0)$ plus $\Phi^{\mathrm{dd}}/\sqrt{M_\kappa M_{\kappa'}}$: a $3\times3$ problem for the direction $\bn$ of the relative motion.

The field acts on the electrons: the long-range vertex

An electron in an LO wave feels the potential energy $-eA_0$ of the field, an energy landscape that rises and falls with the wave, with an amplitude proportional to the wavelength. In the language of Section A.2 this is one more potential that changes when the atoms move, and it has a matrix element between Bloch states. This subsection computes it, first from the macroscopic field and then from the dipoles at the atoms.

The macroscopic field

  1. The potential energy of an electron, of charge $-e$, in the field of the wave is $V^{\mathrm{lr}}=-eA_0$. With $\bE=-\ii\bq A_0$ and ,

    $$\begin{gathered}V^{\mathrm{lr}}(\br)=4\pi\ii e\,\frac{\bq\cdot\bP}{\bq\cdot\boldsymbol\varepsilon_\infty\cdot\bq}\\ \times\ee^{\ii\bq\cdot\br} .\end{gathered} \tag{A4.11}$$
    The scalar product of $\bE=-\ii\bq A_0$ with $\bq$ gives $A_0=\ii\,\bq\cdot\bE/q^2$, and gives $\bq\cdot\bE=-4\pi\,\bq\cdot\bP/(\hat\bq\cdot\boldsymbol\varepsilon_\infty\cdot\hat\bq)$.

  2. Take the wave of a single phonon mode. The term $\bq\nu$ of the displacement operator is a wave with the amplitudes $\bu_\kappa=N_\ell^{-1/2}\big(\hbar/2M_\kappa\omega_{\bq\nu}\big)^{1/2}\be_{\kappa\nu}(\bq)\,\hat\phi_{\bq\nu}$. As in Section A.2, the factor $N_\ell^{-1/2}\hat\phi_{\bq\nu}$ stays in the Hamiltonian, and the potential per unit phonon field, $\Delta_{\bq\nu}V^{\mathrm{lr}}$, follows from and with $\bu_\kappa=\big(\hbar/2M_\kappa\omega_{\bq\nu}\big)^{1/2}\be_{\kappa\nu}(\bq)$:

    $$\begin{gathered}\Delta_{\bq\nu}V^{\mathrm{lr}}(\br)=\ii\,\frac{4\pi e^2}{\OV}\sum_\kappa\Big(\frac{\hbar}{2M_\kappa\omega_{\bq\nu}}\Big)^{1/2}\\ \times\frac{\bq\cdot\mathbf Z^*_\kappa\cdot\be_{\kappa\nu}(\bq)}{\bq\cdot\boldsymbol\varepsilon_\infty\cdot\bq}\,\ee^{\ii\bq\cdot\br} .\end{gathered}$$
    This is the bookkeeping of $\Delta_{\bq\nu}V$ in Section A.2, applied to the long-range potential; $\big(\hbar/2M_\kappa\omega_{\bq\nu}\big)^{1/2}$ is the zero-point amplitude.

  3. Take the matrix element between Bloch states $\psi_{n\bk}=\ee^{\ii\bk\cdot\br}u_{n\bk}/\sqrt{N_\ell}$:

    $$\langle\psi_{m\bk+\bq}|\ee^{\ii\bq\cdot\br}|\psi_{n\bk}\rangle=\int_{\mathrm{cell}}u^*_{m\bk+\bq}(\br)\,u_{n\bk}(\br)\,\dd^3r .$$
    The phases $\ee^{-\ii(\bk+\bq)\cdot\br}\,\ee^{\ii\bq\cdot\br}\,\ee^{\ii\bk\cdot\br}$ cancel, the remaining integrand repeats from cell to cell, and the integral over the $N_\ell$ cells of the supercell is $N_\ell$ times the integral over one cell, which cancels the normalization factor $1/N_\ell$.

  4. As $q\to0$, $u_{m\bk+\bq}\to u_{m\bk}$, and the overlap tends to $\delta_{mn}$:

    $$\begin{gathered}g^{\mathrm{lr}}_{mn\nu}(\bk,\bq)\simeq\ii\,\frac{4\pi e^2}{\OV}\,\delta_{mn}\\ \times\sum_\kappa\Big(\frac{\hbar}{2M_\kappa\omega_{\bq\nu}}\Big)^{1/2}\\ \times\frac{\bq\cdot\mathbf Z^*_\kappa\cdot\be_{\kappa\nu}(\bq)}{\bq\cdot\boldsymbol\varepsilon_\infty\cdot\bq} .\end{gathered} \tag{A4.12}$$
    The $u_{n\bk}$ of different bands at the same $\bk$ are eigenfunctions of one Hermitian problem, normalized on the cell, so they are orthonormal there (Section A.2).

The numerator grows as $q$ and the denominator as $q^2$, so the vertex grows as $1/q$. Three selection rules can be read off. Only modes whose dipole has a component along $\bq$ couple: the LO modes, not the TO modes. Acoustic modes do not couple at long wavelength, because their dipoles cancel by . And the electron stays in its band, $m=n$, and is deflected by the small angle that a small $\bq$ allows: the long-range coupling is intraband and favours forward scattering.

The dipoles sit at the atoms: the full vertex

The macroscopic field is the field averaged over a cell. Near the atoms, the point dipoles create a potential that varies within the cell, and the Fourier decomposition of such a potential contains, besides $\bq$, the wavevectors $\bq+\bG$ with every reciprocal lattice vector $\bG$. Verdi and Giustino, and independently Sjakste and co-workers, wrote the potential of all the dipoles explicitly in 2015; the review presents it as the long-range part of the matrix element. The derivation reuses .

  1. A single point dipole $\mathbf p$ at $\br_0$ is a polarization concentrated at one point, $\mathbf p\,\delta^3(\br-\br_0)=\int\frac{\dd^3q'}{(2\pi)^3}\,\mathbf p\,\ee^{-\ii\mathbf q'\cdot\br_0}\,\ee^{\ii\mathbf q'\cdot\br}$, a superposition of polarization waves. Each wave contributes , so the electron's potential energy is

    $$V(\br)=\int\frac{\dd^3q'}{(2\pi)^3}\,4\pi\ii e\,\frac{\mathbf q'\cdot\mathbf p}{\mathbf q'\cdot\boldsymbol\varepsilon_\infty\cdot\mathbf q'}\,\ee^{\ii\mathbf q'\cdot(\br-\br_0)} .$$
    Gauss's law is linear, so the potentials of the waves add. Here $\mathbf q'$ is an integration variable that runs over all wavevectors.

  2. The dipoles of the wave sit at the atoms: atom $\kappa$ of cell $\ell$, at $\btau_{\ell\kappa}=\bR_\ell+\btau_\kappa$, carries $\mathbf p_{\ell\kappa}=e\,\mathbf Z^*_\kappa\cdot\bu_\kappa\,\ee^{\ii\bq\cdot\bR_\ell}$. Summing step 1 over all of them,

    $$\begin{gathered}V(\br)=\sum_\kappa\int\frac{\dd^3q'}{(2\pi)^3}\,4\pi\ii e^2\,\frac{\mathbf q'\cdot\mathbf Z^*_\kappa\cdot\bu_\kappa}{\mathbf q'\cdot\boldsymbol\varepsilon_\infty\cdot\mathbf q'}\\ \times\ee^{\ii\mathbf q'\cdot(\br-\btau_\kappa)}\sum_\ell\ee^{\ii(\bq-\mathbf q')\cdot\bR_\ell} .\end{gathered}$$
    Each dipole carries the phase of its cell, $\ee^{\ii\bq\cdot\bR_\ell}$, and its position $\br_0=\bR_\ell+\btau_\kappa$ enters through $\ee^{-\ii\mathbf q'\cdot\br_0}$.

  3. The sum over cells is a lattice of delta functions:

    $$\sum_\ell\ee^{\ii(\bq-\mathbf q')\cdot\bR_\ell}=\frac{(2\pi)^3}{\OV}\sum_\bG\delta^3(\mathbf q'-\bq-\bG) .$$
    The phases add up only where $\mathbf q'-\bq$ is a reciprocal lattice vector, as in the lattice sums of Section A.1. Integrating both sides over one Brillouin zone, of volume $(2\pi)^3/\OV$, fixes the weight: on the left, only the term $\ell=0$ survives the integral.

  4. The integral collapses onto $\mathbf q'=\bq+\bG$:

    $$\begin{gathered}V(\br)=\ii\,\frac{4\pi e^2}{\OV}\sum_{\bG}\sum_\kappa\\ \times\frac{(\bq+\bG)\cdot\mathbf Z^*_\kappa\cdot\bu_\kappa}{(\bq+\bG)\cdot\boldsymbol\varepsilon_\infty\cdot(\bq+\bG)}\\ \times\ee^{\ii(\bq+\bG)\cdot(\br-\btau_\kappa)} .\end{gathered} \tag{A4.13}$$
    The term $\bG=0$ is with , apart from the phase $\ee^{-\ii\bq\cdot\btau_\kappa}$, which is close to 1 when $qa\ll1$. The terms $\bG\neq0$ describe how the potential varies inside the cell.

  5. Take the wave of one phonon per unit phonon field, $\bu_\kappa=\big(\hbar/2M_\kappa\omega_{\bq\nu}\big)^{1/2}\be_{\kappa\nu}(\bq)$, form the matrix element between Bloch states as in the previous subsection, and leave out the single term with $\bq+\bG=0$.

    At $\bq=0$ the term $\bG=0$ describes a uniform polarization, whose macroscopic field depends on the surfaces of the sample; the zero-field convention of the Born charges and of periodic calculations removes it.

The result is the long-range vertex of Verdi and Giustino:11

$$\begin{gathered}g^{\mathrm{lr}}_{mn\nu}(\bk,\bq)=\ii\,\frac{4\pi e^2}{\OV}\\ \times\sum_\kappa\Big(\frac{\hbar}{2M_\kappa\omega_{\bq\nu}}\Big)^{1/2}\sum_{\bG\neq-\bq}\\ \times\frac{(\bq+\bG)\cdot\mathbf Z^*_\kappa\cdot\be_{\kappa\nu}(\bq)}{(\bq+\bG)\cdot\boldsymbol\varepsilon_\infty\cdot(\bq+\bG)}\\ \times\ee^{-\ii(\bq+\bG)\cdot\btau_\kappa}\\ \times\langle\psi_{m\bk+\bq}|\ee^{\ii(\bq+\bG)\cdot\br}|\psi_{n\bk}\rangle .\end{gathered} \tag{A4.14}$$

Reading the vertex

  • The kernel. $4\pi e^2/[\OV\,(\bq+\bG)\cdot\boldsymbol\varepsilon_\infty\cdot(\bq+\bG)]$ is the Coulomb interaction screened by the electrons, per cell. In a cubic crystal it is $v(\bq+\bG)/(\OV\varepsilon_\infty)$ with the bare $v(\bq)=4\pi e^2/q^2$ of Section A.3. Its $\bG=0$ term is the long-wavelength, static limit of the screened interaction $v/\varepsilon^{\mathrm e}$ that Section A.3 builds into every vertex, with the electronic dielectric function $\varepsilon^{\mathrm e}\to\varepsilon_\infty$.
  • The projection. $(\bq+\bG)\cdot\mathbf Z^*_\kappa\cdot\be_{\kappa\nu}$ keeps only the component of each atom's dipole along the wavevector, the part that creates a potential.
  • The amplitude. $\big(\hbar/2M_\kappa\omega_{\bq\nu}\big)^{1/2}\be_{\kappa\nu}$ is the zero-point amplitude of atom $\kappa$ in the mode, as in every matrix element of Section A.2.
  • The sum over $\bG$. The terms $\bG\neq0$ are smooth near $\Gamma$, but each repeats the $1/|\bq+\bG|$ singularity at the point $\bq=-\bG$, which is equivalent to $\Gamma$. With them, $g^{\mathrm{lr}}$ has the singularity at every image of $\Gamma$, as the full $g$ has. The electronic dielectric constant is not accurate at wavevectors as large as $\bG$, but the terms it governs there are smooth, and any error they carry ends up in $g^{\mathrm{sr}}$.
  • The phase. $\ee^{-\ii(\bq+\bG)\cdot\btau_\kappa}$ matters for acoustic modes, for $\bG\neq0$ and for wavevectors comparable to the size of the zone; the box below shows why.
  • No adjustable parameter. $\mathbf Z^*_\kappa$ and $\boldsymbol\varepsilon_\infty$ come from density-functional perturbation theory, the eigenvectors and frequencies from the phonon calculation.
Derivation: why the phase on the atom matters for acoustic modes

In a long acoustic wave every atom follows a smooth displacement field evaluated at its own position. The physical amplitude of atom $\kappa$ is $\boldsymbol\xi_\kappa$$\,\simeq\bd\,\ee^{\ii\bq\cdot\btau_\kappa}$, and the normalized eigenvector is $\be_{\kappa\nu}(\bq)\simeq\sqrt{M_\kappa/M_{\mathrm c}}\;\hat\bd\,\ee^{\ii\bq\cdot\btau_\kappa}$, with $\hat\bd$ the unit vector along $\bd$, up to corrections of order $q$ from the relaxation of the atoms inside a strained cell.

  1. With the phase, the $\bG=0$ term of contains

    $$\begin{gathered}\sum_\kappa\frac{\bq\cdot\mathbf Z^*_\kappa\cdot\be_{\kappa\nu}}{\sqrt{M_\kappa}}\,\ee^{-\ii\bq\cdot\btau_\kappa}\\ =\frac{1}{\sqrt{M_{\mathrm c}}}\sum_\kappa\bq\cdot\mathbf Z^*_\kappa\cdot\hat\bd=0 .\end{gathered}$$
    The two phases cancel, every atom contributes the same vector $\hat\bd$, and the sum rule removes the sum. The $1/q$ singularity is absent, as it must be for a wave that carries no dipole.

  2. Without the phase, the same sum is

    $$\begin{gathered}\frac{1}{\sqrt{M_{\mathrm c}}}\sum_\kappa\bq\cdot\mathbf Z^*_\kappa\cdot\hat\bd\;\ee^{\ii\bq\cdot\btau_\kappa}\\ \simeq\frac{\ii}{\sqrt{M_{\mathrm c}}}\sum_\kappa(\bq\cdot\btau_\kappa)\;\bq\cdot\mathbf Z^*_\kappa\cdot\hat\bd .\end{gathered}$$
    Expand $\ee^{\ii\bq\cdot\btau_\kappa}\simeq1+\ii\bq\cdot\btau_\kappa$; the first term vanishes by .

  3. This is of order $q^2$, like the denominator, so their ratio stays finite as $q\to0$. The zero-point amplitude $\big(\hbar/2M_\kappa\omega_{\bq\nu}\big)^{1/2}$ grows as $q^{-1/2}$, because $\omega_{\bq\nu}\propto q$ for an acoustic mode, so the vertex without the phase grows as $q^{-1/2}$: a spurious long-range coupling of long acoustic waves.

    It also changes when an atom is assigned to a neighbouring cell, $\btau_\kappa\to\btau_\kappa+\bR_\ell$, a choice without physical meaning. With the phase, each term depends only on where the atom actually is; Section B.1 proves this invariance in general.

Fröhlich's vertex

Fröhlich, Pelzer and Zienau (1950) wrote the coupling of an electron to the LO phonons of an ionic crystal in terms of measured dielectric constants. Their vertex is reduced to its singular core under four assumptions: a cubic crystal with two atoms per cell, the LO mode at small $q$, the term $\bG=0$ only, and an overlap equal to $\delta_{mn}$.

  1. Insert the LO eigenvector, the eigenvector of the LO–TO derivation with $\bn=\hat\bq$, into :

    $$\begin{gathered}\sum_\kappa\Big(\frac{\hbar}{2M_\kappa\omega_{\mathrm{LO}}}\Big)^{1/2}\bq\cdot\mathbf Z^*_\kappa\cdot\be_\kappa\\ =\Big(\frac{\hbar}{2\omega_{\mathrm{LO}}}\Big)^{1/2}\frac{Z^*q}{\sqrt{M_{\mathrm r}}} .\end{gathered}$$
    This is the sum of step 3 of the LO–TO derivation, multiplied by $q\,(\hbar/2\omega_{\mathrm{LO}})^{1/2}$.

  2. With $\bq\cdot\boldsymbol\varepsilon_\infty\cdot\bq=\varepsilon_\infty q^2$, the vertex is

    $$g^{\mathrm{lr}}=\frac{\ii}{q}\,\frac{4\pi e^2Z^*}{\OV\varepsilon_\infty}\Big(\frac{\hbar}{2M_{\mathrm r}\omega_{\mathrm{LO}}}\Big)^{1/2}\delta_{mn} .$$
    Collect the factors of .

  3. Square it and replace $4\pi e^2Z^{*2}/(\OV M_{\mathrm r})$ by $\varepsilon_\infty(\omega^2_{\mathrm{LO}}-\omega^2_{\mathrm{TO}})$:

    $$|g^{\mathrm{lr}}|^2=\frac{4\pi e^2}{\OV q^2}\,\frac{\hbar}{2\omega_{\mathrm{LO}}}\,\frac{\omega^2_{\mathrm{LO}}-\omega^2_{\mathrm{TO}}}{\varepsilon_\infty} .$$
    This is the LO–TO splitting : the same Born charge and dielectric constant fix both.

  4. Use the Lyddane–Sachs–Teller relation , $\omega^2_{\mathrm{TO}}=\omega^2_{\mathrm{LO}}\varepsilon_\infty/\varepsilon_{\mathrm s}$, so that $(\omega^2_{\mathrm{LO}}-\omega^2_{\mathrm{TO}})/\varepsilon_\infty=\omega^2_{\mathrm{LO}}(1/\varepsilon_\infty-1/\varepsilon_{\mathrm s})$, and take the square root with the phase of step 2.

    The phase $\ii$ follows from the sign chosen for the eigenvector in step 1, which is free (Section A.1); measurable quantities contain $|g|^2$.

The result is Fröhlich's vertex, for $\nu$ the LO branch:12

$$\begin{gathered}g^{\mathrm{lr}}_{mn\nu}(\bk,\bq)\simeq\frac{\ii}{q}\,\delta_{mn}\\ \times\Big[\frac{4\pi e^2}{\OV}\,\frac{\hbar\omega_{\mathrm{LO}}}{2}\Big(\frac{1}{\varepsilon_\infty}-\frac{1}{\varepsilon_{\mathrm s}}\Big)\Big]^{1/2} .\end{gathered} \tag{A4.15}$$

The masses and the Born charge have disappeared: Fröhlich's vertex needs only the two dielectric constants and the LO frequency, all of which can be measured optically. Its strength is set by $1/\varepsilon_\infty-1/\varepsilon_{\mathrm s}$, the part of the screening that the ions contribute. For GaAs it gives $|g^{\mathrm{lr}}|\,q=0.032$ eV Å⁻¹, so $|g^{\mathrm{lr}}|=0.32$ eV at $q=0.1$ Å⁻¹. The Born-charge form of step 2 with $Z^*=2.07$ gives 0.030 eV Å⁻¹; the 5% difference reflects the slight inconsistency of the literature values noted after . For LiF, $|g^{\mathrm{lr}}|\,q=0.43$ eV Å⁻¹, thirteen times more.

For comparison, an acoustic phonon couples through the deformation potential $E_{\mathrm{def}}$, the shift of a band edge per unit relative change of the cell volume. A long acoustic wave changes the volume locally by the fraction $\nabla\cdot\bu$, which gives $|g|=E_{\mathrm{def}}\,q\,\big(\hbar/2M_{\mathrm c}\omega_{\bq\nu}\big)^{1/2}$ with $\omega_{\bq\nu}=v_{\mathrm s}q$: a coupling that grows as $q^{1/2}$, with the sound velocity $v_{\mathrm s}$ and the cell mass $M_{\mathrm c}$. For GaAs ($E_{\mathrm{def}}\approx7$ eV, $v_{\mathrm s}=5.2$ km/s; illustrative) the LO coupling is the larger one for all $q$ below 0.37 Å⁻¹, a third of the way to the zone boundary. The $1/q$ divergence does not make scattering rates infinite. Rates contain $|g|^2$ summed over $\bq$; the sum becomes an integral over $\dd^3q$, which in spherical coordinates carries the factor $q^2\,\dd q$, and that factor cancels the $1/q^2$. Quantities with an extra energy denominator that vanishes as $q\to0$ can still diverge (Section A.8).

Physical picture: Fröhlich's strength is the ionic part of screening

A slow test charge in a polar crystal is screened by the electrons and the ions, a fast one by the electrons alone; the LO phonons are the ions' share. Two electrons that exchange one virtual LO phonon, one emitting it and the other absorbing it, gain the interaction $-2|g^{\mathrm{lr}}|^2/(N_\ell\hbar\omega_{\mathrm{LO}})$ at zero energy transfer: there are two time orders, each with the energy denominator $-\hbar\omega_{\mathrm{LO}}$. Their Coulomb interaction screened by the electrons is $v(q)/(N_\ell\OV\varepsilon_\infty)$ for states normalized on the supercell. If the sum is to be the fully screened static interaction $v(q)/(N_\ell\OV\varepsilon_{\mathrm s})$, then

$$\frac{v(q)}{\OV\varepsilon_\infty}-\frac{2|g^{\mathrm{lr}}|^2}{\hbar\omega_{\mathrm{LO}}}=\frac{v(q)}{\OV\varepsilon_{\mathrm s}} ,$$

which, with $v(q)=4\pi e^2/q^2$, is exactly . Fröhlich's coupling is the one that turns electronic screening into full screening. Section A.5 shows that this is the polar case of the split of the screened interaction into an electronic and a phonon part, at every frequency.

Fröhlich's coupling constant

Whether the coupling is weak or strong depends not only on the vertex but also on how heavy the electron is. Near the bottom of a band an electron moves like a free particle with an effective mass $m^*$, quoted in units of the electron mass $m_{\mathrm e}$. Confining it to a region of size $r$ costs a kinetic energy of order $\hbar^2/2m^*r^2$, which equals the phonon energy $\hbar\omega_{\mathrm{LO}}$ at the polaron length $r_{\mathrm p}=(\hbar/2m^*\omega_{\mathrm{LO}})^{1/2}$. Fröhlich's dimensionless coupling constant compares the ionic part of the Coulomb energy at that distance with twice the phonon energy:13

$$\begin{gathered}\alpha_{\mathrm F}=\frac{e^2}{\hbar}\Big(\frac{m^*}{2\hbar\omega_{\mathrm{LO}}}\Big)^{1/2}\\ \times\Big(\frac{1}{\varepsilon_\infty}-\frac{1}{\varepsilon_{\mathrm s}}\Big)\\ =\frac{e^2}{2\hbar\omega_{\mathrm{LO}}\,r_{\mathrm p}}\Big(\frac{1}{\varepsilon_\infty}-\frac{1}{\varepsilon_{\mathrm s}}\Big) .\end{gathered} \tag{A4.16}$$
$\alpha_{\mathrm F}$
Fröhlich's coupling constant (dimensionless)
$m^*$
effective mass of the electron (or hole) in its band
$r_{\mathrm p}$
polaron length (Å): the size of the region within which confinement costs one phonon energy

In these terms the vertex reads $|g^{\mathrm{lr}}|^2=4\pi\alpha_{\mathrm F}(\hbar\omega_{\mathrm{LO}})^2r_{\mathrm p}/(\OV q^2)$. In practical units $\alpha_{\mathrm F}=(1/\varepsilon_\infty-1/\varepsilon_{\mathrm s})\,\big[(m^*/m_{\mathrm e})(13.606\ \mathrm{eV})/\hbar\omega_{\mathrm{LO}}\big]^{1/2}$, where $13.606$ eV $=m_{\mathrm e}e^4/2\hbar^2$ is the Rydberg energy. For an electron in GaAs ($m^*=0.067\,m_{\mathrm e}$), $\alpha_{\mathrm F}=0.071$ and $r_{\mathrm p}=40$ Å. For MgO ($m^*\approx0.35\,m_{\mathrm e}$), $\alpha_{\mathrm F}\approx1.7$. For LiF and NaCl with $m^*=m_{\mathrm e}$, an illustrative choice, $\alpha_{\mathrm F}\approx5$. When $\alpha_{\mathrm F}\ll1$ the electron moves with a light cloud of virtual phonons, about $\alpha_{\mathrm F}/2$ of them on average; its energy is lowered by $\alpha_{\mathrm F}\hbar\omega_{\mathrm{LO}}$, 2.6 meV in GaAs, and its mass is raised by the factor $1+\alpha_{\mathrm F}/6$. Such a dressed electron is called a polaron. When $\alpha_{\mathrm F}$ exceeds about 6, the electron digs a potential well in the lattice deep enough to trap itself; the change from weak to strong coupling is gradual, not a sharp transition. The review calls the $1/q$ coupling "the physical basis for the phenomenon of electron self-trapping in polarons". puts these numbers side by side.

Why polar crystals break Wannier interpolation

Many electron–phonon quantities are sums over the Brillouin zone whose terms vary on a very fine scale. Their denominators contain differences such as $E_{m\bk+\bq}-E_{n\bk}\pm\hbar\omega_{\bq\nu}$, with the band energies $E_{n\bk}$, which become small whenever an electronic energy difference approaches a phonon energy, and phonon energies (10–200 meV) are 10 to 100 times smaller than band widths. Converged sums can need up to a million wavevectors $\bk$ or $\bq$, while each density-functional perturbation calculation for one $\bq$ costs about as much as one ground-state calculation.14 The way out, developed by Giustino, Cohen and Louie, is to compute $g$ on a coarse grid of wavevectors, transform it to a representation in real space where it is short-ranged, and transform it back on as fine a grid as needed. The real-space representation uses localized electron orbitals, the Wannier functions.

Wannier functions skip if familiar

Bloch states extend over the whole crystal. For a group of bands separated by gaps from all other bands, they can be recombined into orbitals localized near one cell:

$$\begin{gathered}w_{m\ell}(\br)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_\ell}}\sum_\bk\ee^{-\ii\bk\cdot\bR_\ell}\sum_n\mathcal U_{nm\bk}\,\psi_{n\bk}(\br),\\ \psi_{n\bk}(\br)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_\ell}}\sum_\ell\ee^{\ii\bk\cdot\bR_\ell}\sum_m\mathcal U^\dagger_{mn\bk}\,w_{m\ell}(\br) .\end{gathered}$$

Here $\mathcal U_\bk$ is a unitary matrix that mixes the bands of the group at each $\bk$, and the second relation inverts the first. The functions $w_{m\ell}$ are the Wannier functions: $w_{m\ell}(\br)=w_{m0}(\br-\bR_\ell)$ is orbital $m$ centred in cell $\ell$, and they are orthonormal. The matrices $\mathcal U_\bk$ are free, and Marzari and Vanderbilt choose them to make the orbitals as compact as possible. These maximally localized Wannier functions decay exponentially in insulators and resemble atomic or bond orbitals. A Hamiltonian written in this basis, $\langle w_{m0}|H|w_{n\ell}\rangle$, decays quickly with $\bR_\ell$, like the hopping integrals of a tight-binding model, so band energies computed on a coarse grid can be interpolated to any $\bk$ by a short Fourier series.

In this basis the electron–phonon coupling becomes the amplitude of a hop between two orbitals caused by the displacement of one atom:

$$\begin{gathered}g_{mn\kappa\alpha}(\bR_\ell,\bR_{\ell'})\\ =\Big\langle w_{m0}\Big|\frac{\partial V^{\mathrm{KS}}}{\partial u_{\ell'\kappa\alpha}}\Big|w_{n\ell}\Big\rangle .\end{gathered} \tag{A4.17}$$

Writing the Bloch states of the definition through Wannier functions, and shifting each term so that the hop ends in cell 0, gives the transform back to wavevectors:

$$\begin{gathered}g_{mn\nu}(\bk,\bq)=\sum_{\ell\ell'}\ee^{\ii(\bk\cdot\bR_\ell+\bq\cdot\bR_{\ell'})}\\ \times\sum_{m'n'\kappa\alpha}\mathcal U_{mm'\bk+\bq}\,g_{m'n'\kappa\alpha}(\bR_\ell,\bR_{\ell'})\\ \times\mathcal U^\dagger_{n'n\bk}\Big(\frac{\hbar}{2M_\kappa\omega_{\bq\nu}}\Big)^{1/2}e_{\kappa\alpha,\nu}(\bq) .\end{gathered} \tag{A4.18}$$

As a function of $\bq$, is a Fourier series whose coefficients are the couplings to the displaced atom in cell $\ell'$; as a function of $\bk$, one whose coefficients are the hops from cell $\ell$. The recipe is: compute $g$ on a coarse grid of $N_{\mathrm c}$ points along each reciprocal vector; invert , which is a discrete Fourier transform; keep the coefficients inside the Wigner–Seitz supercell of the coarse grid, the lattice vectors $\bR_\ell$ that lie closer to the origin than to any other vector of the superlattice spanned by $N_{\mathrm c}\ba_1$, $N_{\mathrm c}\ba_2$ and $N_{\mathrm c}\ba_3$, with $\ba_j$ the primitive vectors; and evaluate at any $\bk$ and $\bq$. This works when the coefficients have died off within the Wigner–Seitz supercell.15

In $\bR_\ell$ they decay like the overlap of two Wannier functions, exponentially in an insulator. In $\bR_{\ell'}$ they decay like the potential created by one displaced atom, screened by the electrons. In a nonpolar insulator that potential falls off at least as fast as $1/R^3$, and the interpolation converges quickly: in diamond the couplings drop by four orders of magnitude within 12 Å. In a polar crystal it is the dipole potential of the displaced Born charge, which falls off as $1/R^2$. Its Fourier transform is the $1/q$ of , and no Fourier series with finitely many terms can reproduce a function that diverges at every point equivalent to $\Gamma$. The truncated series rings near each $\Gamma$, the coefficients near the edge of the Wigner–Seitz supercell are polluted, and the error decreases only slowly as the coarse grid is refined. The review notes that this singularity corresponds to the Fröhlich coupling.

The repair uses the fact that the singular part is known analytically:

  1. On the coarse grid, subtract the long-range vertex : $g^{\mathrm{sr}}=g-g^{\mathrm{lr}}$.

    $g^{\mathrm{lr}}$ contains the dipole field and its $1/q$ singularity at every image of $\Gamma$; what remains comes from short-range potentials.

  2. Interpolate $g^{\mathrm{sr}}$ with .

    Its real-space coefficients decay quickly, as in a nonpolar crystal.

  3. On the fine grid, add $g^{\mathrm{lr}}$ back, computed from . For small $\bq+\bG$ the overlap is evaluated as $\langle\psi_{m\bk+\bq}|\ee^{\ii(\bq+\bG)\cdot\br}|\psi_{n\bk}\rangle\simeq[\mathcal U_{\bk+\bq}\,\mathcal U^\dagger_\bk]_{mn}$.

    In the Wannier basis the band mixtures $\sum_n\psi_{n\bk}\,\mathcal U_{nm\bk}$ vary smoothly with $\bk$, so their periodic parts at $\bk$ and $\bk+\bq$ nearly coincide and their overlap is close to the unit matrix; only the rotations to the Bloch bands remain. As $\bq+\bG\to0$ the product tends to $\delta_{mn}$.

In practice the sum over $\bG$ is limited to small $|\bq+\bG|$ by a factor $\ee^{-r_{\mathrm c}^2|\bq+\bG|^2}$, with a cutoff length $r_{\mathrm c}$ of the order of the cell size. The results do not depend on $r_{\mathrm c}$ as long as the coarse grid resolves what the factor removes: that part is smooth in $\bq$, extends over about $2r_{\mathrm c}$ in real space, and ends up in $g^{\mathrm{sr}}$.16 For anatase TiO₂ the remainder $g^{\mathrm{sr}}$ stays below about 150 meV and varies smoothly, while the full $g$ diverges on both sides of $\Gamma$; a coarse $4\times4\times4$ grid then reproduces direct calculations. Phonon dispersions of polar crystals are interpolated in the same way, with $\Phi^{\mathrm{dd}}$ playing the part of $g^{\mathrm{lr}}$. reproduces the whole logic in one dimension.

Where electrostatics stops

Every factor of is electrostatic: the kernel is the instantaneous Coulomb interaction, and $\boldsymbol\varepsilon_\infty$ is a static response of the electrons. The word retardation is used for two different delays, and contains neither.

  • The electrons' delay. Electrons need time to screen a change, about $\hbar$ divided by their excitation energy. In an insulator that energy is the band gap, a few eV, so the delay is much shorter than a vibration period, $2\pi/\omega_{\mathrm{LO}}$ with $\hbar\omega_{\mathrm{LO}}$ of tens of meV, and a static $\boldsymbol\varepsilon_\infty$ is accurate. It fails when free carriers are present, as in doped polar semiconductors, whose collective oscillation, the plasmon, can be as slow as the phonon; the two then mix into coupled modes (Section B.9). Section A.6 treats the same delay in metals.
  • The light's delay. Step 3 of the derivation of assumed an electrostatic field. An oscillating polarization is also a current, $\partial\bP/\partial t$. The part of that current along $\bq$, which an LO wave has, creates only a longitudinal field, fixed at each instant by the bound charges, so electrostatics holds for it exactly. The part transverse to $\bq$, which a TO wave has, radiates a transverse electromagnetic field. That field is negligible while light in the crystal, of speed $c/\sqrt{\varepsilon}$ with $\varepsilon$ the dielectric constant at the frequency of the wave, is much faster than the pattern of the wave, $\omega\ll cq/\sqrt{\varepsilon}$; it becomes resonant where the two match. The optical frequencies cross the line $\omega=cq/\sqrt{\varepsilon}$ near
    $$q_{\mathrm c}=\frac{\sqrt{\varepsilon_{\mathrm s}}\,\omega_{\mathrm{TO}}}{c}=\frac{\sqrt{\varepsilon_\infty}\,\omega_{\mathrm{LO}}}{c} , \tag{A4.19}$$
    where $c$ is the speed of light and the two forms are equal by .

For GaAs, $q_{\mathrm c}=6\times10^{-5}$ Å⁻¹, 3.6 times $\omega_{\mathrm{TO}}/c$ and about $5\times10^{-5}$ of the size of the Brillouin zone (about 1.1 Å⁻¹); the inverse polaron length, $1/r_{\mathrm p}=0.025$ Å⁻¹, lies 400 times higher. Electrostatics is therefore exact for all but a tiny region around $\Gamma$. Inside that region, the light cone, a TO phonon, dark for electrostatics, mixes with light into a phonon polariton, and whether the LO and TO frequencies differ at $\bq=0$ depends on the order in which the limits $\bq\to0$ and $c\to\infty$ are taken. What the electrostatic dipole picture leaves out

The dipole is the first term of a multipole series. The next one, from dynamical quadrupoles, adds a long-range contribution that stays finite as $q\to0$ but depends on the direction of $\bq$; it was worked out after the review. In piezoelectric crystals such as GaAs and GaN, acoustic waves also couple at long range: a strained cell develops a polarization, partly through the internal relaxation of its atoms, which the derivation box on acoustic modes neglected, and partly through the quadrupoles.18 Fröhlich's form adds assumptions of its own: one isotropic LO mode, the term $\bG=0$ and intraband scattering. Crystals with several LO branches, such as anatase TiO₂, need the full , and the classic Fröhlich model further assumes a parabolic band and an LO frequency independent of $\bq$. Free carriers screen the long-range field at small $q$. And in a single two-dimensional sheet the field of a dipole wave spreads into the space above and below: the coupling stays finite as $q\to0$, and the LO–TO splitting vanishes at $\Gamma$ (Section B.10).

Where these objects go next

The Fröhlich vertex is the polar part of the phonon-mediated interaction of Section A.5. It dominates the scattering of electrons in polar semiconductors: the emission of LO phonons sets a threshold in the relaxation of hot carriers and limits the room-temperature mobility of GaAs (Section A.8, Section A.9), and its $1/q^2$ makes the adiabatic zero-point renormalization of band gaps diverge, the Fröhlich catastrophe of Section A.8.

Part B returns to every object of this section. Section B.1 rederives the polar vertex from the EPP manuscript and proves why the phase on the atom is needed. Section B.2 couples the Born-charge dipoles to the electromagnetic field, and Section B.3 turns them into the dielectric function of a polar crystal. The LO–TO splitting returns as a phonon self-energy from the exchange of photons (Section B.7), and $\Phi^{\mathrm{dd}}$ as its electrostatic limit (Section B.9). Check yourself: three short questions

  1. Silicon has two identical atoms per cell, related by an inversion through the centre of their bond. What are their Born charges? Does silicon have an LO–TO splitting or a Fröhlich coupling?

    Answer. The inversion maps one atom onto the other and leaves a Born-charge tensor unchanged, because both the displacement and the dipole change sign; so $\mathbf Z^*_1=\mathbf Z^*_2$. The sum rule then forces $\mathbf Z^*_1=\mathbf Z^*_2=0$. Without Born charges, $\Phi^{\mathrm{dd}}=0$ and $g^{\mathrm{lr}}=0$: the three optical modes of silicon stay degenerate at $\Gamma$ (about 64 meV), there is no Fröhlich coupling, and Wannier interpolation needs no dipole correction.

  2. MgO has $\hbar\omega_{\mathrm{LO}}=89$ meV, $\varepsilon_\infty=2.96$ and $\varepsilon_{\mathrm s}=9.8$. What is $\hbar\omega_{\mathrm{TO}}$? How does Fröhlich's strength $1/\varepsilon_\infty-1/\varepsilon_{\mathrm s}$ compare with that of GaAs?

    Answer. By , $\hbar\omega_{\mathrm{TO}}=89\ \text{meV}\times(2.96/9.8)^{1/2}=49$ meV. The strength is $1/2.96-1/9.8=0.236$, against $1/10.9-1/12.9=0.014$ for GaAs: 17 times larger, because in MgO the ions provide most of the static screening.

  3. An electron in GaAs is scattered from $\bk$ to $\bk+\bq$ with $q=0.05$ Å⁻¹. Compare its coupling to the LO phonon with its coupling to a longitudinal acoustic phonon of the same $\bq$.

    Answer. From , $|g^{\mathrm{lr}}|=(0.032\ \text{eV Å}^{-1})/(0.05\ \text{Å}^{-1})=0.64$ eV. The deformation-potential coupling is $E_{\mathrm{def}}\,(\hbar q/2M_{\mathrm c}v_{\mathrm s})^{1/2}$; with $E_{\mathrm{def}}=7$ eV, $M_{\mathrm c}=144.6$ amu and $v_{\mathrm s}=5.2$ km/s, $\hbar/2M_{\mathrm c}v_{\mathrm s}=4.2\times10^{-4}$ Å, so $|g|=7\ \text{eV}\times(2.1\times10^{-5})^{1/2}=0.032$ eV. The LO coupling is 20 times larger.

$V^{\mathrm{lr}}(\br)$, $\Delta_{\bq\nu}V^{\mathrm{lr}}$
potential energy of an electron in the long-range field of a polar lattice wave (eV), ; the same per unit phonon field. EPP: $\Delta V^{\mathcal L}_{\rm cr}$ (Eq. 123).
$g^{\mathrm{lr}}_{mn\nu}(\bk,\bq)$, $g^{\mathrm{sr}}$
long-range (dipole, Fröhlich-type) part of the electron–phonon matrix element, (eV); the short-range rest $g^{\mathrm{sr}}=g-g^{\mathrm{lr}}$. Giustino: $g^{\mathcal L}_{mn\nu}$, $g^{\mathcal S}_{mn\nu}$ (Eq. 180), with $N_p$ inside the square root. EPP: $g^{\mathcal L}_{nm\alpha}(\mathbf k,\mathbf q)$ (Eq. 131), with $N^{-1/2}$ inside, shifted labels and no basis phase.
$E_{\mathrm{def}}$
acoustic deformation potential: shift of a band edge per unit relative change of the cell volume (eV), as in Section A.2. Giustino: $E_{1,n\mathbf k}$ (Eq. 6).
$\alpha_{\mathrm F}$
Fröhlich's dimensionless coupling constant, . Printed in neither source; usually written $\alpha$.
$m^*$
effective mass of an electron or hole in its band, quoted in units of the electron mass $m_{\mathrm e}$.
$r_{\mathrm p}$
polaron length $(\hbar/2m^*\omega_{\mathrm{LO}})^{1/2}$ (Å), within which confinement costs one LO phonon energy; $1/r_{\mathrm p}$ is the inverse polaron length.
$w_{m\ell}(\br)$, $\mathcal U_{nm\bk}$
Wannier function $m$ centred in cell $\ell$; unitary matrix that mixes the Bloch bands of a group at $\bk$ to build the Wannier functions. Giustino: $\mathrm w_{mp}$, $U_{nm\mathbf k}$ (Eqs. 175–176).
$g_{mn\kappa\alpha}(\bR_\ell,\bR_{\ell'})$
electron–phonon coupling in the Wannier representation, (eV/Å). Giustino: $g_{mn\kappa\alpha}(\mathbf R_p,\mathbf R_{p'})$ (Eq. 177).
$N_{\mathrm c}$
number of points of the coarse grid along each reciprocal vector.
$r_{\mathrm c}$
cutoff length in the factor $\ee^{-r_{\mathrm c}^2|\bq+\bG|^2}$ that limits the sum over $\bG$ (Å). Giustino: $a=r_{\mathrm c}^2$ (footnote 7).
$q_{\mathrm c}$; $c$
light-cone wavevector , below which light mixes with the polar optical phonons (Å⁻¹); speed of light.
