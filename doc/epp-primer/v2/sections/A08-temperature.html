Band gaps versus temperature, lifetimes and the Fröhlich catastrophe

Atoms never stand still, not even at zero temperature, so every band energy is shifted by the lattice and every excited carrier eventually scatters. This section turns the self-energies of Section A.7 into numbers that experiments measure: the temperature dependence of band gaps, with the zero-point renormalization that remains at zero temperature, and the lifetimes of carriers. It also shows why the adiabatic formula for the shift, used for decades, diverges in polar crystals, and why the cure is the phonon energy and not the speed of light.

The picture: electrons in a vibrating lattice

A calculation with clamped nuclei, by density-functional theory or GW, gives every Bloch state $\psi_{n\bk}$ a sharp energy $E_{n\bk}$. Real nuclei move. Even at zero temperature $T$ each phonon mode keeps its zero-point motion, and at finite $T$ each mode $\bq\nu$ holds on average $n_{\bq\nu}$ phonons. Every atom is therefore spread over a region whose mean-square size is a sum over the modes, each weighted by $2n_{\bq\nu}+1$ (Section A.1). An electron feels this motion in two ways, and both are of second order in the displacements:

  • the Debye–Waller effect: the electron sees the crystal potential averaged over the vibrations, which blurs it. For ions that move rigidly with their potentials, the part of the Fourier component of the potential at a reciprocal lattice vector $\bG$ that comes from atom $\kappa$ is multiplied by $\exp[-\langle(\bG\cdot\bu_\kappa)^2\rangle/2]$, where $\bu_\kappa$ is the displacement of that atom. The square of this factor reduces the Bragg peaks of x-ray diffraction and gives the effect its name. To second order the blurring is a static shift of every energy;
  • the Fan–Migdal effect: the electron emits and reabsorbs virtual phonons, phonons that exist only in intermediate states that do not conserve energy (Section A.7). It displaces the ions around it, and the displaced ions act back on it. This is the lattice analogue of the polarization of the other electrons that GW describes.

Both effects are proportional to the mean-square displacement, so both carry the factor $(2n_{\bq\nu}+1)\,\hbar/2M_\kappa\omega_{\bq\nu}$. At $T=0$ they leave a shift, the zero-point renormalization (ZPR); as the temperature rises and $n_{\bq\nu}$ grows, the shift grows with it. The shifts are not small. The direct gap of diamond, made of light atoms with large zero-point amplitudes, is reduced by more than 0.5 eV at $T=0$; the indirect gap of silicon by about 60 meV. In most semiconductors the gap shrinks on heating, by a few tenths of a meV per kelvin at room temperature.

The same coupling makes states decay. A carrier put into $\psi_{n\bk}$ does not stay there: it emits or absorbs a phonon and moves to another state. Its energy level acquires a width, and the inverse width is its lifetime, from femtoseconds to picoseconds in common semiconductors.   3. the Varshni curve of a gap against temperature, and how the ZPR is read off it;
  4. lifetimes as a Fermi golden rule, with the emission threshold of polar semiconductors;
  5. why the adiabatic formula diverges in polar crystals, the Fröhlich catastrophe;
  6. methods that average over frozen distortions instead of using perturbation theory.

Quasiparticle energies: where the pole moves

Section A.5 described an added electron by its propagator $G$. With clamped nuclei, the propagator of state $n\bk$ has a single pole at $E_{n\bk}$: an electron added to that state keeps its energy forever. The phonons move the pole and push it off the real axis. Section A.7 wrote the propagator of one state with the phonons included. It is the diagonal element of the Dyson equation in the basis of Bloch states, for the retarded propagator:2

$$\begin{gathered}G_{n\bk}(\omega)\\ =\frac{1}{\hbar(\omega+\ii\eta)-E_{n\bk}-\Sigma^{\mathrm{ep}}_{n\bk}(\omega)},\\ \Sigma^{\mathrm{ep}}_{n\bk}(\omega)=\Sigma^{\mathrm{FM}}_{n\bk}(\omega)+\Sigma^{\mathrm{DW}}_{n\bk} .\end{gathered} \tag{A8.1}$$

$\Sigma^{\mathrm{ep}}_{n\bk}(\omega)$
diagonal element of the electron–phonon self-energy of Section A.7, the part of the electron self-energy due to the phonons: the sum of the Fan–Migdal and Debye–Waller self-energies $\Sigma^{\mathrm{FM}}$, $\Sigma^{\mathrm{DW}}$. It is an energy (meV) written as a function of $\omega$, so it is evaluated at an energy $E$ as $\Sigma^{\mathrm{ep}}_{n\bk}(E/\hbar)$

Off-diagonal elements $\Sigma^{\mathrm{ep}}_{nn'}$ have been dropped, which is safe for a nondegenerate state when the coupling is weak; a degenerate set of states needs the matrix form. The small change that the phonons cause in the purely electronic GW self-energy is neglected, as in Section A.7. The pole follows in three steps:

  1. The pole is where the denominator vanishes. Call its complex energy $z$:

    $$z-E_{n\bk}-\Sigma^{\mathrm{ep}}_{n\bk}(z/\hbar)=0 .$$
    Near a zero of the denominator, $G_{n\bk}$ behaves as $Z^{\mathrm{qp}}_{n\bk}/(\hbar\omega-z)$: the propagator of a single level of complex energy $z$, with the weight $Z^{\mathrm{qp}}_{n\bk}$ of Section A.7, a constant close to 1 where $\Sigma$ varies slowly with energy.

  2. Write $z=E^{\mathrm{qp}}_{n\bk}+\ii y$ with real $E^{\mathrm{qp}}_{n\bk}$ and $y$, and assume that $\Sigma$ changes little over the small distance $|y|$ from the real axis: $\Sigma^{\mathrm{ep}}_{n\bk}(z/\hbar)\simeq\Sigma^{\mathrm{ep}}_{n\bk}(E^{\mathrm{qp}}_{n\bk}/\hbar)$.

    The shift and the width are of second order in the coupling, while $\Sigma$ varies on the scale of phonon and band energies. The assumption fails where $\Sigma$ has structure on the scale of the width, for example at the threshold for phonon emission met below.

  3. Take the real and the imaginary parts of the pole condition:

    $$\begin{gathered}E^{\mathrm{qp}}_{n\bk}=E_{n\bk}+\mathrm{Re}\,\Sigma^{\mathrm{ep}}_{n\bk}\big(E^{\mathrm{qp}}_{n\bk}/\hbar\big),\\ y=\mathrm{Im}\,\Sigma^{\mathrm{ep}}_{n\bk}\big(E^{\mathrm{qp}}_{n\bk}/\hbar\big)\le0 .\end{gathered} \tag{A8.2}$$
    The imaginary part of a retarded self-energy is never positive, so $y\le0$: the pole lies just below the real axis, as for every retarded function. Its distance $|y|$ from the axis sets the lifetime, the subject of the lifetime subsection below.

The first line of is the quasiparticle equation of Section A.7. It is an equation for $E^{\mathrm{qp}}_{n\bk}$, because the self-energy is evaluated at the unknown energy itself. This is the structure of Brillouin–Wigner perturbation theory; evaluating the self-energy at $E_{n\bk}$ instead is the Rayleigh–Schrödinger form.

Rayleigh–Schrödinger and Brillouin–Wigner perturbation theory skip if familiar

Take a Hamiltonian $H_0+V$, the eigenstates $|m\rangle$ of $H_0$ with energies $E_m$, and a nondegenerate level $|n\rangle$ among them. Write $V_{mn}=\langle m|V|n\rangle$. To second order in $V$, Rayleigh–Schrödinger perturbation theory gives the energy

$$E=E_n+V_{nn}+\sum_{m\neq n}\frac{|V_{mn}|^2}{E_n-E_m},$$

with the unperturbed energy $E_n$ in every denominator. Brillouin–Wigner perturbation theory puts the exact energy there instead,

$$E=E_n+V_{nn}+\sum_{m\neq n}\frac{|V_{mn}|^2}{E-E_m},$$

an equation that is solved for $E$ self-consistently. Writing $E-E_m=(E_n-E_m)+(E-E_n)$ and expanding shows that the two agree to second order in $V$ and differ in higher orders. A second-order self-energy evaluated at the unknown energy, as in , has the Brillouin–Wigner structure; evaluated at the unperturbed energy, it gives the Rayleigh–Schrödinger result.

On shell and off shell

Three ways of solving are in use. They differ in the energy at which the frequency-dependent self-energy is evaluated.

  • On shell: $\Sigma$ is evaluated at the clamped-nuclei energy, $E^{\mathrm{qp}}_{n\bk}\simeq E_{n\bk}+\mathrm{Re}\,\Sigma^{\mathrm{ep}}_{n\bk}(E_{n\bk}/\hbar)$. The name comes from the mass shell of particle physics, the set of energies that a free particle can have; here it is the unperturbed band energy. This is Rayleigh–Schrödinger perturbation theory to second order in the coupling.
  • Off shell, self-consistent: is solved as it stands, with $\Sigma$ evaluated at $E^{\mathrm{qp}}_{n\bk}$. This is the Brillouin–Wigner form.
  • Off shell, linearized: $\mathrm{Re}\,\Sigma$ is expanded to first order about $E_{n\bk}$,
    $$\begin{aligned}&\mathrm{Re}\,\Sigma^{\mathrm{ep}}_{n\bk}(E^{\mathrm{qp}}_{n\bk}/\hbar)\simeq\mathrm{Re}\,\Sigma^{\mathrm{ep}}_{n\bk}(E_{n\bk}/\hbar)\\ &\quad+(E^{\mathrm{qp}}_{n\bk}-E_{n\bk})\,\frac{\partial\,\mathrm{Re}\,\Sigma^{\mathrm{ep}}_{n\bk}}{\partial(\hbar\omega)} .\end{aligned}$$
    Solving for the shift gives $E^{\mathrm{qp}}_{n\bk}\simeq E_{n\bk}+Z^{\mathrm{qp}}_{n\bk}\,\mathrm{Re}\,\Sigma^{\mathrm{ep}}_{n\bk}(E_{n\bk}/\hbar)$, with the quasiparticle weight $Z^{\mathrm{qp}}_{n\bk}=\big[1-\partial\,\mathrm{Re}\,\Sigma^{\mathrm{ep}}_{n\bk}/\partial(\hbar\omega)\big]^{-1}$ of , here evaluated at $\hbar\omega=E_{n\bk}$ instead of at $E^{\mathrm{qp}}_{n\bk}$.

All three agree to second order in the coupling. They differ in higher orders, where the self-energy of Section A.7, built from a single phonon line, is incomplete anyway, so the self-consistent form is not automatically better. Off-shell evaluation matters where $\Sigma$ changes quickly with energy near $E_{n\bk}$: within a phonon energy of the Fermi level of a metal (the kinks of Section A.7), near the threshold for phonon emission in a semiconductor, and when the coupling is strong. A single pole then no longer describes the state, and the spectral function is needed.

A worked example: the band edge of a polar semiconductor

The simplest case with all these features is an electron at the bottom of an otherwise empty conduction band of a polar semiconductor, at $T=0$. Take one parabolic band, $E_{\bk}=\hbar^2k^2/2m^*$ with the effective mass $m^*$ of Section A.4, measured from its minimum at $\bk=0$, and one dispersionless longitudinal optical phonon, with the same energy $\hbar\omega_{\mathrm{LO}}$ at every $\bq$. Near the zone centre the coupling is dominated by the long-range Fröhlich interaction of Section A.4, , which for one isotropic LO branch and a transition within the band reads3

$$|g(q)|^2=\frac{4\pi e^2}{\OV q^2}\,\frac{\hbar\omega_{\mathrm{LO}}}{2}\Big(\frac1{\varepsilon_\infty}-\frac1{\varepsilon_{\mathrm s}}\Big) . \tag{A8.3}$$

Inserting into the Fan–Migdal self-energy with an empty band and no phonons present leaves a single term, the emission of a virtual LO phonon. Call the result $\Sigma_{\bk}(\omega)$, the self-energy of the state $\bk$ of this band (the band index is dropped). The sum over $\bq$ has a closed form, below, which sets the natural wavevector of the problem, the polaron wavevector

$$q_{\mathrm{pol}}=\Big(\frac{2m^*\omega_{\mathrm{LO}}}{\hbar}\Big)^{1/2},$$

at which the kinetic energy $\hbar^2q^2/2m^*$ equals one phonon energy. It is the inverse of the polaron length $r_{\mathrm p}$ of Section A.4, the size of the cloud of lattice polarization that dresses the electron: $1/q_{\mathrm{pol}}=r_{\mathrm p}=40$ Å in GaAs. plots the self-energy and solves graphically: the solution is where the straight line $\hbar\omega-E_{\bk}$ crosses the curve $\mathrm{Re}\,\Sigma$.

The lowest energy at which the electron can emit a real phonon is that of an electron at the band bottom plus one LO phonon, $\hbar\omega_{\mathrm{LO}}$: the emission threshold. Below it, $\hbar\omega\lt\hbar\omega_{\mathrm{LO}}$, no intermediate state has the energy $\hbar\omega$, and the self-energy is real:4

$$\begin{aligned}\Sigma_{\bk}(\omega)&=-\alpha_{\mathrm F}\hbar\omega_{\mathrm{LO}}\Big(\frac{\hbar\omega_{\mathrm{LO}}}{E_{\bk}}\Big)^{1/2}\\ &\quad\times\operatorname{arctan}\Big(\frac{E_{\bk}}{\hbar\omega_{\mathrm{LO}}-\hbar\omega}\Big)^{1/2},\\ \Sigma_{\mathbf 0}(\omega)&=-\alpha_{\mathrm F}\hbar\omega_{\mathrm{LO}}\Big(\frac{\hbar\omega_{\mathrm{LO}}}{\hbar\omega_{\mathrm{LO}}-\hbar\omega}\Big)^{1/2} .\end{aligned} \tag{A8.4}$$
$\Sigma_{\bk}(\omega)$, $\Sigma_{\mathbf 0}(\omega)$
self-energy of the state $\bk$ of the single band (the band index is dropped) and its value at the band minimum (meV)
$\alpha_{\mathrm F}$
Fröhlich's dimensionless coupling constant : $(e^2/\hbar)(m^*/2\hbar\omega_{\mathrm{LO}})^{1/2}$ times $(1/\varepsilon_\infty-1/\varepsilon_{\mathrm s})$, which is 0.071 for GaAs, 0.44 for GaN and 1.7 for MgO

Above the threshold the self-energy acquires an imaginary part, because the electron can now emit a real LO phonon; at $\bk=0$ it is $\Sigma_{\mathbf 0}(\omega)=-\ii\,\alpha_{\mathrm F}\hbar\omega_{\mathrm{LO}}\,[\hbar\omega_{\mathrm{LO}}/(\hbar\omega-\hbar\omega_{\mathrm{LO}})]^{1/2}$.

Derivation: the self-energy of the polar band edge

  1. With $f=0$ and $n_{\mathrm B}(\omega_{\mathrm{LO}})=0$, the Fan–Migdal self-energy keeps one term:

    $$\begin{aligned}\Sigma_{\bk}(\omega)&=\frac1{N_\bq}\sum_\bq|g(q)|^2\\ &\quad\times\frac{1}{\hbar(\omega+\ii\eta)-E_{\bk+\bq}-\hbar\omega_{\mathrm{LO}}} .\end{aligned}$$
    An empty band has no occupied final states, so the absorption-type term, weighted by $f+n_{\mathrm B}$, vanishes, and the emission term has weight $1-f+n_{\mathrm B}=1$. For $\hbar\omega\lt\hbar\omega_{\mathrm{LO}}$ the denominator never vanishes and $\eta$ can be dropped.

  2. Write the denominator as $-(\hbar^2/2m^*)\big(|\bk+\bq|^2+q_\omega^2\big)$ with $q_\omega=q_{\mathrm{pol}}(1-\omega/\omega_{\mathrm{LO}})^{1/2}$, and turn the grid average into an integral, $\frac1{N_\bq}\sum_\bq\to\frac{\OV}{(2\pi)^3}\int\dd^3q=\frac{\OV}{4\pi^2}\int_0^\infty q^2\dd q\int_{-1}^{1}\dd(\cos\theta)$, where $\theta$ is the angle between $\bk$ and $\bq$.

    $E_{\bk+\bq}=\hbar^2(k^2+2kq\cos\theta+q^2)/2m^*$, and $\hbar\omega_{\mathrm{LO}}-\hbar\omega=\hbar^2q_\omega^2/2m^*$ by the definition of $q_{\mathrm{pol}}$. The integrand falls as $1/q^4$, so extending the integral to infinity changes little when the zone is much larger than $q_{\mathrm{pol}}$ (the finite zone is kept in ).

  3. The angular integral is elementary:

    $$\begin{aligned}&\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{\dd(\cos\theta)}{k^2+q^2+2kq\cos\theta+q_\omega^2}\\ &\quad=\frac{1}{2kq}\ln\frac{(q+k)^2+q_\omega^2}{(q-k)^2+q_\omega^2} .\end{aligned}$$
    The denominator is linear in $\cos\theta$.

  4. The remaining integral is

    $$\int_0^\infty\frac{\dd q}{q}\ln\frac{(q+k)^2+q_\omega^2}{(q-k)^2+q_\omega^2}=2\pi\operatorname{arctan}\frac{k}{q_\omega} .$$
    Both sides vanish at $k=0$. Their derivatives with respect to $k$ agree: on the left the integrand becomes even in $q$, so the integral is half the integral over the whole real axis, which the residues at $q=\pm k+\ii q_\omega$ give as $4\pi q_\omega/(k^2+q_\omega^2)$. Two limits check the result: for $k\ll q_\omega$ both sides are $2\pi k/q_\omega$, and for $q_\omega\to0$ both are $\pi^2$.

  5. Collect the factors. By , $|g(q)|^2q^2$ does not depend on $q$, and the prefactor of the arctangent is $-\OV m^*|g(q)|^2q^2/(2\pi\hbar^2k)=-\alpha_{\mathrm F}\hbar\omega_{\mathrm{LO}}\,q_{\mathrm{pol}}/k$. With $k/q_\omega=[E_{\bk}/(\hbar\omega_{\mathrm{LO}}-\hbar\omega)]^{1/2}$ and $q_{\mathrm{pol}}/k=(\hbar\omega_{\mathrm{LO}}/E_{\bk})^{1/2}$ this is .

    $\OV|g(q)|^2q^2=2\pi e^2\hbar\omega_{\mathrm{LO}}(1/\varepsilon_\infty-1/\varepsilon_{\mathrm s})$, and inserting the definitions of $\alpha_{\mathrm F}$ and $q_{\mathrm{pol}}$ gives $\OV m^*|g(q)|^2q^2/2\pi\hbar^2=\alpha_{\mathrm F}\hbar\omega_{\mathrm{LO}}q_{\mathrm{pol}}$. For $k\to0$, $\operatorname{arctan}(k/q_\omega)/k\to1/q_\omega$, which gives the second line.

  6. Above the threshold, $q_\omega$ becomes imaginary. Following $\omega+\ii\eta$ around the branch point gives a negative imaginary part, the form quoted in the text at $\bk=0$; uses the continuation for every $\bk$.

    The retarded prescription fixes the branch: $q_\omega=-\ii\,q_{\mathrm{pol}}(\omega/\omega_{\mathrm{LO}}-1)^{1/2}$ for $\omega>\omega_{\mathrm{LO}}$.

At the band edge, $\bk=0$, the on-shell shift is $\Sigma_{\mathbf 0}(0)=-\alpha_{\mathrm F}\hbar\omega_{\mathrm{LO}}$, the textbook shift of a weakly coupled polaron, the electron dressed by its cloud of lattice polarization. Off shell, reads $E^{\mathrm{qp}}=-\alpha_{\mathrm F}\hbar\omega_{\mathrm{LO}}\,[\hbar\omega_{\mathrm{LO}}/(\hbar\omega_{\mathrm{LO}}-E^{\mathrm{qp}})]^{1/2}$, and the linearized form, with the slope $-\alpha_{\mathrm F}/2$ of $\Sigma_{\mathbf 0}$ at zero energy, gives $E^{\mathrm{qp}}=-\alpha_{\mathrm F}\hbar\omega_{\mathrm{LO}}/(1+\alpha_{\mathrm F}/2)$. For GaAs the three values are $-2.58$, $-2.50$ and $-2.49$ meV, within 4% of each other. For GaN ($\alpha_{\mathrm F}=0.44$) they are $-40.9$, $-34.8$ and $-33.4$ meV, and for MgO ($\alpha_{\mathrm F}\approx1.7$) they spread from $-154$ meV on shell to $-104$ and $-82$ meV off shell. The spread signals that the coupling is too strong for second-order theory. Here the weak-coupling expansion of the exact polaron energy, $-(\alpha_{\mathrm F}+0.0159\,\alpha_{\mathrm F}^2+\dots)\hbar\omega_{\mathrm{LO}}$, shows that the on-shell value is the more accurate one at weak and intermediate coupling: the off-shell forms add a term $+\alpha_{\mathrm F}^2\hbar\omega_{\mathrm{LO}}/2$ of the wrong sign.5

Away from the band edge, panel (b) of shows where the linearized form fails. As $E_{\bk}$ approaches $\hbar\omega_{\mathrm{LO}}$ from below, the slope of $\mathrm{Re}\,\Sigma_{\bk}$ at $\hbar\omega=E_{\bk}$, $-\alpha_{\mathrm F}\big/\big[2(1-E_{\bk}/\hbar\omega_{\mathrm{LO}})^{1/2}\big]$, grows without limit and $Z^{\mathrm{qp}}$ falls to zero. Above the threshold $\mathrm{Re}\,\Sigma_{\bk}$ is constant near $\hbar\omega=E_{\bk}$, so $Z^{\mathrm{qp}}=1$. The linearized band therefore jumps at $k=q_{\mathrm{pol}}$ from the bare band to the on-shell value. The self-consistent band has no jump: it flattens as it rises towards $\hbar\omega_{\mathrm{LO}}$, which it reaches with zero slope somewhat beyond $k=q_{\mathrm{pol}}$.

The on-shell shift in any crystal

For a general crystal, the self-energies of Section A.7 give the shift explicitly. Evaluate the retarded Fan–Migdal self-energy at temperature $T$ on shell, at $\omega=E_{n\bk}/\hbar$, take its real part, and add the static Debye–Waller self-energy . The result is the on-shell energy shift:6

Each term of the Fan–Migdal sum is a second-order energy shift: a squared coupling over an energy denominator. The first fraction describes a virtual process in which the electron emits a phonon $-\bq\nu$ and lands in $m\,\bk+\bq$; its weight $1-f+n$ combines spontaneous emission (the 1) with emission stimulated by the $n_{\bq\nu}$ phonons already present, reduced by Pauli blocking of an occupied final state. The second fraction is absorption, with weight $f+n$; Section A.7 draws both processes. An intermediate state whose energy, the final electron energy plus the emitted phonon energy (or minus the absorbed one), lies above $E_{n\bk}$ makes the denominator negative and pushes the level down; one below pushes it up. This is the level repulsion of second-order perturbation theory. The Debye–Waller term is a first-order shift in the second-order coupling and has no denominator.

The adiabatic limit: the Allen–Heine formula

Two approximations turn the quasiparticle equation , with these self-energies, into the formula of Allen and Heine (1976), the standard tool of band-gap calculations for four decades. Steps 1 and 2 are the approximations; steps 3 and 4 simplify the result:

  1. Evaluate the self-energy on shell, which gives .

    This is the Rayleigh–Schrödinger form, a single evaluation without self-consistency.

  2. Drop $\hbar\omega_{\bq\nu}$ next to $E_{n\bk}-E_{m\bk+\bq}$ in both denominators.

    Phonon energies are 10 to 200 meV, while the energy differences between bands are of the order of eV. This is the adiabatic approximation for the electron: during the virtual process the lattice does not move, so the electron sees a frozen distortion.

  3. The two fractions now have the same denominator, and their weights add: $(1-f+n_{\bq\nu})+(f+n_{\bq\nu})=1+2n_{\bq\nu}$.

    The occupations of the electrons drop out. The result depends on temperature only through $2n_{\bq\nu}+1=\coth(\hbar\omega_{\bq\nu}/2\kB T)$, the factor of the mean-square displacement of an atom.

  4. Let $\eta\to0$. The real part becomes a principal value, and states degenerate with $n\bk$ are left out of the sum.

    Degenerate states belong to degenerate perturbation theory; their denominators vanish.

The result is the adiabatic Allen–Heine formula:7

$$\begin{aligned}\Delta E_{n\bk}&=\frac1{N_\bq}\sum_{\bq\nu}\Big[{\sum_m}'\frac{|g_{mn\nu}(\bk,\bq)|^2}{E_{n\bk}-E_{m\bk+\bq}}\\ &\quad+g^{\mathrm{DW}}_{nn\nu\nu}(\bk,\bq,-\bq)\Big]\\ &\quad\times(2n_{\bq\nu}+1) .\end{aligned} \tag{A8.6}$$
${\sum_m}'$
sum over bands that leaves out the states with $E_{m\bk+\bq}=E_{n\bk}$

They expanded the band energy of a crystal with frozen displacements to second order and averaged over the displacements with Bose–Einstein statistics, a route that below retraces. Two different "adiabatic" approximations

Both rest on the assumption that $\hbar\omega_{\bq\nu}$ is small next to the electronic energy differences involved, but they apply it to different processes. The adiabatic phonons of Sections A.1 and A.6 use static electronic screening: the electrons follow the ions instantly. In an insulator the electronic excitations that screen a phonon cross the gap, so this is excellent. The adiabatic Allen–Heine formula drops $\hbar\omega_{\bq\nu}$ next to $E_{n\bk}-E_{m\bk+\bq}$: the electron sees a frozen distortion. Near a band extremum the differences $E_{n\bk}-E_{n\bk+\bq}$ within one band vanish as $\bq\to0$, so the second assumption always fails at small $\bq$, even in a wide-gap insulator. With a coupling that stays finite as $q\to0$ that region carries little weight; with the polar coupling $|g|^2\propto1/q^2$ it dominates, which is the root of the Fröhlich catastrophe below.

Debye–Waller from first-order matrix elements

The Debye–Waller term needs the second derivatives of the Kohn–Sham potential $V^{\mathrm{KS}}$ with respect to the displacements $u_{\ell\kappa\alpha}$, which second-order density-functional perturbation theory can compute only at high cost. Allen and Heine avoided them with one physical requirement: moving the whole crystal rigidly cannot change any electron energy, even when the crystal is already distorted. Combined with the rigid-ion approximation of Section A.3, in which the Kohn–Sham potential is a sum of atom-centred pieces carried rigidly by their atoms, this requirement writes the Debye–Waller term with the same first-order matrix elements as the Fan–Migdal term. In steps:

  1. Write the Debye–Waller shift with Cartesian displacements. It is first-order perturbation theory in the second-order part of the potential, $\frac12\sum(\partial^2V^{\mathrm{KS}}/\partial u\,\partial u)\,u\,u$, averaged over the vibrations:

    $$\begin{aligned}&\Sigma^{\mathrm{DW}}_{n\bk}=\frac12\sum_{\ell\kappa\alpha}\sum_{\ell'\kappa'\beta}\langle u_{\ell\kappa\alpha}u_{\ell'\kappa'\beta}\rangle\\ &\ \times\Big\langle\psi_{n\bk}\Big|\frac{\partial^2V^{\mathrm{KS}}}{\partial u_{\ell\kappa\alpha}\,\partial u_{\ell'\kappa'\beta}}\Big|\psi_{n\bk}\Big\rangle .\end{aligned} \tag{A8.8}$$

    The thermal correlation of two displacements follows from the displacement operator , with its eigenvectors $e_{\kappa\alpha,\nu}(\bq)$, and from the average of two phonon fields, $\langle\hat\phi_{\bq\nu}\hat\phi_{\bq'\nu'}\rangle=\delta_{\bq',-\bq}\delta_{\nu\nu'}(2n_{\bq\nu}+1)$ (Section A.1):

    $$\begin{gathered}\langle u_{\ell\kappa\alpha}u_{\ell'\kappa'\beta}\rangle\\ =\frac1{N_\ell}\sum_{\bq\nu}\frac{\hbar\,(2n_{\bq\nu}+1)}{2\omega_{\bq\nu}\sqrt{M_\kappa M_{\kappa'}}}\\ \times e_{\kappa\alpha,\nu}(\bq)\,e^*_{\kappa'\beta,\nu}(\bq)\,\ee^{\ii\bq\cdot(\bR_\ell-\bR_{\ell'})} .\end{gathered} \tag{A8.9}$$
    Inserting into and grouping the derivatives into $\Delta_{\bq\nu}\Delta_{-\bq\nu}V^{\mathrm{KS}}$ gives back the last term of : the two forms of the Debye–Waller term are the same.

  2. Rigid ions: keep only second derivatives with respect to the displacements of one and the same atom, $\ell'\kappa'=\ell\kappa$. This is the Debye–Waller shift in the rigid-ion form of Section A.3:

    $$\begin{aligned}\Sigma^{\mathrm{DW}}_{n\bk}&\simeq\frac12\sum_{\ell\kappa\alpha\beta}\langle u_{\kappa\alpha}u_{\kappa\beta}\rangle\\ &\quad\times\Big\langle\psi_{n\bk}\Big|\frac{\partial^2V^{\mathrm{KS}}}{\partial u_{\ell\kappa\alpha}\,\partial u_{\ell\kappa\beta}}\Big|\psi_{n\bk}\Big\rangle .\end{aligned}$$
    In the rigid-ion approximation each atom-centred piece of $V^{\mathrm{KS}}$ depends on the displacement of its own atom only. The on-site correlation $\langle u_{\kappa\alpha}u_{\kappa\beta}\rangle$, with $\ell'\kappa'=\ell\kappa$, is the same in every cell; its diagonal elements are the mean-square displacements of Section A.1.

  3. Translational invariance of an energy level gives two sum rules. To first and second order in the displacements (derivation below),8

    $$\begin{gathered}\sum_{\ell'\kappa'}\Big\langle\psi_{n\bk}\Big|\frac{\partial V^{\mathrm{KS}}}{\partial u_{\ell'\kappa'\beta}}\Big|\psi_{n\bk}\Big\rangle=0,\\ \sum_{\ell'\kappa'}\Big\langle\psi_{n\bk}\Big|\frac{\partial^2V^{\mathrm{KS}}}{\partial u_{\ell\kappa\alpha}\,\partial u_{\ell'\kappa'\beta}}\Big|\psi_{n\bk}\Big\rangle\\ =-2\,\mathrm{Re}{\sum_{m\bk'}}'\frac{1}{E_{n\bk}-E_{m\bk'}}\\ \times\Big\langle\psi_{n\bk}\Big|\frac{\partial V^{\mathrm{KS}}}{\partial u_{\ell\kappa\alpha}}\Big|\psi_{m\bk'}\Big\rangle\\ \times\sum_{\ell'\kappa'}\Big\langle\psi_{m\bk'}\Big|\frac{\partial V^{\mathrm{KS}}}{\partial u_{\ell'\kappa'\beta}}\Big|\psi_{n\bk}\Big\rangle .\end{gathered} \tag{A8.10}$$
    A rigid translation moves every wavefunction with the crystal and changes no energy, and this holds also for a crystal that is already distorted. The first rule is the electronic counterpart of the acoustic sum rule : it says that the diagonal matrix elements, between $\psi_{n\bk}$ and itself, of the three translations at $\bq=0$ vanish. The matrix elements are integrals over the Born–von Kármán supercell, and the primed sum leaves out states degenerate with $n\bk$.

  4. Apply the rigid-ion approximation to the left side of the second rule: only $\ell'\kappa'=\ell\kappa$ survives, so the on-site second derivative equals the right side. On the right, the sum over $\ell'$ turns $\partial V^{\mathrm{KS}}/\partial u_{\ell'\kappa'\beta}$ into $\partial_{\kappa'\beta,\mathbf 0}V^{\mathrm{KS}}=\sum_{\ell'}\partial V^{\mathrm{KS}}/\partial u_{\ell'\kappa'\beta}$, the change of the potential when every atom $\kappa'$ moves along $\beta$.

    $\partial_{\kappa'\beta,\mathbf 0}V^{\mathrm{KS}}$ is the $\bq=0$ member of $\partial_{\kappa\alpha,\bq}V$ . It has the period of the lattice, so it conserves crystal momentum and connects $\psi_{n\bk}$ only to states with $\bk'=\bk$.

  5. Insert the on-site second derivative into step 2 and sum over the cells $\ell$, which turns the remaining $\partial V^{\mathrm{KS}}/\partial u_{\ell\kappa\alpha}$ into $\partial_{\kappa\alpha,\mathbf 0}V^{\mathrm{KS}}$:

    $$\begin{aligned}&\Sigma^{\mathrm{DW}}_{n\bk}\simeq-\sum_{\kappa\kappa'\alpha\beta}\langle u_{\kappa\alpha}u_{\kappa\beta}\rangle\\ &\ \ \times{\sum_m}'\frac{1}{E_{n\bk}-E_{m\bk}}\\ &\ \ \times\mathrm{Re}\big[\langle\psi_{n\bk}|\partial_{\kappa\alpha,\mathbf 0}V^{\mathrm{KS}}|\psi_{m\bk}\rangle\\ &\quad\ \ \times\langle\psi_{m\bk}|\partial_{\kappa'\beta,\mathbf 0}V^{\mathrm{KS}}|\psi_{n\bk}\rangle\big],\\ &\langle u_{\kappa\alpha}u_{\kappa\beta}\rangle\\ &\ \ =\frac1{N_\bq}\sum_{\bq\nu}\frac{\hbar\,(2n_{\bq\nu}+1)}{2M_\kappa\omega_{\bq\nu}}\\ &\ \ \times e_{\kappa\alpha,\nu}(\bq)\,e^*_{\kappa\beta,\nu}(\bq) .\end{aligned} \tag{A8.11}$$
    The on-site correlation does not depend on $\ell$, so the sum over cells acts on the matrix element alone. The factor $\frac12$ of step 2 cancels the factor 2 of the sum rule.9

  1. Label the displacements by $I=\ell\kappa\alpha$ and $J=\ell'\kappa'\beta$. Freezing them changes the Kohn–Sham potential by $\sum_I(\partial V^{\mathrm{KS}}/\partial u_I)\,u_I+\frac12\sum_{IJ}(\partial^2V^{\mathrm{KS}}/\partial u_I\partial u_J)\,u_Iu_J$. Perturbation theory to second order in $u$ for the nondegenerate level $n\bk$ gives

    $$\begin{gathered}E_{n\bk}(u)=E_{n\bk}+\sum_IA_Iu_I\\ +\frac12\sum_{IJ}B_{IJ}\,u_Iu_J,\\ A_I=\Big\langle\frac{\partial V^{\mathrm{KS}}}{\partial u_I}\Big\rangle,\\ B_{IJ}=\Big\langle\frac{\partial^2V^{\mathrm{KS}}}{\partial u_I\partial u_J}\Big\rangle+2\,\mathrm{Re}\,X_{IJ},\\ X_{IJ}={\sum_{m\bk'}}'\frac{1}{E_{n\bk}-E_{m\bk'}}\\ \times\Big\langle\psi_{n\bk}\Big|\frac{\partial V^{\mathrm{KS}}}{\partial u_I}\Big|\psi_{m\bk'}\Big\rangle\\ \times\Big\langle\psi_{m\bk'}\Big|\frac{\partial V^{\mathrm{KS}}}{\partial u_J}\Big|\psi_{n\bk}\Big\rangle ,\end{gathered}$$
    $\langle\cdot\rangle$ is the expectation value in $\psi_{n\bk}$. First-order perturbation theory in the whole change gives the terms with $A_I$ and the first part of $B_{IJ}$; second-order perturbation theory in its linear part gives $\sum_{IJ}u_Iu_JX_{IJ}$. Since $u_Iu_J$ is symmetric and $X_{JI}=X_{IJ}^*$, only $\mathrm{Re}\,X_{IJ}$ survives. The derivatives are those of the self-consistent potential, which already contain the response of the other electrons.

  2. Translational invariance: $E_{n\bk}(u+\bd)=E_{n\bk}(u)$ for any configuration $u$ and any common displacement $\bd$ of every atom. Differentiating with respect to $d_\beta$ at $\bd=0$ gives $\sum_{J\in\beta}\partial E_{n\bk}/\partial u_J=0$ for every $u$, where $J=\ell'\kappa'\beta$ runs over all atoms $\ell'\kappa'$ with the direction $\beta$ fixed.

    This is the same argument as for the acoustic sum rule , applied to an electron energy instead of the total energy.

  3. At $u=0$ this reads $\sum_{J\in\beta}A_J=0$, the first rule of . Differentiating once more with respect to $u_I$ gives $\sum_{J\in\beta}B_{IJ}=0$, the second rule.

    The invariance holds for every distorted configuration, so its derivative with respect to any further displacement also vanishes.

  4. Averaged over the vibrations, the linear terms vanish and $\langle E_{n\bk}\rangle=E_{n\bk}+\frac12\sum_{IJ}B_{IJ}\langle u_Iu_J\rangle$. The part with $\langle\partial^2V^{\mathrm{KS}}\rangle$ is the Debye–Waller term ; the part $\sum_{IJ}\mathrm{Re}\,X_{IJ}\langle u_Iu_J\rangle$ is the adiabatic Fan–Migdal term of written with Cartesian displacements.

    $\langle u_I\rangle=0$ in a state with definite phonon numbers. This average is Allen and Heine's original route to ; below starts from it too.

Equation needs only the first-order matrix elements of density-functional perturbation theory at $\bq=0$ and the phonon modes, which enter through the on-site displacement tensor $\langle u_{\kappa\alpha}u_{\kappa\beta}\rangle$. It has the shape of a Fan–Migdal term with the opposite sign, with intermediate states at the same $\bk$ and each atom weighted by its own mean-square displacement. This is what translational invariance requires. In the language of the derivation box, the adiabatic Fan–Migdal term weights each pair of displacements $I,J$ by their correlation $\langle u_Iu_J\rangle$, while uses the on-site correlation of each atom instead. In a rigid translation every atom has the same displacement, the two weights are equal, and the two terms cancel exactly. Long-wavelength acoustic phonons, which look locally like a translation, nearly cancel in the same way; without the Debye–Waller term they would contribute a large, spurious shift.

In practice the rigid-ion approximation changes the ZPR of diamond by about 10 meV, and by less than 4 meV in every case that Poncé and coworkers tested. In diamond the Fan–Migdal and Debye–Waller terms are of comparable size, so neither can be dropped. Both terms involve sums over many empty bands, which converge slowly; Gonze, Boulanger and Côté (2011) replaced most of them by the solution of a Sternheimer equation, as in Section A.3.10

Gaps against temperature: the Varshni curve

In 1949 Becker and Fan found that the gap of a semiconductor shrinks on heating, which led Fan to the first theory of the effect in 1951. This shift of the gap to lower energy, a red shift, is called the Varshni effect, after Varshni's empirical formula for it (1967). The review explains its shape with the simplest model that contains the physics:

  • parabolic, nondegenerate valence and conduction bands;
  • one dispersionless (Einstein) phonon of frequency $\omega_0$, as in the models of Section A.7, that couples the band extrema to all other states;
  • Debye–Waller matrix elements much smaller than the Fan–Migdal ones.

  1. Set $\omega_{\bq\nu}=\omega_0$ for every mode in and drop $g^{\mathrm{DW}}$. The factor $2n_{\mathrm B}(\omega_0)+1$ no longer depends on $\bq$ or $\nu$ and comes out of the sum:

    $$\Delta E_{n\bk}(T)=\Delta E^{\mathrm{ZPR}}_{n\bk}\,\big[1+2n_{\mathrm B}(\omega_0)\big].$$
    What remains of the sum is the same at every temperature, and at $T=0$, where $n_{\mathrm B}=0$, it is the ZPR. $n_{\mathrm B}(\omega)$ is the Bose–Einstein function of Section A.1.

  2. At a band extremum the largest terms are those within the same band, $m=n$, at small $q$, whose denominators vanish as $q\to0$:

    $$\begin{gathered}E_{\mathrm c}(\bk_{\mathrm c})-E_{\mathrm c}(\bk_{\mathrm c}+\bq)\simeq-\hbar^2q^2/2m^*_{\mathrm c},\\ E_{\mathrm v}(\bk_{\mathrm v})-E_{\mathrm v}(\bk_{\mathrm v}+\bq)\simeq+\hbar^2q^2/2m^*_{\mathrm v}.\end{gathered}$$
    Near its extremum each band is parabolic. $E_{\mathrm c}(\bk)$ and $E_{\mathrm v}(\bk)$ are the conduction and valence bands, $\bk_{\mathrm c}$ and $\bk_{\mathrm v}$ the positions of the conduction-band minimum and the valence-band maximum, and $m^*_{\mathrm c}$, $m^*_{\mathrm v}>0$ the electron and hole masses.

  3. So the conduction-band minimum moves down, $\Delta E^{\mathrm{ZPR}}_{\mathrm c}\lt0$, and the valence-band maximum moves up, $\Delta E^{\mathrm{ZPR}}_{\mathrm v}>0$; by the gap closes, $\Delta E^{\mathrm{ZPR}}_{\mathrm g}\lt0$:11

    $$\begin{gathered}E^{\mathrm{qp}}_{\mathrm g}(T)=E_{\mathrm g}-\big|\Delta E^{\mathrm{ZPR}}_{\mathrm g}\big|\\ \times\big[1+2n_{\mathrm B}(\omega_0)\big]\\ =E_{\mathrm g}-\big|\Delta E^{\mathrm{ZPR}}_{\mathrm g}\big|\coth\frac{\hbar\omega_0}{2\kB T}\end{gathered} \tag{A8.12}$$
    The states of the same band lie above the conduction-band minimum and below the valence-band maximum, and level repulsion pushes each extremum away from them. $1+2/(\ee^x-1)=\coth(x/2)$.

$\omega_0$

Two regimes follow from the hyperbolic cotangent:

  • Low temperature, $\kB T\ll\hbar\omega_0$: $n_{\mathrm B}$ is exponentially small and the gap sits on a plateau at $E_{\mathrm g}-|\Delta E^{\mathrm{ZPR}}_{\mathrm g}|$, set by zero-point motion alone. The shift stays within about 10% of its zero-point value up to $\kB T\approx\hbar\omega_0/3$, where $\coth(3/2)=1.105$.
  • High temperature, $\kB T\gg\hbar\omega_0$: $\coth(\hbar\omega_0/2\kB T)\simeq2\kB T/\hbar\omega_0+\hbar\omega_0/6\kB T$, and the gap falls linearly with the slope $-2\kB|\Delta E^{\mathrm{ZPR}}_{\mathrm g}|/\hbar\omega_0$.

The straight line that the high-temperature gap follows, extrapolated back to $T=0$, lands on the clamped-nuclei gap $E_{\mathrm g}$, not on the measured gap $E^{\mathrm{qp}}_{\mathrm g}(0)$: the classical part of the motion vanishes at $T=0$, the zero-point part does not. The distance between the intercept and the measured low-temperature gap is the ZPR. This is how ZPRs are extracted from experiment, and the dashed asymptote of shows it.

For the direct gap of diamond, Giustino, Louie and Cohen (2010) confirmed with density-functional theory the ZPR above 0.5 eV that Zollner, Cardona and Gopalan (1992) had found with empirical pseudopotentials. Antonius and coworkers (2014) found that GW corrections to the matrix elements raise the ZPR by up to about 200 meV, to 628 meV, and their curve follows the measured gap between about 120 and 630 K. With $E_{\mathrm g}=7.73$ eV, $\hbar\omega_0=125$ meV and $|\Delta E^{\mathrm{ZPR}}_{\mathrm g}|=0.628$ eV, reproduces that curve: 7.10 eV at 0 K, 7.03 eV at 500 K and 6.95 eV at 650 K. For the indirect gap of silicon, $E_{\mathrm g}=1.23$ eV, $|\Delta E^{\mathrm{ZPR}}_{\mathrm g}|=60$ meV and $\hbar\omega_0=30$ meV give 1.17 eV at 0 K and 1.12 eV at 300 K, close to the measured values.12

The model also predicts an isotope effect. Replace every atom by a heavier isotope, so that every mass $M_\kappa$ grows by the same factor. The force constants do not change, so every $\omega_{\bq\nu}$ scales as $M_\kappa^{-1/2}$, and the squared zero-point amplitude $\hbar/2M_\kappa\omega_{\bq\nu}$ in the squared matrix element scales as $M_\kappa^{-1/2}$. So does the ZPR: heavier isotopes have larger gaps at low temperature. The high-temperature slope $2\kB|\Delta E^{\mathrm{ZPR}}_{\mathrm g}|/\hbar\omega_0$ scales as $M_\kappa^{-1/2}M_\kappa^{1/2}$ and does not depend on the mass, as expected for classical motion, whose mean-square displacement $\kB T/M_\kappa\omega_{\bq\nu}^2$ is set by the force constants alone.

The red shift is common but not universal. Copper halides and lead-halide perovskites show an inverse Varshni effect, a gap that opens on heating, and some chalcopyrites are nonmonotonic. Couplings to other bands, which push an extremum the opposite way (valence states below the conduction-band minimum push it up), or effects outside these formulas such as thermal expansion, can then outweigh the repulsion within each band of step 3.13

Lifetimes: the imaginary part as a golden rule

A carrier added to $\psi_{n\bk}$ leaves it by emitting or absorbing a phonon. In the propagator this appears as the distance $|y|$ of the pole below the real axis, . Two steps connect it to a decay rate:

  1. Transform the single-pole propagator $Z^{\mathrm{qp}}_{n\bk}/(\hbar\omega-z)$ to time with the primer's convention, $G_{n\bk}(t)=\int\frac{\dd\omega}{2\pi}\ee^{-\ii\omega t}G_{n\bk}(\omega)$:

    $$G_{n\bk}(t)=-\frac{\ii}{\hbar}\,Z^{\mathrm{qp}}_{n\bk}\,\Theta(t)\,\ee^{-\ii E^{\mathrm{qp}}_{n\bk}t/\hbar}\,\ee^{-|y|\,t/\hbar} .$$
    For $t>0$ the contour is closed below the real axis around the pole $z$; for $t\lt0$ it is closed above, where there is no pole, which makes the function retarded. $\Theta$ is the unit step. The constant weight multiplies the whole function and does not change how fast it decays.

  2. The probability of finding the carrier still in $\psi_{n\bk}$ is proportional to $|G_{n\bk}(t)|^2\propto\ee^{-2|y|\,t/\hbar}$. It decays at the rate14

    $$\begin{aligned}\frac1{\tau_{n\bk}}&=-\frac2\hbar\,\mathrm{Im}\,\Sigma^{\mathrm{ep}}_{n\bk}\big(E^{\mathrm{qp}}_{n\bk}/\hbar\big)\\ &=\frac{2\,\big|\mathrm{Im}\,\Sigma^{\mathrm{ep}}_{n\bk}\big|}{\hbar} .\end{aligned} \tag{A8.13}$$
    The amplitude decays at half the rate of the probability. The full width at half maximum of the peak of the spectral function is $2|\mathrm{Im}\,\Sigma|=\hbar/\tau$, and its half width at half maximum, called the half width below, is $|\mathrm{Im}\,\Sigma|$. Mixing up the amplitude rate, the probability rate, the half width and the full width is the usual source of factors of 2.
$\tau_{n\bk}$
lifetime of the state, and $1/\tau_{n\bk}$ its scattering rate (fs and ps⁻¹); the retarded $\mathrm{Im}\,\Sigma$ is negative, hence the minus sign

On shell, $E^{\mathrm{qp}}_{n\bk}$ is replaced by $E_{n\bk}$. The Fan–Migdal self-energy has the imaginary part $\mathrm{Im}\,[1/(x+\ii\hbar\eta)]=-\pi\delta(x)$ in each fraction, and the Debye–Waller term has none. The rate is therefore

$$\begin{aligned}&\frac1{\tau_{n\bk}}=\frac{2\pi}{\hbar}\,\frac1{N_\bq}\sum_{\bq m\nu}|g_{mn\nu}(\bk,\bq)|^2\\ &\ \times\Big[(1-f_{m\bk+\bq}+n_{\bq\nu})\\ &\qquad\times\delta(E_{n\bk}-\hbar\omega_{\bq\nu}-E_{m\bk+\bq})\\ &\quad\ +(f_{m\bk+\bq}+n_{\bq\nu})\\ &\qquad\times\delta(E_{n\bk}+\hbar\omega_{\bq\nu}-E_{m\bk+\bq})\Big] .\end{aligned} \tag{A8.14}$$

This is Fermi's golden rule: $2\pi/\hbar$ times a squared matrix element times the number of final states at the right energy, summed over two channels. The first term in the bracket is phonon emission: the carrier drops to a state one phonon energy lower. The second term is absorption: it rises by one phonon energy. For the retarded propagator the weights count the rate of scattering out of $n\bk$ plus the rate of scattering into it: for emission $(1-f)(1+n)+fn=1-f+n$, and for absorption $(1-f)n+f(1+n)=f+n$. In a semiconductor with an empty conduction band, $f=0$, they reduce to $n+1$ and $n$. The Debye–Waller term does not contribute: it is a static potential with real diagonal elements, so it shifts levels but cannot make them decay.

In the one-phonon model of the Varshni curve, dropping $\hbar\omega_0$ in the delta functions gives both channels the same delta function and the total weight $1+2n_{\mathrm B}(\omega_0)$. The half width then follows the same Bose factor as the shift, $|\mathrm{Im}\,\Sigma^{\mathrm{ep}}_{n\bk}(T)|=|\mathrm{Im}\,\Sigma^{\mathrm{ep}}_{n\bk}(0)|\coth(\hbar\omega_0/2\kB T)$, and the lifetime falls as $1/\coth$. This needs a state at least one phonon energy from a band extremum, so that both channels are open. With $|\mathrm{Im}\,\Sigma|=50$ meV, typical of states deep in a band, $\tau=\hbar/2|\mathrm{Im}\,\Sigma|=6.6$ fs; panel (b) of shows this model.

A more careful treatment evaluates the width at $E^{\mathrm{qp}}_{n\bk}$ and multiplies it by $Z^{\mathrm{qp}}_{n\bk}$, so that the lifetime becomes $\tau_{n\bk}/Z^{\mathrm{qp}}_{n\bk}$. Linearizing the propagator about the pole gives a peak of weight $Z^{\mathrm{qp}}_{n\bk}$ and half width $Z^{\mathrm{qp}}_{n\bk}|\mathrm{Im}\,\Sigma^{\mathrm{ep}}_{n\bk}|$ (Section A.7). For the photoluminescence linewidths of lead-iodide perovskites the bare golden rule overestimates the data, and this factor restores agreement.15

Adiabatic versus nonadiabatic: the Fröhlich catastrophe

The adiabatic formula was the standard tool when Poncé and coworkers (2015) noticed a problem: for the band extrema of polar crystals, the computed shift does not converge. It grows without limit as the grid of $\bq$ points becomes denser and the infinitesimal $\eta$ is sent to zero. The reason is power counting, the comparison of the powers of $q$ in the integrand and in the volume element near $\bq=0$:

  1. At the conduction-band minimum, placed at $\bk=0$, the term of within the band ($m=n$) has the denominator $E_{\mathrm c}(\mathbf 0)-E_{\mathrm c}(\bq)=-\hbar^2q^2/2m^*$, which vanishes as $q\to0$.

    The band is parabolic near its minimum.

  2. The polar coupling grows as $|g|^2\propto1/q^2$.

    An LO wave of long wavelength carries a macroscopic electric field (Section A.4).

  3. The grid average becomes an integral with the volume element $4\pi q^2\dd q$. Altogether, the adiabatic shift $\Delta E^{\mathrm A}_{\mathrm c}$ of the conduction-band minimum behaves as17

    $$\begin{aligned}\Delta E^{\mathrm A}_{\mathrm c}&\propto-\int_{q_{\mathrm{min}}}\frac{4\pi q^2\,\dd q}{q^2\;q^2}\\ &=-4\pi\int_{q_{\mathrm{min}}}\frac{\dd q}{q^2}\\ &\propto-\frac{1}{q_{\mathrm{min}}}\to-\infty .\end{aligned} \tag{A8.16}$$
    Four powers of $q$ in the denominator, two from the coupling and two from the energy denominator, beat the two powers of the volume element. Here $q_{\mathrm{min}}$ is the smallest wavevector that the calculation resolves; the shift diverges as it goes to zero.

Keeping the phonon energy cures the divergence. In the emission denominator at the band bottom is $-(\hbar^2q^2/2m^*+\hbar\omega_{\mathrm{LO}})$, which tends to $-\hbar\omega_{\mathrm{LO}}$ instead of zero as $q\to0$. Below the polaron wavevector $q_{\mathrm{pol}}$ the energy cost of a virtual phonon is set by $\hbar\omega_{\mathrm{LO}}$, not by the vanishing kinetic energy, and the integrand $q^2\cdot q^{-2}/\hbar\omega_{\mathrm{LO}}$ stays finite. A uniform grid of $N_1$ points along each direction resolves wavevectors down to about $q_{\mathrm{min}}=q_{\mathrm D}/N_1$, where $q_{\mathrm D}$, the radius of a sphere with the volume of the Brillouin zone, measures the size of the zone. follows both prescriptions as the grid is refined.

With a spherical zone of radius $q_{\mathrm D}$ and the integrals of the worked example, the shifts of the conduction-band minimum at $T=0$ are

The angular integral is then $2/(q^2+q_{\mathrm{pol}}^2)$, and the factors collected in the last step of that derivation turn the shift into $-(2/\pi)\,\alpha_{\mathrm F}\hbar\omega_{\mathrm{LO}}\int\dd q\,q_{\mathrm{pol}}/(q^2+q_{\mathrm{pol}}^2)$. The nonadiabatic shift converges to the polaron shift $-\alpha_{\mathrm F}\hbar\omega_{\mathrm{LO}}$ of the worked example once $q_{\mathrm{min}}\ll q_{\mathrm{pol}}\ll q_{\mathrm D}$, but slowly: the missing part falls only as $q_{\mathrm{min}}/q_{\mathrm{pol}}$, that is as $1/N_1$. Physical picture: a slow electron in a fast lattice

Dropping $\hbar\omega$ treats the zero-point displacements as a frozen random distortion to which the electron adjusts instantly. That is fine at short wavelengths, where a scattering event changes the electron's energy by much more than $\hbar\omega_{\mathrm{LO}}$. For $q\ll q_{\mathrm{pol}}$ the change, $\hbar^2q^2/2m^*$, is smaller than $\hbar\omega_{\mathrm{LO}}$: during one ionic oscillation the electron barely moves, so it is the slow partner, and it cannot exploit these long-wavelength fluctuations as if they were static. Instead the ions follow the electron and build a cloud of polarization of radius about $1/q_{\mathrm{pol}}$ (40 Å in GaAs) that travels with it: a large polaron, so called because it extends over many unit cells. The frozen-lattice formula keeps collecting energy from ever longer wavelengths of the dipole field, where its denominators vanish, and the sum diverges.

The review lists four remedies: principal-value integration with the phonon frequencies kept (Fan, 1951); analytic integration of the region near $\bq=0$ (Nery and Allen, 2016); the Brillouin–Wigner form, with the infinitesimal $\eta$ replaced by the physical linewidth; and full spectral functions (Kawai and coworkers, 2014; Antonius and coworkers, 2015). All of them keep $\hbar\omega_{\bq\nu}$, or a physical width, where the adiabatic formula has a vanishing denominator.18

The Fröhlich part of a ZPR is not small. Botti and Marques (2013) computed the screened interaction including the screening by the lattice, with a model dielectric function built from the Lyddane–Sachs–Teller relation, and found ZPRs above 1 eV for LiF, LiCl, NaCl and MgO; the review notes that this amounts to a Fan–Migdal calculation with only the long-range polar coupling. Nery and Allen (2016) found a Fröhlich contribution of 45 meV to the 150 meV ZPR of the gap of GaN. For the conduction-band minimum of GaN, the one-band model of this section gives $-\alpha_{\mathrm F}\hbar\omega_{\mathrm{LO}}\approx-41$ meV, of the same size.19

The finite speed of light plays no part in the cure. Part A treats the Coulomb interaction as instantaneous, and Part B replaces it by the full electromagnetic field, whose transverse part matters only within the light cone of Section A.4, at wavevectors $q\lesssim q_{\mathrm c}=\sqrt{\varepsilon_\infty}\,\omega_{\mathrm{LO}}/c$, where light in the crystal has the frequency of the LO phonon. (Section A.4 calls this wavevector $q_{\mathrm c}$; here the label c marks the conduction band.) In GaAs, $q_{\mathrm c}=6.1\times10^{-5}$ Å⁻¹, about 400 times smaller than $q_{\mathrm{pol}}$ (400 to 1200 times in the three materials of ). The light cone holds about $10^{-3}$ of the converged nonadiabatic shift, and the adiabatic integral cut off at $q_{\mathrm c}$ instead of $q_{\mathrm{min}}$ would still give $-0.69$ eV in GaAs, 270 times the converged value. The divergence is cured by the phonon energy, at $q\approx q_{\mathrm{pol}}$, far outside the light cone. Moreover, the LO coupling that drives it is longitudinal, and the longitudinal, Coulomb part of the field does not depend on $c$ (Section X.1). The word "retardation" adds to the confusion: in the review it means the frequency dependence of the electronic screening, in the EPP manuscript the finite speed of light.20

Beyond perturbation theory: frozen phonons, special displacements and molecular dynamics

Another route avoids matrix elements and perturbation theory altogether. Freeze the atoms in a distorted configuration in a supercell, compute the band energies with density-functional theory, and average over configurations drawn from the vibrational state. To see what this computes, expand an energy level to second order in the displacements, as in the derivation box of the Debye–Waller subsection, and average over the harmonic phonon state with occupations $n_{\bq\nu}$. The linear terms average to zero, and the quadratic ones give21

$$\begin{aligned}&\langle E_{n\bk}\rangle_T=E_{n\bk}+\frac12\sum_{\ell\kappa\alpha}\sum_{\ell'\kappa'\beta}\\ &\quad\times\langle u_{\ell\kappa\alpha}u_{\ell'\kappa'\beta}\rangle\,\frac{\partial^2E_{n\bk}}{\partial u_{\ell\kappa\alpha}\,\partial u_{\ell'\kappa'\beta}}\\ &\quad=E_{n\bk}+\frac1{N_\bq}\sum_{\bq\nu}\Big(n_{\bq\nu}+\frac12\Big)\\ &\qquad\times\Delta_{\bq\nu}\Delta_{-\bq\nu}E_{n\bk} .\end{aligned} \tag{A8.18}$$
$\langle E_{n\bk}\rangle_T$
band energy averaged over the vibrations at temperature $T$, adiabatically: each configuration is frozen while the electrons are computed (eV)
$\Delta_{\bq\nu}\Delta_{-\bq\nu}E_{n\bk}$
second derivative of the energy level with respect to the displacements of the mode pair $\pm\bq\nu$, each scaled by its zero-point amplitude: $\Delta_{\bq\nu}$ of Section A.2 applied to $E_{n\bk}$ instead of a potential (meV)

The second line follows from the first with the correlation . Evaluating the second derivatives by perturbation theory gives back the adiabatic Allen–Heine formula , with the full Debye–Waller term, before any rigid-ion approximation; this was the starting point of Allen and Cardona (1981). Evaluating them instead by finite differences, from band energies in supercells displaced along the phonon eigenvectors, is the frozen-phonon method, the supercell technique that Section A.2 described for the coupling. It needs no rigid-ion approximation and can include anharmonic effects, at the price of large supercells and one calculation per mode. Several ideas reduce the cost:

  • Monte Carlo sampling of displaced configurations (Patrick and Giustino, 2013), refined by constrained sampling (Monserrat, 2016);
  • a single special configuration (Zacharias and Giustino, 2016). Every mode of the supercell is displaced by its thermal amplitude, $[\hbar(2n_{\mathrm B}(\omega)+1)/2M_\kappa\omega]^{1/2}$ for a mode of frequency $\omega$, times its eigenvector, with signs that alternate from one mode to the next in order of frequency. The cross terms between different modes in the second-order expansion then nearly cancel, and the result becomes exact as the supercell grows. The approach is now called the special displacement method;
  • nondiagonal supercells (Lloyd-Williams and Monserrat, 2015), which reach a grid of $N_\ell$ wavevectors with supercells of only about $N_\ell^{1/3}$ cells;
  • Brooks' theorem, $\partial E_{n\bk}/\partial n_{\bq\nu}=\hbar\,\partial\omega_{\bq\nu}/\partial f_{n\bk}$: the shift of an electron level per added phonon equals the shift of a phonon energy per added electron, so the gap renormalization can be computed from the change of phonon frequencies when electrons are promoted across the gap. King-Smith and coworkers (1989) used it for the first first-principles temperature dependence of a gap.

Because these methods treat the motion of the nuclei explicitly, they reach effects beyond Allen–Heine: anharmonic vibrations, with large zero-point effects found in LiH, LiD, high-pressure helium, molecular crystals such as solid methane and ammonia, and ice. They are also close to molecular dynamics. Path-integral molecular dynamics samples the quantum motion of the nuclei, and classical molecular dynamics suffices at high temperature, where $n_{\bq\nu}\simeq\kB T/\hbar\omega_{\bq\nu}$. The same averaging over configurations gives temperature-dependent optical spectra (the Williams–Lax approach, Section A.9).22

All these methods are adiabatic: each configuration is frozen while the electrons are computed. In polar crystals they therefore inherit the long-wavelength problem of , and one expects their Fröhlich part to grow with the size of the supercell instead of converging. This is our inference from the model above; the review does not discuss it.

What these formulas leave out

The perturbative formulas of this section are harmonic, use the single-phonon-line self-energy of Section A.7 with adiabatic phonons, and keep the lattice parameter fixed. A measured $E^{\mathrm{qp}}_{\mathrm g}(T)$ also contains the effect of thermal expansion, which these formulas do not. The trend of the width in the one-phonon model needs states at least one phonon energy away from a band extremum, and the model misses subtle low-temperature behaviour analysed by Allen and Nery (2017). The matrix elements of density-functional theory are usually too small, because its screening of the vertex is too strong (Section A.3). The ZPR scales as $\varepsilon_\infty^{-2}$, so a 20% error in the electronic dielectric constant becomes about 40% in the ZPR, while the phonon frequencies, which depend on the screening only through a term in $\varepsilon_\infty^{-1/2}$, change by about 10%. GW-corrected couplings raise the ZPR of diamond by 40%.23

Check yourself: three short questions

  1. A measured gap is 5.40 eV at low temperature. At high temperature it approaches a straight line of slope $-0.50$ meV/K that extrapolates to 5.55 eV at $T=0$. What are the ZPR and the effective phonon energy of the one-phonon model?

    Answer. The intercept is $E_{\mathrm g}$, so $|\Delta E^{\mathrm{ZPR}}_{\mathrm g}|=5.55-5.40=0.15$ eV. The slope is $-2\kB|\Delta E^{\mathrm{ZPR}}_{\mathrm g}|/\hbar\omega_0$, so $\hbar\omega_0=2\times0.0862\ \text{meV/K}\times150\ \text{meV}/0.50\ \text{meV/K}\approx52$ meV.

  2. Every atom of a crystal is replaced by an isotope twice as heavy. How do the ZPR and the high-temperature slope of the gap change in the model of ?

    Answer. The force constants do not change, so $\omega_0\propto M_\kappa^{-1/2}$ and the squared zero-point amplitude $\hbar/2M_\kappa\omega_0\propto M_\kappa^{-1/2}$. The ZPR is proportional to it and drops by $\sqrt2$, to 71%. The slope $2\kB|\Delta E^{\mathrm{ZPR}}_{\mathrm g}|/\hbar\omega_0$ is unchanged: at high temperature the motion is classical and does not depend on the mass.

  3. In GaAs at 4 K, compare the LO-phonon scattering of an electron 20 meV above the conduction-band minimum with that of one 70 meV above it.

    Answer. 20 meV is below $\hbar\omega_{\mathrm{LO}}=36.2$ meV, so only absorption is possible, with $n_{\mathrm B}(\omega_{\mathrm{LO}})\approx\ee^{-36.2/0.345}\approx10^{-46}$: no LO scattering at all, and other mechanisms set the lifetime. At 70 meV $=1.93\,\hbar\omega_{\mathrm{LO}}$, spontaneous emission is open, and gives $1/\tau=7.85\ \text{ps}^{-1}\times(1/1.93)^{1/2}\operatorname{arsinh}(0.93)^{1/2}\approx4.8$ ps⁻¹, a lifetime of about 210 fs.

$E^{\mathrm{qp}}_{n\bk}$; $z$, $y$

The imaginary part of the pole is $y=\mathrm{Im}\,\Sigma^{\mathrm{ep}}_{n\bk}(E^{\mathrm{qp}}_{n\bk}/\hbar)\le0$. Giustino: $E_{n\bk}$, with the complex pole $\tilde E_{n\bk}=E_{n\bk}+i\Gamma_{n\bk}$ (his $\varepsilon_{n\bk}$ is our $E_{n\bk}$).
$m^*$, $m^*_{\mathrm c}$, $m^*_{\mathrm v}$
effective mass of a parabolic band, as in Section A.4, $E_{\bk}=\hbar^2k^2/2m^*$; masses of the electron at the conduction-band minimum and of the hole at the valence-band maximum (units of $m_{\mathrm e}$). Giustino: $m^*$, $m^*_n$.
$E_{\bk}$, $k$
energy of a single parabolic band measured from its minimum, $\hbar^2k^2/2m^*$, with $k=|\bk|$ (meV), as in Sections A.6 and A.7.
$q_{\mathrm{pol}}$
polaron wavevector $(2m^*\omega_{\mathrm{LO}}/\hbar)^{1/2}$, at which $\hbar^2q^2/2m^*=\hbar\omega_{\mathrm{LO}}$; $1/q_{\mathrm{pol}}$ is the polaron length $r_{\mathrm p}$ of Section A.4, the size of the polarization cloud (GaAs: 0.025 Å⁻¹, 40 Å). Not named in the sources.
$\Sigma_{\bk}(\omega)$, $\Sigma_{\mathbf 0}(\omega)$; $q_\omega$, $\theta$
self-energy of the state $\bk$ of the single parabolic band of the polar model, and its value at the band minimum, (meV); in its derivation, the wavevector $q_\omega=q_{\mathrm{pol}}(1-\omega/\omega_{\mathrm{LO}})^{1/2}$ and the angle $\theta$ between $\bk$ and $\bq$.
$\Delta E_{n\bk}$
shift of the band energy by the electron–phonon interaction, $E^{\mathrm{qp}}_{n\bk}-E_{n\bk}$, on shell or adiabatic (meV); for one electron in an empty band at $T=0$, without the Debye–Waller term, the second-order shift of Section A.7. Giustino: $E_{n\bk}-\varepsilon_{n\bk}$.
$\Delta E^{\mathrm{ZPR}}_{n\bk}$, $\Delta E^{\mathrm{ZPR}}_{\mathrm g}$; $\Delta E^{\mathrm{ZPR}}_{\mathrm c}$, $\Delta E^{\mathrm{ZPR}}_{\mathrm v}$
zero-point renormalization of a band energy and of the gap, ; of the conduction-band minimum (label c) and the valence-band maximum (label v) (meV). Giustino: $\Delta E^{\rm ZP}_{n\bk}$, $\Delta E_g^{\rm ZP}$.
$E_{\mathrm g}$; $E_{\mathrm c}$, $E_{\mathrm v}$

$\langle u_{\ell\kappa\alpha}u_{\ell'\kappa'\beta}\rangle$, $\langle u_{\kappa\alpha}u_{\kappa\beta}\rangle$
thermal correlation of two displacements, ; its on-site form, the displacement tensor of atom $\kappa$ of Section A.3, the same in every cell, , whose diagonal elements are the mean-square displacements of Section A.1 (Å²).
$I$, $J$; $A_I$, $B_{IJ}$, $X_{IJ}$
compound labels $I=\ell\kappa\alpha$ and $J=\ell'\kappa'\beta$ of two displacement coordinates; first and second derivatives of a band energy with respect to them, and the part of the second derivative that comes from second-order perturbation theory in the linear coupling (derivation box of the Debye–Waller subsection).
$\omega_0$

$E^{\mathrm{qp}}_{\mathrm g}(T)$; $E_{\mathrm c}(\bk)$, $E_{\mathrm v}(\bk)$, $\bk_{\mathrm c}$, $\bk_{\mathrm v}$
