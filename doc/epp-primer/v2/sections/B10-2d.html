B.10Sheet Coulomb law · 2D screening · LO dispersion · excitons · light cone

Two-dimensional crystals: no LO–TO splitting

A polar monolayer holds all its charges in one plane, and the field lines of those charges spread into the space around it. This section works out what that geometry does to the long-range coupling of Part B. It derives the Coulomb interaction and the electronic screening of a sheet, and shows why the longitudinal optical branch then starts at the transverse frequency and rises linearly instead of being split from it. The same screened interaction sets the binding of excitons and couples the phonons of a doped sheet to its plasmon; a finite speed of light matters only in a narrow window of wavevectors, where the sheet radiates.

What you need: Section A.1 for normal modes, their eigenvectors and the reduced mass; Section A.3 for the independent-particle density response; Section A.4 for Born charges, the polarization of a lattice wave and the field that splits LO from TO in a bulk crystal; Section A.5 for the screened interaction $W$$\,=\varepsilon^{-1}v$ and the random-phase approximation (RPA); and, for the general formula, Section B.5, Section B.6, Section B.7 and Section B.9: the ionic current vertex, the charge and current correlators, the phonon self-energy built from photon exchange, and its electrostatic limit. From electrostatics you need Poisson's equation and the boundary conditions at the surface of a dielectric. Two background boxes recall Fourier transforms in a plane and the hydrogen-like model of excitons.1

The picture: where the depolarizing field goes

In a longitudinal optical (LO) wave of a polar crystal the two sublattices move against each other along the wavevector. Each cell then carries an electric dipole, the Born-charge dipole of Section A.4, and the dipoles form a wave of polarization that points along the direction of propagation and changes sign every half wavelength. Where neighbouring dipoles point towards each other their charges do not cancel, and a bound charge $-\nabla\cdot\bP$ piles up, with $\bP$ the polarization (dipole moment per volume); where they point away from each other the opposite charge appears. The field of this bound charge pulls the ions back, on top of the springs between them, and stiffens the LO mode. In a transverse optical (TO) wave the ions move perpendicular to the wavevector; neighbouring dipoles never point towards each other, no charge appears, and the frequency is set by the springs alone.

Bulk and sheet differ in where the field lines of this bound charge go. In a bulk crystal the bound charge forms infinite planes perpendicular to the wavevector, alternately positive and negative. The field of an infinite charged plane does not weaken with distance, so the field between such planes does not depend on their spacing, as in a parallel-plate capacitor: here it is $4\pi$ times the polarization, $\bE=-4\pi\bP$. It is therefore the same at every wavelength: the LO mode is stiffened by the same amount at every wavevector, even as the wavevector goes to zero, and the LO frequency stays above the TO frequency at the zone centre $\Gamma$. This is the LO–TO splitting of Section A.4 ().

In a single sheet, a monolayer of hexagonal boron nitride (hBN) for example, the bound charge forms lines inside the plane. Its field lines leave the plane, arc through the empty space above and below to a height of about a wavelength divided by $2\pi$, and come back to the opposite charge half a wavelength away. The longer the wavelength, the higher the arcs: the same flux spreads over a larger height, and fewer lines pass close to the sheet, where they push the ions. The restoring field in the plane therefore weakens in proportion to the wavevector, and at the zone centre only the springs remain: the LO and TO frequencies meet at $\Gamma$. compares the two geometries.

The section follows this picture in steps:

  1. derive the Coulomb interaction of charges confined to a plane;
  2. compute the field of a sheet of dipoles and the LO frequency of the simplest polar sheet;
  3. add the screening by the electrons of the sheet, which brings in one length, the screening length $r_0$;
  4. rebuild the result from the photon exchange of Part B, which gives the general formula for any polar sheet;
  5. follow the same screened interaction to excitons, to doped sheets and to a finite speed of light.

Coulomb's law in a sheet

Split every position into its part along the sheet and its height above it, $\br=(\br_\parallel,z)$, with the sheet in the plane $z=0$. The sheet is unchanged by translations along its plane but not along $z$. Functions are therefore expanded in plane waves $\ee^{\ii\bQ_\parallel\cdot\br_\parallel}$ along the plane, while the height is kept as a coordinate: a partial Fourier transform, recalled in the box below. A quantity written as a function of $\bQ_\parallel$ and of the heights is said to be in the mixed representation. The in-plane wavevector $\bQ_\parallel$, of length $Q_\parallel$ and direction $\hat\bQ_\parallel=\bQ_\parallel/Q_\parallel$, is written as a capital with the mark $\parallel$; a wavevector component normal to the sheet, where one appears, is lowercase, $q_z$. In a crystal $\bQ_\parallel$ lies in the two-dimensional Brillouin zone, and $\Gamma$ is $\bQ_\parallel=0$. The long waves of this section have $Q_\parallel$ far smaller than the size of the zone, so reciprocal lattice vectors play no role.

Fourier transforms in a plane skip if familiar

A function of position is written as a superposition of in-plane plane waves, one for each $\bQ_\parallel$, with a coefficient that still depends on the height:

$$\begin{gathered}f(\br_\parallel,z)=\int\frac{\dd^2Q_\parallel}{(2\pi)^2}\,\ee^{\ii\bQ_\parallel\cdot\br_\parallel}\,f(\bQ_\parallel;z),\\ f(\bQ_\parallel;z)=\int\dd^2r_\parallel\,\ee^{-\ii\bQ_\parallel\cdot\br_\parallel}\,f(\br_\parallel,z).\end{gathered}$$

A kernel $K(\br,\br')$ that depends on the two in-plane positions only through $\br_\parallel-\br'_\parallel$ becomes $K(\bQ_\parallel;z,z')$, with both heights kept. A product of two kernels integrated over an intermediate point, $\int\dd^3r_1\,K(\br,\br_1)\,L(\br_1,\br')$, becomes $\int\dd z_1\,K(\bQ_\parallel;z,z_1)\,L(\bQ_\parallel;z_1,z')$: the in-plane convolution turns into a product, as in three dimensions, while the integral over the intermediate height stays.

For a function of the in-plane distance $r=|\br_\parallel|$ alone, write $\dd^2Q_\parallel=Q_\parallel\,\dd Q_\parallel\,\dd\theta$, with $\theta$ the angle between $\bQ_\parallel$ and $\br_\parallel$. The angular integral is a Bessel function of the first kind, $\int_0^{2\pi}\ee^{\ii Q_\parallel r\cos\theta}\,\dd\theta=2\pi J_0(Q_\parallel r)$, so

$$f(r)=\int_0^\infty\frac{Q_\parallel\,\dd Q_\parallel}{2\pi}\,J_0(Q_\parallel r)\,f(Q_\parallel) .$$

$J_0(x)$ equals 1 at $x=0$, oscillates with a period close to $2\pi$, decays as $\sqrt{2/\pi x}$, and integrates to $\int_0^\infty J_0(x)\,\dd x=1$. In a crystal the integral is a sum over the grid of allowed wavevectors, $\frac1{N_\ell}\sum_{\bQ_\parallel}\to\frac{\OS}{(2\pi)^2}\int\dd^2Q_\parallel$, where $\OS$ is the area of the unit cell and $N_\ell$ the number of cells (Section A.1).

The potential of a charged sheet

The simplest source is a wave of charge confined to the sheet, with charge per unit area $\rho^{\mathrm{2D}}(\br_\parallel)=\rho_0\,\ee^{\ii\bQ_\parallel\cdot\br_\parallel}$ and amplitude $\rho_0$. Its electrostatic potential follows from Poisson's equation. The potential is the scalar potential $A_0$ of Section B.2: for static charges $\bE=-\nabla A_0$, and a point charge has the energy $A_0$ times its charge. (Part B works in the temporal gauge $A_0=0$, where the same longitudinal field is carried by $\bA$; for static charges in a plane the scalar potential is the shorter route.)

  1. The charge density in space is $\rho(\br)=\rho_0\,\ee^{\ii\bQ_\parallel\cdot\br_\parallel}\,\delta(z)$, and Poisson's equation reads $\nabla^2A_0=-4\pi\rho$.

    The delta function puts all the charge in a plane of zero thickness: integrating $\rho$ over $z$ returns the charge per area. Poisson's equation is Gauss's law, $\nabla\cdot\bE=4\pi\rho$, with $\bE=-\nabla A_0$.

  2. Try $A_0=f(z)\,\ee^{\ii\bQ_\parallel\cdot\br_\parallel}$. Then $\nabla^2A_0=\big(f''-Q_\parallel^2f\big)\,\ee^{\ii\bQ_\parallel\cdot\br_\parallel}$, and

    $$f''(z)-Q_\parallel^2f(z)=-4\pi\rho_0\,\delta(z) .$$
    The problem is unchanged by translations along the plane, so the potential carries the same in-plane wave as its source. Each in-plane derivative brings down $\ii Q_x$ or $\ii Q_y$, and the two second derivatives together give $-Q_\parallel^2$.

  3. Away from the sheet the right side vanishes and $f''=Q_\parallel^2f$. The solution that stays finite on both sides and is continuous at $z=0$ is $f(z)=f_0\,\ee^{-Q_\parallel|z|}$.

    The other solution, $\ee^{+Q_\parallel|z|}$, grows without bound far from the sheet. A jump of the potential would need an infinite field inside the sheet, which a finite charge per area cannot produce.

  4. Integrate the equation of step 2 across the sheet, from just below ($z=0^-$) to just above ($z=0^+$): $f'(0^+)-f'(0^-)=-4\pi\rho_0$. With $f'(0^\pm)=\mp Q_\parallel f_0$ this gives $f_0=2\pi\rho_0/Q_\parallel$.

    The term $Q_\parallel^2f$ is finite, so its integral over a vanishing interval vanishes. The left side is minus the jump of the normal field $E_z=-\partial_zA_0$: Gauss's law for a flat box around a piece of the sheet says that the normal field jumps by $4\pi$ times the charge per area, half of the flux leaving upwards and half downwards.

The potential of the charge wave is

$$A_0(\br_\parallel,z)=\frac{2\pi\rho_0}{Q_\parallel}\,\ee^{-Q_\parallel|z|}\,\ee^{\ii\bQ_\parallel\cdot\br_\parallel} . \tag{B10.1}$$

It decays away from the sheet over the length $1/Q_\parallel$, the wavelength divided by $2\pi$. A point charge $e$ in the plane ($e\gt0$ is the elementary charge) is the sheet density $e\,\delta(\br_\parallel)$, a superposition of in-plane waves that all have the amplitude $e$ (box). By each wave produces the potential $(2\pi e/Q_\parallel)\,\ee^{-Q_\parallel|z|}$, and a second charge $e$ at height $z$ has $e$ times this potential as its energy. Moving both charges up or down together changes nothing in vacuum, so for two charges at heights $z$ and $z'$ only $z-z'$ enters, and the Coulomb energy $v$$\,=e^2/|\br-\br'|$ of Section A.3, transformed along the plane, is therefore

$$\begin{gathered}v(\bQ_\parallel;z,z')=\frac{2\pi e^2}{Q_\parallel}\,\ee^{-Q_\parallel|z-z'|},\\ v^{\mathrm{2D}}(Q_\parallel)=v(\bQ_\parallel;0,0)=\frac{2\pi e^2}{Q_\parallel} .\end{gathered} \tag{B10.2}$$
$v(\bQ_\parallel;z,z')$
Coulomb interaction energy of two charges $e$ at heights $z$ and $z'$, Fourier transformed along the plane (eV Å²)
$v^{\mathrm{2D}}(Q_\parallel)$
the same for two charges in the sheet: the Coulomb interaction of a sheet, per unit area in $\bQ_\parallel$ space (eV Å²)

The same kernel follows from the bulk one.2 A charge confined to the plane $z=0$ contains every normal wavevector $q_z$ with equal weight, since $\delta(z)=\int\frac{\dd q_z}{2\pi}\ee^{\ii q_zz}$. Each three-dimensional wave $(\bQ_\parallel,q_z)$ interacts through $v(\bq)=4\pi e^2/q^2$ with $q^2=Q_\parallel^2+q_z^2$, and summing over all of them gives

$$\begin{gathered}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\dd q_z}{2\pi}\,\frac{4\pi e^2\,\ee^{\ii q_z(z-z')}}{Q_\parallel^2+q_z^2}\\ =\frac{2\pi e^2}{Q_\parallel}\,\ee^{-Q_\parallel|z-z'|}\end{gathered} \tag{B10.3}$$

(for $z\gt z'$ close the contour in the upper half plane around the pole $q_z=\ii Q_\parallel$). The $1/Q_\parallel$ of the sheet is the $1/q^2$ of the bulk integrated over the normal direction. Coulomb's law itself has not changed: transforming back with the box gives $e^2\int_0^\infty J_0(Q_\parallel r)\,\dd Q_\parallel=e^2/r$ for two charges in the plane. Only its Fourier transform differs, and the difference has a simple meaning. Stack identical sheets at spacing $d$ into a bulk crystal and give each the charge wave $\rho_0$. The potentials of all sheets add up. For $Q_\parallel d\ll1$, $\sum_n\ee^{-Q_\parallel|n|d}\simeq2/(Q_\parallel d)$: about that many sheets lie within one decay length and contribute, and the total in-plane potential is $4\pi\rho_0/(Q_\parallel^2d)$. That is the bulk potential of the charge density $\rho_0/d$. An isolated sheet lacks all its neighbours, so its potential is smaller by the factor $Q_\parallel d/2$, which vanishes at long wavelength.

A sheet of dipoles and the LO frequency

A lattice wave of a polar sheet produces such a charge wave through the dipoles of its displaced ions. This subsection computes its field, and then the LO frequency of the simplest polar sheet, still without the screening by the sheet's own electrons.

The field of a polarization wave

Let a long lattice wave of in-plane wavevector $\bQ_\parallel$ displace atom $\kappa$ of cell $\ell$ by $\boldsymbol\xi_\kappa\,\ee^{\ii\bQ_\parallel\cdot\bR_\ell}$, where $\bR_\ell$ is the lattice vector of the cell and $\boldsymbol\xi_\kappa$ the complex amplitude of atom $\kappa$; the real part is the physical displacement (Section A.1). Atom $\kappa$ carries the Born effective charge tensor $\mathbf Z^*_\kappa$ (dimensionless), so it adds the dipole $e\mathbf Z^*_\kappa\cdot\boldsymbol\xi_\kappa$ times the phase of its cell. Averaged over a cell, the dipoles form the sheet polarization, the dipole moment per unit area:

$$\bP^{\mathrm{2D}}(\br_\parallel)=\frac{e}{\OS}\sum_\kappa\mathbf Z^*_\kappa\cdot\boldsymbol\xi_\kappa\,\ee^{\ii\bQ_\parallel\cdot\br_\parallel} . \tag{B10.4}$$
$\bP^{\mathrm{2D}}$
sheet polarization: dipole moment per unit area (charge per length; $e$/Å)
$\OS$
area of the unit cell of the sheet (Å²), as in Section A.1

This is the polarization of a bulk wave, , with the cell volume $\OV$ replaced by the cell area $\OS$. The smooth average over a cell holds when the wavelength is much longer than the cell, $Q_\parallel a\ll1$ with $a$ the lattice constant; the phase differences between the atoms of one cell are then negligible, and the general formula below restores them. Seen from three dimensions, the dipoles fill the plane $z=0$: $\bP(\br)=\bP^{\mathrm{2D}}(\br_\parallel)\,\delta(z)$. For in-plane dipoles the field follows in three steps.

  1. The bound charge is $\rho=-\nabla\cdot\bP=-\big(\nabla_\parallel\cdot\bP^{\mathrm{2D}}\big)\,\delta(z)$, a sheet charge of amplitude $\rho^{\mathrm{2D}}=-\ii\bQ_\parallel\cdot\bP^{\mathrm{2D}}$.

    Where neighbouring dipoles converge their charges do not cancel. The in-plane divergence of a wave brings down $\ii\bQ_\parallel$, so only the component of $\bP^{\mathrm{2D}}$ along $\bQ_\parallel$ creates charge. A component normal to the sheet would instead give $-P^{\mathrm{2D}}_z\,\delta'(z)$, a double layer, which is set aside until the caveat below.

  2. Its potential is with this amplitude: $A_0=\dfrac{2\pi}{Q_\parallel}\big(-\ii\bQ_\parallel\cdot\bP^{\mathrm{2D}}\big)\,\ee^{-Q_\parallel|z|}$.

    Poisson's equation is linear and does not care where a sheet charge comes from.

  3. The in-plane field is $\bE_\parallel=-\nabla_\parallel A_0=-\ii\bQ_\parallel A_0$.

    The in-plane gradient of a wave brings down $\ii\bQ_\parallel$. The two factors $-\ii$ multiply to $-1$, and $\bQ_\parallel(\bQ_\parallel\cdot\bP^{\mathrm{2D}})/Q_\parallel$ is $Q_\parallel\hat\bQ_\parallel(\hat\bQ_\parallel\cdot\bP^{\mathrm{2D}})$.
$$\begin{gathered}\bE_\parallel(\br_\parallel,z)=-2\pi Q_\parallel\,\hat\bQ_\parallel\big(\hat\bQ_\parallel\cdot\bP^{\mathrm{2D}}\big)\\ \times\ee^{-Q_\parallel|z|} .\end{gathered} \tag{B10.5}$$
$\bE_\parallel$
components of the electric field along the sheet, at height $z$ above the point $\br_\parallel$, with $\bP^{\mathrm{2D}}$ taken at $\br_\parallel$ (charge per area in Gaussian units, $e$/Å²; $e\bE_\parallel$ is a force in eV/Å)

Compare the bulk field of a polarization wave, $\bE=-4\pi\hat\bq(\hat\bq\cdot\bP)$, which is without electronic screening.3 Three statements follow.

  • TO waves have no field in either geometry, because $\hat\bQ_\parallel\cdot\bP^{\mathrm{2D}}=0$.
  • The bulk field does not depend on the length of $\bq$: it is the capacitor field of the picture.
  • The sheet field is proportional to $Q_\parallel$. For a layer of thickness $d$ cut from a bulk crystal, with the same dipoles, $\bP=\bP^{\mathrm{2D}}/d$ and the ratio of the two fields is $Q_\parallel d/2$, the factor met above. For hBN, with $d=3.33$ Å, a wavelength of 100 Å ($Q_\parallel=0.063$ Å⁻¹) gives 0.105.

The normal component, $E_z=-\partial_zA_0$, has equal and opposite values just above and just below the sheet. It averages to zero in the plane and pushes no in-plane dipole: it is the part of each field line that arcs out of the sheet in .

Two ions per cell: the simplest polar sheet

Take a sheet with two atoms per cell, like hBN, with masses $M_1$ and $M_2$ and in-plane Born charges $\mathbf Z^*_1=-\mathbf Z^*_2=Z^*$ times the unit tensor. (The two charges are opposite because of the Born-charge sum rule of Section A.4, and isotropic in the plane because of the hexagonal symmetry.) In a long optical wave the centre of mass of each cell is at rest, $M_1\boldsymbol\xi_1+M_2\boldsymbol\xi_2=0$. The short-range springs alone give the optical frequency $\omega_{\mathrm{TO}}$, the same for both in-plane directions of motion by symmetry.

  1. Newton's equations for the two atoms, with the spring force and the force $\pm eZ^*\bE_\parallel$ of the field, divided by the masses and subtracted, give for the relative amplitude

    $$\omega^2\,(\boldsymbol\xi_1-\boldsymbol\xi_2)=\omega_{\mathrm{TO}}^2\,(\boldsymbol\xi_1-\boldsymbol\xi_2)-\frac{eZ^*}{M_{\mathrm r}}\,\bE_\parallel .$$
    At long wavelength the springs pull the two atoms back with equal and opposite forces proportional to $\boldsymbol\xi_1-\boldsymbol\xi_2$. Dividing each equation by its mass and subtracting collects $1/M_1+1/M_2=1/M_{\mathrm r}$, the reduced mass $M_{\mathrm r}$ of Section A.1, in front of both forces. Without the field this is the optical branch at $\Gamma$, $\omega=\omega_{\mathrm{TO}}$.

  2. The sheet polarization is $\bP^{\mathrm{2D}}=(eZ^*/\OS)\,(\boldsymbol\xi_1-\boldsymbol\xi_2)$, times the wave.

    The two dipoles $eZ^*\boldsymbol\xi_1$ and $-eZ^*\boldsymbol\xi_2$ add to $eZ^*$ times the relative displacement.

  3. Insert the field at $z=0$. For a longitudinal wave, $\boldsymbol\xi_1-\boldsymbol\xi_2$ along $\hat\bQ_\parallel$, the field adds $2\pi Q_\parallel(eZ^*)^2/(\OS M_{\mathrm r})$ to $\omega_{\mathrm{TO}}^2$ on the right side; for a transverse wave it adds nothing.

    The field is antiparallel to the polarization along $\hat\bQ_\parallel$, so it acts as an extra spring on the LO mode only.

Without electronic screening, the LO branch of the sheet is therefore

$$\omega_{\mathrm{LO}}^2(Q_\parallel)=\omega_{\mathrm{TO}}^2+\frac{2\pi e^2Z^{*2}}{\OS M_{\mathrm r}}\,Q_\parallel . \tag{B10.6}$$

The LO frequency $\omega_{\mathrm{LO}}$ starts at $\omega_{\mathrm{TO}}$ and its square rises linearly. The bulk counterpart, without the electronic screening constant ($\varepsilon_\infty$$\,=1$), adds the constant $4\pi e^2Z^{*2}/(\OV M_{\mathrm r})$ at every wavevector; the ratio of the two shifts is again $Q_\parallel d/2$ with $\OV=\OS d$. For hBN ($\OS=5.43$ Å², $M_{\mathrm r}=6.10$ amu, $Z^*=2.7$, $\hbar\omega_{\mathrm{TO}}=170$ meV) the coefficient $\hbar^2\cdot2\pi e^2Z^{*2}/(\OS M_{\mathrm r})$ is $0.0832$ eV² Å, so at $Q_\parallel=0.1$ Å⁻¹ the LO energy rises from 170 to 193 meV. The electrons of the sheet reduce this, as the next subsection shows.

The sheet screens itself

The sheet's own electrons respond to the in-plane field of any charge in the plane. In an insulating sheet they cannot move far; each cell polarizes, and the new dipoles form bound charge that partly cancels the charge that caused it. The model keeps only dipoles in the plane, driven by the in-plane field. The screening they produce is weak for long waves: the induced dipoles then change slowly along the sheet, neighbours nearly cancel, and little bound charge is left. The simplest model gives this response one number.

  1. Let the electrons form a sheet polarization proportional to the in-plane field, $\bP^{\mathrm{2D}}_{\rme}=\chi^{\mathrm{2D}}\bE_\parallel$.

    This defines the sheet polarizability $\chi^{\mathrm{2D}}$, a dipole per area per unit field: a length in Gaussian units. It holds for a sheet with a band gap, and for fields that vary slowly over a cell and oscillate slowly, with $\hbar\omega$ well below the gap.

  2. An external charge wave in the sheet creates the potential $A_0^{\mathrm{ext}}$ in the plane. The total potential is $A_0=A_0^{\mathrm{ext}}+A_0^{\mathrm{ind}}$, where $A_0^{\mathrm{ind}}$ is the potential of the electrons' bound charge.

    The electrons respond to the total field, which includes their own contribution: the response must be made self-consistent.

  3. The induced charge is $\rho^{\mathrm{2D}}_{\mathrm{ind}}=-\chi^{\mathrm{2D}}Q_\parallel^2A_0$.

    Step 1 of the field derivation: the bound charge is $-\ii\bQ_\parallel\cdot\bP^{\mathrm{2D}}_{\rme}$, and the field is $\bE_\parallel=-\ii\bQ_\parallel A_0$, so $\bP^{\mathrm{2D}}_{\rme}=-\ii\chi^{\mathrm{2D}}\bQ_\parallel A_0$.

  4. By its potential in the plane is $A_0^{\mathrm{ind}}=(2\pi/Q_\parallel)\,\rho^{\mathrm{2D}}_{\mathrm{ind}}=-2\pi\chi^{\mathrm{2D}}Q_\parallel\,A_0$, so $A_0=A_0^{\mathrm{ext}}/\big(1+2\pi\chi^{\mathrm{2D}}Q_\parallel\big)$.

    The induced charge is a sheet charge like any other. The kernel $2\pi/Q_\parallel$ grows at long wavelength while the induced charge falls as $Q_\parallel^2$; their product, the screening, falls as $Q_\parallel$.

The ratio of external to total potential is the static dielectric function of the sheet:

$$\begin{gathered}\varepsilon^{\mathrm{2D}}(Q_\parallel)=1+r_0Q_\parallel,\\ r_0=2\pi\chi^{\mathrm{2D}} .\end{gathered} \tag{B10.7}$$
$\varepsilon^{\mathrm{2D}}(Q_\parallel)$
electronic dielectric function of the sheet (ions clamped), for an in-plane wave of wavevector $\bQ_\parallel$ (dimensionless)
$r_0$
screening length of the sheet (Å)
$\chi^{\mathrm{2D}}$
sheet polarizability: induced dipole per area per unit in-plane field (Å)

Unlike a bulk insulator, with its constant $\varepsilon_\infty$, a sheet does not screen long waves at all: $\varepsilon^{\mathrm{2D}}\to1$ as $Q_\parallel\to0$, because the field lines of a long wave run almost entirely outside the sheet. A layer of thickness $d$ cut from a bulk crystal of dielectric constant $\varepsilon_\infty$ has $\chi^{\mathrm{2D}}=d(\varepsilon_\infty-1)/4\pi$ (the susceptibility $(\varepsilon_\infty-1)/4\pi$ of the bulk times the thickness, since an in-plane field is the same inside and outside a thin layer), so $r_0\approx d(\varepsilon_\infty-1)/2$. This estimate gives 6.4 Å for hBN ($d=3.33$ Å, $\varepsilon_\infty=4.87$) and 43 Å for MoS₂ ($d=6.15$ Å, $\varepsilon_\infty=15$); calculations of the monolayers give about 10 Å and 40 Å.4

A substrate below the sheet and a capping layer above it, with dielectric constants $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{sub}}$ and $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{cap}}$, screen as well. Repeating the steps with the jump condition of a sheet between two dielectrics (box below) gives

$$\begin{gathered}\varepsilon^{\mathrm{2D}}(Q_\parallel)=\varepsilon_{\mathrm{env}}+r_0Q_\parallel,\\ \varepsilon_{\mathrm{env}}=\tfrac12\big(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{sub}}+\varepsilon_{\mathrm{cap}}\big) .\end{gathered} \tag{B10.8}$$

A freestanding sheet has $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{env}}=1$; a sheet on SiO₂ about 2.5, and a sheet between two hBN layers about 4.5. The environment must be taken at the frequency of the process: for a phonon, at the phonon frequency, which for a polar substrate such as SiO₂ differs from the static value. With and the screened interaction of the sheet, in the sense of Section A.5 ($W=\varepsilon^{-1}v$, here with the electrons of the sheet and the dielectric response of its environment, and with the ions of the sheet clamped), is

$$\begin{gathered}W^{\mathrm{e,2D}}(Q_\parallel)=\frac{v^{\mathrm{2D}}(Q_\parallel)}{\varepsilon^{\mathrm{2D}}(Q_\parallel)}\\ =\frac{2\pi e^2}{Q_\parallel\,\big(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{env}}+r_0Q_\parallel\big)} .\end{gathered} \tag{B10.9}$$

It has two regimes, which meet at $Q_\parallel=\varepsilon_{\mathrm{env}}/r_0$ (0.1 Å⁻¹ for freestanding hBN, 0.025 Å⁻¹ for MoS₂). For longer waves the environment screens alone, $W^{\mathrm{e,2D}}\simeq2\pi e^2/(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{env}}Q_\parallel)$. For shorter waves the sheet's electrons dominate, $W^{\mathrm{e,2D}}\simeq2\pi e^2/(r_0Q_\parallel^2)$, which has the $1/q^2$ form of a bulk interaction.

Derivation: a sheet between two dielectrics

  1. Above the sheet the potential of a charge wave $\rho_0$ is $f_0\,\ee^{-Q_\parallel z}$ in a medium of dielectric constant $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{cap}}$; below, $f_0\,\ee^{Q_\parallel z}$ in $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{sub}}$.

    Inside each uniform medium Poisson's equation has the same decaying solutions as before; continuity fixes one common amplitude.

  2. Across the sheet the normal displacement field $\varepsilon E_z$ jumps by $4\pi\rho_0$: $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{cap}}Q_\parallel f_0+\varepsilon_{\mathrm{sub}}Q_\parallel f_0=4\pi\rho_0$.

    In a dielectric, Gauss's law counts only free charges when written for $\varepsilon\bE$; the bound charges of the media are in $\varepsilon$.

  3. Hence $f_0=2\pi\rho_0/(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{env}}Q_\parallel)$ with $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{env}}=\frac12(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{sub}}+\varepsilon_{\mathrm{cap}})$: every sheet potential is divided by $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{env}}$.

    In vacuum the same jump condition reads $(1+1)\,Q_\parallel f_0=4\pi\rho_0$. The potential is continuous, so $E_z$ leaves the sheet with the same strength upwards and downwards, and each side contributes its own constant times that field: the sum $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{sub}}+\varepsilon_{\mathrm{cap}}$ replaces $1+1$, and the average replaces 1.

  4. The sheet's own response of the main text now gives $A_0^{\mathrm{ind}}=-(r_0Q_\parallel/\varepsilon_{\mathrm{env}})A_0$ and $A_0^{\mathrm{ext}}=2\pi\rho_0/(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{env}}Q_\parallel)$, so $A_0=2\pi\rho_0/\big[Q_\parallel(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{env}}+r_0Q_\parallel)\big]$, which is .

    Measured against the bare vacuum kernel $2\pi/Q_\parallel$, the total screening is $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{env}}+r_0Q_\parallel$.

The general formalism: the RPA chain of a sheet

Many-body theory gives the same result and says what $\chi^{\mathrm{2D}}$ is. The independent-particle response of the sheet, $\chi^0$ of Section A.3, , is built from its Bloch states $\psi_{n\mathbf K_\parallel}$, labelled by an in-plane electron wavevector $\mathbf K_\parallel$, with band energies $E_{n\mathbf K_\parallel}$ and occupations $f_{n\mathbf K_\parallel}$:

$$\begin{gathered}\chi^0(\bQ_\parallel,\omega)=\frac{2}{\OSSC}\sum_{\mathbf K_\parallel nm}\\ \times\frac{f_{n\mathbf K_\parallel}-f_{m\mathbf K_\parallel+\bQ_\parallel}}{\hbar\omega+E_{n\mathbf K_\parallel}-E_{m\mathbf K_\parallel+\bQ_\parallel}+\ii\eta}\\ \times\big|\langle\psi_{m\mathbf K_\parallel+\bQ_\parallel}|\ee^{\ii\bQ_\parallel\cdot\br}|\psi_{n\mathbf K_\parallel}\rangle\big|^2 .\end{gathered} \tag{B10.10}$$

Where the strictly two-dimensional model stops

The plateau is reached at $Q_\parallel\gtrsim\varepsilon_{\mathrm{env}}/r_0$, and $r_0$ is only a few times the thickness $d$ of the layer. So the plateau region approaches $Q_\parallel d\sim1$, where the finite thickness neglected in step 2 of the RPA chain starts to matter: there the strictly two-dimensional model is least reliable. The screening of the EPP manuscript is the RPA without local fields or exchange and correlation; a production calculation uses the macroscopic dielectric response of the layer, computed with density-functional perturbation theory and a Coulomb interaction truncated along $z$. A calculation of a monolayer repeats the layer along $z$ with some vacuum in between; truncating the Coulomb interaction beyond about half that period stops each layer from interacting with its copies, so that the result describes one isolated sheet.

The general formalism: photon exchange in a sheet

The two-ion model assumed one polar mode and a field from macroscopic electrostatics. Part B derives the long-range part of the phonon self-energy for any crystal from the exchange of photons: a phonon emits a photon through its ionic current, the photon travels through the polarizable electrons, and a phonon reabsorbs it (Section B.7). The EPP manuscript repeats this construction for a sheet. It carries over step by step, with one change of geometry.

  1. The ions radiate from the plane only. Repeat the derivation of the ionic photon self-energy $\Pi^{\ion}$, , with every dipole in the plane $z=0$. The photon line of a sheet, and the modes left out

For the ionic self-energy, going from bulk to sheet does mean replacing $\OV$ by $\OS$. For the photon line it does not: a sheet exchanges photons with itself at $z=z'=0$, so it sees the bulk propagator summed over all $q_z$, as the Coulomb kernel showed. The kernel $2\pi e^2/Q_\parallel$ comes out of that integral, not out of a change of normalization.

Everything above concerns in-plane polar modes, such as the in-plane LO–TO pair of hBN or the in-plane polar mode of MoS₂ (labelled E′ by its symmetry). In a sheet with mirror symmetry through its plane, the in-plane and out-of-plane modes do not mix. An out-of-plane polar mode (ZO type, with a Born charge $Z^*_{zz}$) couples through the $zz$ component, where the derivatives of act on $\ee^{-Q_\parallel|z-z'|}$ and produce a contact term:

$$\partial_z\partial_{z'}\ee^{-Q_\parallel|z-z'|}=2Q_\parallel\,\delta(z-z')-Q_\parallel^2\,\ee^{-Q_\parallel|z-z'|} .$$

The delta-function term is the depolarizing field inside a sheet of normal dipoles. It gives such modes a shift that does not vanish at $\Gamma$, and its size depends on the thickness of the layer. The EPP manuscript discards it and keeps only $\alpha,\beta=x,y$.

The coupling to electrons

The same kernel changes the long-range electron–phonon coupling of Section A.4. An electron in the sheet feels the potential energy $-eA_0$ of the polarization wave. Its amplitude follows from the polarization , its bound charge $-\ii\bQ_\parallel\cdot\bP^{\mathrm{2D}}$, and the kernel $2\pi/Q_\parallel$ of divided by the screening $\varepsilon^{\mathrm{2D}}(Q_\parallel)$:

$$-eA_0=\ii\,\frac{2\pi e^2}{\OS}\sum_\kappa\frac{\bQ_\parallel\cdot\mathbf Z^*_\kappa\cdot\boldsymbol\xi_\kappa}{Q_\parallel\,\varepsilon^{\mathrm{2D}}(Q_\parallel)} ,$$

the sheet version of the Verdi–Giustino vertex , with $4\pi e^2/\OV$ and $\bq\cdot\boldsymbol\varepsilon_\infty\cdot\bq$ replaced by $2\pi e^2/\OS$ and $Q_\parallel\varepsilon^{\mathrm{2D}}$. In bulk the coupling diverges as $1/q$, Fröhlich's divergence. In the sheet it tends to a finite value that depends on the direction of $\hat\bQ_\parallel$, proportional to $\hat\bQ_\parallel\cdot\mathbf Z^*_\kappa\cdot\boldsymbol\xi_\kappa/\varepsilon_{\mathrm{env}}$, and it falls as $1/(r_0Q_\parallel)$ beyond $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{env}}/r_0$. This two-dimensional Fröhlich coupling sets the polar scattering of carriers in monolayers.11

The same $r_0$ binds excitons

An electron excited across the gap of a semiconducting sheet and the hole it leaves behind attract each other through the screened interaction of the sheet. At short distances the field lines between them run mostly inside the sheet, where the electrons screen strongly. At large distances they run mostly through the environment, where only $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{env}}$ screens. The crossover lies near $r_0/\varepsilon_{\mathrm{env}}$, and it is the same $r_0$ that sets the LO dispersion. shows the interaction in real space and the exciton it binds.

Wannier excitons in two dimensions skip if familiar

When an electron and a hole are bound over many cells, their relative motion obeys a hydrogen-like equation with the reduced mass $m_{\mathrm X}$ of the pair, $1/m_{\mathrm X}$ being the sum of the inverse effective masses of electron and hole. With an attraction $-e^2/\varepsilon r$ in three dimensions the ground state is bound by one effective Rydberg, $m_{\mathrm X}e^4/(2\varepsilon^2\hbar^2)$. In two dimensions the same attraction gives the series $E_n=-m_{\mathrm X}e^4/[2\varepsilon^2\hbar^2(n-\frac12)^2]$, $n=1,2,\dots$: the ground state is bound four times more strongly and its radius is half as large. In a real sheet the attraction is not $e^2/\varepsilon r$ at short distance. The levels then depart from this series, and the departure is one way to extract the screening length from the exciton spectra of transition-metal dichalcogenides.

Physical picture: one interaction, two probes

An exciton of radius $a_{\mathrm X}$ samples $W^{\mathrm{e,2D}}(Q_\parallel)$ mostly around $Q_\parallel\sim1/a_{\mathrm X}$; an LO phonon of wavevector $Q_\parallel$ samples the same function at that $Q_\parallel$. If a calculation of excitons already gives $r_0$, the LO dispersion needs only the Born charges and the mode eigenvectors on top, and conversely. The exciton itself solves the Wannier equation of the box with the Keldysh attraction:

$$\begin{gathered}\Big[-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m_{\mathrm X}}\nabla_\parallel^2-W^{\mathrm{e,2D}}(r)\Big]\psi_{\mathrm X}\\ =-E_{\mathrm b}\,\psi_{\mathrm X} .\end{gathered} \tag{B10.24}$$

For MoS₂ ($r_0=40$ Å, $m_{\mathrm X}=0.25\,m_{\mathrm e}$) the ground state is bound by 0.57 eV in a freestanding sheet, 0.32 eV on SiO₂ ($\varepsilon_{\mathrm{env}}=2.45$) and 0.19 eV between hBN layers ($\varepsilon_{\mathrm{env}}=4.5$), with a radius $a_{\mathrm X}$ that grows from 10 to 13 Å. The same pair with the unscreened attraction $e^2/r$ would be bound by 13.6 eV: the sheet's own screening reduces the binding by a factor of about 24. For hBN ($r_0=10$ Å, $m_{\mathrm X}\approx0.5\,m_{\mathrm e}$) the binding is about 1.9 eV and the radius only 4 Å, so the Wannier model is only qualitative there.14

Derivation: a variational estimate of the binding energy

  1. Take the trial function $\psi_{\mathrm X}=\sqrt{2/\pi}\,a_{\mathrm X}^{-1}\ee^{-r/a_{\mathrm X}}$, normalized in the plane, with the radius $a_{\mathrm X}$ as the variational parameter. Its kinetic energy is $\hbar^2/(2m_{\mathrm X}a_{\mathrm X}^2)$.

    This is the exact ground state of the two-dimensional hydrogen problem, so the estimate is exact for $r_0=0$.

  2. The potential energy is $-\langle W\rangle=-\int\dd^2r\,|\psi_{\mathrm X}|^2W^{\mathrm{e,2D}}(r)$. In $\bQ_\parallel$ space the density $|\psi_{\mathrm X}|^2$ transforms to $(1+Q_\parallel^2a_{\mathrm X}^2/4)^{-3/2}$, and the substitution $Q_\parallel=(2/a_{\mathrm X})\tan\theta$ gives

    $$\langle W\rangle=\frac{2e^2}{a_{\mathrm X}}\int_0^{\pi/2}\frac{\cos\theta\,\dd\theta}{\varepsilon_{\mathrm{env}}+(2r_0/a_{\mathrm X})\tan\theta} .$$
    The integral of a product over the plane is the integral of the product of the transforms, $\int\frac{\dd^2Q}{(2\pi)^2}W(Q)\,\rho(-Q)$, with $\rho$ the transform of the density. For $r_0=0$ the integral gives $2e^2/(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{env}}a_{\mathrm X})$, the expectation value of $e^2/\varepsilon_{\mathrm{env}}r$.

  3. Minimize $E=\hbar^2/(2m_{\mathrm X}a_{\mathrm X}^2)-\langle W\rangle$ over $a_{\mathrm X}$; minus the minimum is $E_{\mathrm b}$, and the radius at the minimum is the exciton radius.

    The variational energy lies above the true ground-state energy, so this $E_{\mathrm b}$ is a lower bound. For the cases above it lies 2–3% below the exact value: 0.558 against 0.570 eV for freestanding MoS₂. For $r_0=0$ it returns $2m_{\mathrm X}e^4/(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{env}}^2\hbar^2)$, four effective Rydbergs. uses this estimate.

Doped sheets: plasmon–phonon modes

Setting $\omega=0$ inside $W$ fails when free carriers put a plasmon, the collective oscillation of their density, near the phonon frequency, as in a doped bulk semiconductor (Section B.9). In a sheet the plasmon starts at zero frequency and rises as $\sqrt{Q_\parallel}$, so above a modest carrier density it crosses the LO branch at some wavevector. There the two modes mix and repel each other instead of crossing, an anticrossing, and one doped sample shows all of it, where a bulk crystal needs a series of densities. The simplest model describes the carriers as free particles in the sheet.

  1. A free carrier of charge $-e$ and effective mass $m^*$ in the in-plane field $\bE_\parallel\ee^{-\ii\omega t}$ is displaced by $\delta\br_\parallel=e\bE_\parallel/(m^*\omega^2)$.

    Newton's equation $m^*\ddot{\delta\br}_\parallel=-e\bE_\parallel$ with $\ddot{\delta\br}_\parallel=-\omega^2\delta\br_\parallel$. A free charge has no restoring force, so its response is set by its inertia alone.

  2. With $n^{\mathrm{2D}}$ carriers per area, the dipole per area is $-en^{\mathrm{2D}}\delta\br_\parallel$: a sheet polarizability $-n^{\mathrm{2D}}e^2/(m^*\omega^2)$.

    The polarizability is negative: above any resonance a charge moves against the force on it, and a free charge has its resonance at zero frequency.

  3. Add it to the bound response in , with $2\pi$ times the polarizability times $Q_\parallel$ as in :

    $$\begin{gathered}\varepsilon^{\mathrm{2D}}(Q_\parallel,\omega)\\ =\varepsilon_{\mathrm{env}}+r_0Q_\parallel-\frac{\big[\omega_{\mathrm p}^{\mathrm{2D}}\big]^2}{\omega^2},\\[4pt] \big[\omega_{\mathrm p}^{\mathrm{2D}}\big]^2=\frac{2\pi n^{\mathrm{2D}}e^2}{m^*}\,Q_\parallel .\end{gathered} \tag{B10.25}$$
    The same result follows from the intraband terms of for free carriers, the two-dimensional form of Lindhard's response of Section A.2, when the wave is fast compared with the carriers, $\omega\gg v_{\mathrm F}Q_\parallel$ with $v_{\mathrm F}$ the Fermi velocity. In that limit each carrier responds to the field at its own position, as in Drude's model of a metal.

  4. The longitudinal modes of the electrons alone are the zeros of $\varepsilon^{\mathrm{2D}}$, as in : $\omega^2=[\omega_{\mathrm p}^{\mathrm{2D}}]^2/(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{env}}+r_0Q_\parallel)$, the sheet plasmon, with $\omega\propto\sqrt{Q_\parallel}$ at small $Q_\parallel$ (Stern). For $Q_\parallel\gg\varepsilon_{\mathrm{env}}/r_0$ the sheet's own screening flattens it at $\omega^2=2\pi n^{\mathrm{2D}}e^2/(m^*r_0)$, so it reaches $\omega_{\mathrm{TO}}$ only if $n^{\mathrm{2D}}\gt m^*r_0\omega_{\mathrm{TO}}^2/(2\pi e^2)$: about $2\times10^{12}$ cm⁻² for hBN and $7\times10^{11}$ cm⁻² for the MoS₂-like sheet, with $m^*=0.5\,m_{\mathrm e}$.

    At $\varepsilon^{\mathrm{2D}}=0$ a charge wave sustains itself without an external source. In bulk the plasma frequency $\omega_{\mathrm p}$ of Section A.5 stays finite as $q\to0$; in a sheet the restoring field vanishes with $Q_\parallel$, as for the LO phonon.

  5. Take the pole condition $\omega^2=\omega^2_{\bQ_\parallel\nu}+2\omega_{\bQ_\parallel\nu}\Pi^{\ph}_{\nu\nu}/\hbar$ with , keep the frequency in $W^{\mathrm{e,2D}}=v^{\mathrm{2D}}/\varepsilon^{\mathrm{2D}}(Q_\parallel,\omega)$ instead of setting it to zero, apply it to the two-ion sheet, and multiply by $\omega^2\varepsilon^{\mathrm{2D}}$:

    $$\begin{gathered}\big(\omega^2-\omega_{\mathrm{TO}}^2\big)\\ \times\Big[\big(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{env}}+r_0Q_\parallel\big)\,\omega^2\\ -\big[\omega_{\mathrm p}^{\mathrm{2D}}\big]^2\Big]\\ =\frac{2\pi e^2Z^{*2}}{\OS M_{\mathrm r}}\,Q_\parallel\,\omega^2 .\end{gathered} \tag{B10.26}$$
    Without carriers it returns ; without polar coupling ($Z^*=0$) its roots are the TO frequency and the plasmon. It is a quadratic equation in $\omega^2$, with two coupled modes.

At the wavevector where the plasmon crosses $\omega_{\mathrm{TO}}$, the two roots of are

$$\omega\simeq\omega_{\mathrm{TO}}\pm\frac12\Big[\frac{2\pi e^2Z^{*2}Q_\parallel}{\OS M_{\mathrm r}(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{env}}+r_0Q_\parallel)}\Big]^{1/2},$$

so the splitting is the square root of the squared-frequency shift of the undoped sheet at that $Q_\parallel$, not the shift itself. For a weak coupling this is far larger than the undoped shift. In the MoS₂-like sheet with $n^{\mathrm{2D}}=10^{12}$ cm⁻² and $m^*=0.5\,m_{\mathrm e}$ the crossing lies near $Q_\parallel=0.05$ Å⁻¹ and the modes are split by 4.3 meV, while the undoped LO is shifted there by only 0.2 meV. In hBN with $n^{\mathrm{2D}}=10^{13}$ cm⁻² (a thought experiment: hBN cannot be doped this far in practice) the splitting is 41 meV near 0.03 Å⁻¹. Beyond the crossing the upper branch turns plasmon-like and the lower branch is pushed below $\omega_{\mathrm{TO}}$: below the plasma frequency the carriers overscreen, $\varepsilon^{\mathrm{2D}}\lt0$, which reverses the sign of the shift. The phonon spectral function $-\frac1\pi\mathrm{Im}\,F_{\nu\nu}$ in the "doped" view of shows the two peaks.15

The Drude form holds while the plasmon lies well above the continuum of intraband electron–hole pairs, whose energies reach up to $\hbar v_{\mathrm F}Q_\parallel$: that is the condition $\omega\gg v_{\mathrm F}Q_\parallel$. It drops both the correction of order $(v_{\mathrm F}Q_\parallel/\omega)^2$ to the plasmon dispersion and the damping by pair creation (Landau damping). At the crossings of both examples $\hbar v_{\mathrm F}Q_\parallel$ is 0.15–0.4 times $\hbar\omega_{\mathrm{TO}}$, so the curves are qualitatively right there. Finite $c$: when the sheet radiates

Everything so far used the electrostatic limit $c\to\infty$, in which the field follows the charges instantly. (The effects of a finite speed of light are often called retardation; the review uses that word for the frequency dependence of the electronic screening, so this section avoids it.) A polarization wave in a sheet with in-plane wavevector $\bQ_\parallel$ can emit a photon only if the photon can carry that in-plane wavevector: a photon of frequency $\omega$ in the environment has a wavevector of length $\sqrt{\varepsilon_{\mathrm{env}}}\,\omega/c$, so emission needs $Q_\parallel\lt\sqrt{\varepsilon_{\mathrm{env}}}\,\omega/c$. This is the light cone of a sheet. Inside it the sheet radiates into the half spaces above and below, with the normal wavevector $q_z=\sqrt{\varepsilon_{\mathrm{env}}\omega^2/c^2-Q_\parallel^2}$; outside it the field decays away from the sheet. The propagator of the photon in the plane makes this quantitative.

  1. Add the transverse and longitudinal free propagators $D^{0\mathrm T}$ and $D^{0\mathrm L}$ of Section B.5, and :

    $$D^0_{\alpha\beta}(\bq,\omega)=\frac{4\pi c}{\omega^2}\,\frac{\omega^2\delta_{\alpha\beta}/c^2-q_\alpha q_\beta}{q^2-\omega^2/c^2} .$$
    $D^{0\mathrm T}$ is $-4\pi c(\delta_{\alpha\beta}-\hat q_\alpha\hat q_\beta)$ divided by $\omega^2-c^2q^2$, and $D^{0\mathrm L}=-4\pi c\,\hat q_\alpha\hat q_\beta/\omega^2$. With the common factor $-4\pi c$ taken out, the two $\hat q_\alpha\hat q_\beta$ terms combine over a common denominator to $-c^2q_\alpha q_\beta/[\omega^2(\omega^2-c^2q^2)]$, and the $\delta_{\alpha\beta}$ term is $\omega^2\delta_{\alpha\beta}/[\omega^2(\omega^2-c^2q^2)]$; dividing numerator and denominator by $-c^2$ gives the form shown.

  2. Sum over $q_z$ at fixed in-plane $\alpha,\beta=x,y$, with $\int\frac{\dd q_z}{2\pi}(q_z^2+\zeta^2)^{-1}=1/2\zeta$:

    $$\begin{gathered}D^0_{\alpha\beta}(\bQ_\parallel,\omega;0,0)\\ =\frac{2\pi c}{\omega^2\,\zeta}\Big(\frac{\omega^2}{c^2}\delta_{\alpha\beta}-Q_\alpha Q_\beta\Big),\\[4pt] \zeta=\sqrt{Q_\parallel^2-\omega^2/c^2} .\end{gathered} \tag{B10.27}$$
    As in , a sheet contains every $q_z$. For in-plane components $q_\alpha q_\beta=Q_\alpha Q_\beta$ does not depend on $q_z$, and $q^2-\omega^2/c^2=q_z^2+\zeta^2$.

Here $\zeta$ is the inverse decay length of the field away from the sheet. Projected on $\hat\bQ_\parallel$ the propagator is $-2\pi c\zeta/\omega^2$, which tends to the electrostatic $-\frac{c}{\omega^2}Q_\parallel^2\,(2\pi/Q_\parallel)$ of as $c\to\infty$; the in-plane component perpendicular to $\hat\bQ_\parallel$ is $2\pi/(c\zeta)$, which vanishes in that limit. A homogeneous environment replaces $\omega^2/c^2$ by $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{env}}\omega^2/c^2$ in $\zeta$, because light in the medium obeys with the transverse dielectric function $\varepsilon^{\mathrm T}$ of Section B.6 equal to $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{env}}$, and it divides the longitudinal part by $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{env}}$. Repeating the steps that led to with $\zeta$ in place of $Q_\parallel$, in the kernel and in the screening by the sheet's electrons, gives the LO-like mode at finite $c$:

$$\begin{gathered}\omega^2=\omega_{\mathrm{TO}}^2+\frac{2\pi e^2Z^{*2}}{\OS M_{\mathrm r}}\\ \times\frac{\zeta}{\varepsilon_{\mathrm{env}}+r_0\zeta},\\[4pt] \zeta=\sqrt{Q_\parallel^2-\varepsilon_{\mathrm{env}}\,\omega^2/c^2} .\end{gathered} \tag{B10.28}$$

The equation is self-consistent, since $\zeta$ contains $\omega$. Outside the light cone $\zeta\lt Q_\parallel$ is real, and the shift falls to zero at the light line $Q_\parallel=\sqrt{\varepsilon_{\mathrm{env}}}\,\omega/c$, the edge of the light cone, where $\zeta\to0$. Inside, $\zeta=-\ii q_z$, with the sign that makes $\ee^{-\zeta|z|}=\ee^{\ii q_z|z|}$ an outgoing wave, and the shift of $\omega^2$ becomes complex. Writing it as $\omega^2-\omega_{\mathrm{TO}}^2=\dots-\ii\gamma\omega$, as for a damped oscillator, defines the radiative linewidth $\gamma$, a full width at half maximum.

The two in-plane modes radiate different polarizations. The LO-like mode, whose dipoles lie along $\hat\bQ_\parallel$, radiates p-polarized light, with its electric field in the plane of $\hat\bQ_\parallel$ and the normal. The TO-like mode, whose dipoles are perpendicular to $\hat\bQ_\parallel$, radiates s-polarized light, with its electric field along the sheet and perpendicular to $\hat\bQ_\parallel$; it couples through the perpendicular component of . Their radiative widths are16

$$\begin{gathered}\gamma^{\mathrm{rad}}_{\mathrm p}=\frac{2\pi e^2Z^{*2}}{\OS M_{\mathrm r}\,\omega_{\mathrm{TO}}}\\ \times\frac{\varepsilon_{\mathrm{env}}\,q_z}{\varepsilon_{\mathrm{env}}^2+r_0^2q_z^2},\\[4pt] \gamma^{\mathrm{rad}}_{\mathrm s}=\frac{2\pi e^2Z^{*2}}{\OS M_{\mathrm r}}\,\frac{\omega_{\mathrm{TO}}}{c^2\,q_z} .\end{gathered} \tag{B10.29}$$
$\zeta$
inverse decay length of the field away from the sheet (Å⁻¹); imaginary inside the light cone
$q_z$
normal wavevector of the emitted light inside the light cone (Å⁻¹)
$\gamma^{\mathrm{rad}}_{\mathrm p}$, $\gamma^{\mathrm{rad}}_{\mathrm s}$
radiative linewidths (full width at half maximum) of the LO-like and TO-like in-plane modes (s⁻¹; $\hbar\gamma$ is quoted in μeV)

At $\Gamma$, $q_z=\sqrt{\varepsilon_{\mathrm{env}}}\,\omega_{\mathrm{TO}}/c$ and $r_0q_z\ll\varepsilon_{\mathrm{env}}$, so both widths reduce to $2\pi e^2Z^{*2}/(\OS M_{\mathrm r}c\sqrt{\varepsilon_{\mathrm{env}}})$, as they must, since the two in-plane directions are equivalent there. (The s width leaves out the screening of the emitted field by the sheet's electrons, a factor $1/[1+(r_0\omega_{\mathrm{TO}}^2/c^2q_z)^2]$; at $\Gamma$ it equals the factor $1/[1+(r_0q_z/\varepsilon_{\mathrm{env}})^2]$ contained in the p width, and for hBN both differ from 1 by less than $10^{-6}$.) Away from $\Gamma$ the p width falls as $q_z$ and the s width grows as $1/q_z$ towards the light line; the same two laws govern the radiative widths of excitons in quantum wells. For freestanding hBN the light line lies at $Q_\parallel=8.6\times10^{-5}$ Å⁻¹, a wavelength of 7.3 μm; the radiative width at $\Gamma$ is 42 μeV, and the electrostatic shift at the light line is only 21 μeV. Everything that the finite speed of light changes happens in this tiny window, below about $10^{-4}$ Å⁻¹ or a few times $10^{-5}$ of the size of the zone, which is why the electrostatic result is safe for phonon physics. The "finite c" view of shows the window on a logarithmic scale.

Physical picture: a sheet leaks where a bulk crystal mixes

In a bulk crystal a TO phonon with $q\approx\omega/c$ meets a photon with the same three-dimensional wavevector, and the two mix into a phonon polariton, a stationary mode of lattice and light, . A sheet fixes only the in-plane wavevector, and the photon is free to take any $q_z$. Inside the light cone an outgoing photon with the right in-plane wavevector exists, so the polar modes of the sheet decay into light and acquire a width. Outside it the field is evanescent and the modes stay bound to the sheet; the TO-like mode is pulled slightly below $\omega_{\mathrm{TO}}$ near the light line, the beginning of a guided polariton.

Where these objects go next

This section ends the EPP formalism. Its message is that a single function, the screened interaction $W^{\mathrm{e,2D}}(Q_\parallel)$ of , controls the long-range physics of a polar sheet: the LO dispersion , the two-dimensional Fröhlich coupling of carriers, the binding of excitons and, with carriers, the plasmon–phonon modes . in-plane wavevector of a wave in a sheet, its length and its direction (Å⁻¹); $\Gamma$ is $\bQ_\parallel=0$. EPP: $\mathbf Q=(Q_x,Q_y)$, $Q$.
$\mathbf K_\parallel$
in-plane wavevector of an electron state of the sheet (Å⁻¹). $\hat{\mathbf v}$: velocity operator, used for interband position matrix elements in . EPP: $\mathbf K=(K_x,K_y)$ in the surface Brillouin zone (SBZ).
$\OSSC$
area of the Born–von Kármán supercell of a sheet, $N_\ell\OS$: the whole sheet (Å²); sums over the states of a sheet carry $1/\OSSC$, as bulk sums carry $1/\OVSC$. EPP: $S$ (its cell area $S_{\rm uc}$ is our $\OS$).
$\rho^{\mathrm{2D}}$, $\rho_0$
charge per unit area of a sheet; amplitude of a charge wave $\rho^{\mathrm{2D}}=\rho_0\ee^{\ii\bQ_\parallel\cdot\br_\parallel}$ ($e$/Å²). In space, $\rho(\br)=\rho^{\mathrm{2D}}(\br_\parallel)\delta(z)$.
