From electron–phonon to electron–phonon–photon

Part A describes electrons and lattice vibrations that interact through one force: the Coulomb interaction between charges. It acts instantaneously, and the electrons and, through the vibrations, the ions screen it. Light appears only as an external field that shines on the crystal.

Part B replaces this picture with the full electromagnetic field. Electrons and ions couple to one field. Its longitudinal part reproduces the Coulomb interaction, and its transverse part is light, which travels at a finite speed. The two bridge sections connect the two languages before Part B builds the new one: Section X.1 shows where the two pictures differ and by how much, and Section X.2 maps their objects onto each other.

From the Coulomb line to the full field

Every interaction in Part A travels along one kind of line: the instantaneous Coulomb interaction, screened by the medium. Part B replaces it with the propagator of the full electromagnetic field. The longitudinal part of that propagator is the same Coulomb line; its transverse part is light. This section defines the two new objects, the photon propagator and the photon self-energy, and shows that they obey a Dyson equation of the same form as the screened Coulomb interaction. It then measures the region of wavevectors where the two theories differ: a sphere whose radius is about twenty thousand times smaller than that of the Brillouin zone, which is exactly where optics happens.

What you need: the Coulomb interaction $v$ and electronic screening (Section A.3); Born charges, the field of a longitudinal optical wave, the LO–TO splitting and the light cone (Section A.4); the screened interaction $W$, its Dyson equation and the phonon propagator (Section A.5). From electrodynamics: Maxwell's equations, the scalar and vector potentials, and plane waves. Two background boxes recap the rest: “Potentials, gauges and longitudinal fields” and “Response functions, retarded and time ordered”. Equations are in Gaussian units; the notes give their SI forms.1

Every line in Part A is a Coulomb line

The review builds Part A on the most general nonrelativistic Hamiltonian of electrons and nuclei. It contains the kinetic energies of all particles and one interaction between any two charges, the Coulomb interaction. For two electrons it is the potential energy

$$\begin{gathered}v(\br-\br')=\frac{e^2}{|\br-\br'|},\\ v(\bq)=\frac{4\pi e^2}{q^2},\\ \text{in space and time: }v\,\delta(t-t') .\end{gathered} \tag{X1.1}$$
$v$
bare Coulomb interaction energy of two electrons (eV), as in Section A.3; its Fourier transform $v(\bq)$, at wavevector $\bq$, is per unit volume (eV Å³)
$\delta(t-t')$
the two charges interact at equal times
$e$
elementary charge; in Gaussian units $e^2=14.3996$ eV Å

An electron and a nucleus, or two nuclei, interact by the same law with the product of their charges. The factor $\delta(t-t')$ makes the interaction instantaneous: a charge moved at time $t$ is felt everywhere at the same time $t$, whatever the distance. The Hamiltonian has no vector potential, no current and no magnetic field. Even the probe that the review uses to generate its equations is a scalar potential coupled to the total charge.2 Every object of Part A is built from this one line:

  • the force constants of Section A.1 are second derivatives of the Born–Oppenheimer energy, which is kinetic and Coulomb energy;
  • the electron–phonon matrix element $g$ of Section A.2 is the change of the electron–nucleus Coulomb potential when the nuclei move, screened in Section A.3 by the Hartree (Coulomb) and exchange–correlation potentials of the electrons;
  • the long-range vertex $g^{\mathrm{lr}}$ and the LO–TO splitting of Section A.4 come from Gauss's law: the electrostatic field of the dipoles that a lattice wave carries;
  • in Section A.5 the screened interaction $W$ obeys $W=v+v\chi^{\mathrm{irr}}W$ , where the irreducible polarizability $\chi^{\mathrm{irr}}$ is the density response of all charges to the total potential; letting the nuclei move splits it as $W=W^{\mathrm e}+W^{\mathrm{ph}}$ , into the interaction screened by the electrons with the nuclei clamped and the part carried by the moving nuclei; the self-energies of Sections A.6–A.8 are built from $W$;
  • light, in Section A.9, is a classical field imposed from outside.

In diagrams the bare Coulomb line is drawn dashed and the screened one double dashed (). What the Coulomb line leaves out is the rest of the electromagnetic field: the magnetic forces between currents, the electric field induced by a changing current, and radiation. The omission costs little, and the second half of the section puts a number on it: at the frequencies of lattice vibrations Part A fails only at wavevectors below about $10^{-4}$ Å⁻¹, and beyond that its error falls as the inverse square of the wavevector.

Two meanings of retardation

A response is retarded when it comes later than its cause. Two different delays go by this name in the two sources. Both make an interaction depend on frequency, which is why they are easily confused.

  1. Dynamical screening. The electrons need time to rearrange. If the nuclei move slowly compared with that time, the electrons follow at every instant and screen the motion as if it were static; that is the adiabatic approximation behind density-functional perturbation theory (Section A.3). Otherwise the electronic dielectric function $\varepsilon^{\mathrm e}$ must be taken at the angular frequency $\omega$ of the motion, $\varepsilon^{\mathrm e}(\bq,\omega)$. Bardeen and Pines, and later Baym, kept this frequency dependence, and so does the review. Section A.6 shows where it matters: metals, doped semiconductors, graphene.
  2. Finite speed of light. The field itself needs time to cross a distance. A charge moved at time $t$ is felt at distance $r$ only at $t+r/c$, where $c$ is the speed of light ($\hbar c=1973.27$ eV Å); in a medium of refractive index $\sqrt{\varepsilon_\infty}$, where $\varepsilon_\infty$ is the electronic dielectric constant, the delay is $\sqrt{\varepsilon_\infty}\,r/c$. Part A sets $c\to\infty$. The EPP manuscript restores finite $c$.

What replaces the Coulomb line

The plan of this subsection follows the order of Section A.1: the simplest source of field first, then the general object.

  1. compute the field of the simplest source in a crystal, a wave of oscillating dipoles, with all of Maxwell's equations;
  2. generalize it to any current: the free photon propagator $D^0$, whose longitudinal part turns out to be the Coulomb line;
  3. add the response of the medium: the photon propagator $D$ and the photon self-energy $\Pi$, and connect $\Pi$ to the density response of Part A.

The field of a polarization wave

An optical phonon of a polar crystal carries a wave of oscillating dipoles (Section A.4). Its dipole moment per unit volume, the polarization $\bP$, is a plane wave, $\bP\,\ee^{\ii(\bq\cdot\br-\omega t)}$, where $\bP$ now denotes the complex amplitude; the physical polarization is the real part. Split the polarization into a part along the unit vector $\hat\bq=\bq/q$ and a part perpendicular to it:

$$\bP^{\mathrm L}=\hat\bq\,\big(\hat\bq\cdot\bP\big),\qquad \bP^{\mathrm T}=\bP-\bP^{\mathrm L} .$$

$\bP^{\mathrm L}$ is the longitudinal part and $\bP^{\mathrm T}$ the transverse part, the same words as for the atomic motion of a lattice wave in Section A.1. The two parts behave very differently.

  • A longitudinal wave piles up charge. The bound charge density $\rho$$\,=-\nabla\cdot\bP=-\ii\bq\cdot\bP$ is nonzero only for $\bP^{\mathrm L}$: sheets of positive and negative charge, perpendicular to $\bq$, half a wavelength apart. Their field follows from Gauss's law at each instant.
  • A transverse wave has $\nabla\cdot\bP=0$: no charge anywhere, so Coulomb's law gives it no field at all. It still carries a current density $\bj$$\,=\partial_t\bP$, and a changing current induces a field through Ampère's and Faraday's laws. That induced field depends on $c$, and in Part A it is zero.

To compute both fields with one formalism, describe the field by potentials. The vector potential $\bA$ and the scalar potential $A_0$ of Section A.4 give the electric field $\bE$$\,=-\nabla A_0-\frac1c\partial_t\bA$ and the magnetic field $\bB=\nabla\times\bA$, and they can be changed without changing $\bE$ and $\bB$. This freedom lets one set $A_0=0$, the temporal gauge used throughout Part B (background box).

Potentials, gauges and longitudinal fields skip if familiar

Two of Maxwell's equations, $\nabla\cdot\bB=0$ and Faraday's law $\nabla\times\bE=-\frac1c\partial_t\bB$, are solved identically by writing the fields through potentials: $\bB=\nabla\times\bA$ and $\bE=-\nabla A_0-\frac1c\partial_t\bA$, where $\bA$ is the vector potential and $A_0$ the scalar potential. The potentials are not unique. For any smooth function $\vartheta(\br,t)$, the replacement

$$\bA\to\bA+\nabla\vartheta,\qquad A_0\to A_0-\frac1c\,\partial_t\vartheta$$

leaves $\bE$ and $\bB$ unchanged, because the curl of a gradient vanishes and the two changes of $\bE$ cancel. This is gauge freedom, and fixing $\vartheta$ is choosing a gauge. Choosing $\vartheta$ with $\partial_t\vartheta=cA_0$ removes the scalar potential: in the temporal gauge $A_0=0$ and $\bE=-\frac1c\partial_t\bA$. The Coulomb gauge, $\nabla\cdot\bA=0$, is the other common choice; it keeps $A_0$ as the instantaneous Coulomb potential. Both describe the same physics.

Any vector field splits, Fourier component by Fourier component, into a part along $\hat\bq$ and a part perpendicular to it. The longitudinal part has no curl, because $\ii\bq\times\bA^{\mathrm L}=0$, and the transverse part no divergence, because $\ii\bq\cdot\bA^{\mathrm T}=0$. A longitudinal vector potential therefore produces no magnetic field, only an electric field along $\hat\bq$: in the temporal gauge it does the job of the scalar potential.

In the temporal gauge, Maxwell's equations reduce to one equation for $\bA$ with the current as its source:4

$$\begin{gathered}\bE=-\frac1c\,\partial_t\bA,\qquad \bB=\nabla\times\bA,\\ \nabla\times\big(\nabla\times\bA\big)+\frac{1}{c^2}\,\partial_t^2\bA=\frac{4\pi}{c}\,\bj .\end{gathered} \tag{X1.2}$$
$\bA$
vector potential in the temporal gauge; in Gaussian units it has the units of a potential (V)
$\bE$, $\bB$
electric and magnetic fields; in Gaussian units both have the units of a field (V/Å)
$\bj$
current density: charge per unit area and time, carried by any moving charge

The second line is the Ampère–Maxwell law, $\nabla\times\bB=\frac{4\pi}c\bj+\frac1c\partial_t\bE$, with the potentials inserted. Gauss's law $\nabla\cdot\bE=4\pi\rho$ is not lost. The divergence of the second line, with charge conservation $\partial_t\rho+\nabla\cdot\bj=0$, gives $\partial_t(\nabla\cdot\bE-4\pi\rho)=0$, so Gauss's law holds at all times once it holds at the start. Now insert the polarization wave. As for $\bP$, the symbols $\bE$, $\bA$ and $\bj$ in the steps denote complex amplitudes of plane waves:

  1. For plane waves every derivative brings down a factor: $\nabla\to\ii\bq$ and $\partial_t\to-\ii\omega$. So $\bE=\frac{\ii\omega}c\bA$, and the double curl becomes

    $$\nabla\times(\nabla\times\bA)\to-\bq\times(\bq\times\bA)=q^2\bA^{\mathrm T} .$$
    By the vector identity $\bq\times(\bq\times\bA)=\bq(\bq\cdot\bA)-q^2\bA$, and $q^2\bA-\bq(\bq\cdot\bA)$ is $q^2$ times the transverse part of $\bA$. The longitudinal part has no curl.

  2. The wave equation becomes $q^2\bA^{\mathrm T}-\frac{\omega^2}{c^2}\big(\bA^{\mathrm L}+\bA^{\mathrm T}\big)=\frac{4\pi}{c}\big(\bj^{\mathrm L}+\bj^{\mathrm T}\big)$. Its longitudinal and transverse parts give

    $$\begin{gathered}\bA^{\mathrm L}=-\frac{4\pi c}{\omega^2}\,\bj^{\mathrm L},\\ \bA^{\mathrm T}=-\frac{4\pi c}{\omega^2-c^2q^2}\,\bj^{\mathrm T} .\end{gathered} \tag{X1.3}$$
    Vectors along and across $\hat\bq$ are orthogonal, so the equation holds for each part separately. The longitudinal equation has no $q^2$ term: a longitudinal $\bA$ has no curl, hence no magnetic field and no restoring force that would make it oscillate (Section B.4 returns to this).

  3. For the polarization wave the current is $\bj=\partial_t\bP=-\ii\omega\bP$, and $\bE=\frac{\ii\omega}{c}\bA$. Multiplying by $\frac{\ii\omega}c$ gives the field:

    $$\begin{gathered}\bE^{\mathrm L}=-4\pi\bP^{\mathrm L},\\ \bE^{\mathrm T}=-\frac{4\pi\bP^{\mathrm T}}{1-c^2q^2/\omega^2} .\end{gathered} \tag{X1.4}$$
    $\frac{\ii\omega}c\cdot(-\ii\omega)=\frac{\omega^2}{c}$, and $\frac{\omega^2}c\cdot\big(-\frac{4\pi c}{\omega^2}\big)=-4\pi$; for the transverse part, divide numerator and denominator by $\omega^2$.

The longitudinal field $\bE^{\mathrm L}=-4\pi\bP^{\mathrm L}$ contains neither $c$ nor $q$. It is the field between the sheets of bound charge, set up by Gauss's law at every instant: the Coulomb field of the wave, the longitudinal field of Section A.4 without the electronic screening. The transverse field depends on $c$, and only through the ratio $cq/\omega$, the speed of light divided by the speed $\omega/q$ at which the wave pattern moves (its phase velocity). Three regimes follow.5

  • The pattern moves much slower than light, $cq\gg\omega$. Then $\bE^{\mathrm T}\simeq4\pi\bP^{\mathrm T}\,(\omega/cq)^2$: tiny, and parallel to $\bP$. In GaAs a transverse optical (TO) phonon of 33.3 meV with $q=0.025$ Å⁻¹ has $\omega/cq=6.75\times10^{-4}$, so its transverse field is $(\omega/cq)^2=4.6\times10^{-7}$ of the longitudinal field of the same polarization (without the electrons, which the next paragraph adds). Electrostatics, which sets $c\to\infty$, gives zero.
  • The pattern moves at the speed of light, $cq=\omega$. The wave drives the field resonantly and $\bE^{\mathrm T}$ diverges. In a real crystal light and phonon then mix into new modes, the phonon polaritons that Section A.4 named and Section B.6 builds.
  • The pattern moves faster than light, $cq\ll\omega$: the wavelength is longer than that of light at the same frequency. Then $\bE^{\mathrm T}\to-4\pi\bP^{\mathrm T}$, the same as the longitudinal field. At $\bq\to0$ a uniform polarization produces the same field whatever its direction.

The electrons of an insulator add their own polarization. They divide the longitudinal field by the electronic dielectric constant $\varepsilon_\infty$ and slow light to $c/\sqrt{\varepsilon_\infty}$, so the transverse resonance moves to $cq=\sqrt{\varepsilon_\infty}\,\omega$. Well below the resonance, $cq\ll\sqrt{\varepsilon_\infty}\,\omega$, the transverse field is divided by $\varepsilon_\infty$ too. Well above it the transverse field hardly changes while the longitudinal one shrinks, so their ratio grows by $\varepsilon_\infty$, to $5\times10^{-6}$ at $q=0.025$ Å⁻¹ in GaAs (shown in below).

The resonance sits at the wavevector of light of frequency $\omega$ in the medium, $q_{\mathrm{light}}=\sqrt{\varepsilon_\infty}\,\omega/c$ ($\omega/c$ without the electrons). The wavevectors below it, where the wave pattern outruns light, form the light cone of Section A.4. That section met its radius at the LO frequency $\omega_{\mathrm{LO}}$, as $q_{\mathrm c}$$\,=q_{\mathrm{light}}(\omega_{\mathrm{LO}})$; here the frequency is free, and the size of the light cone below explains the name and measures it. draws both waves and their fields as $q$ crosses $q_{\mathrm{light}}$.

The free photon propagator

Step 2 above never used the fact that the current came from a polarization wave. Any current can be written as a sum of plane waves, and each one produces the field . Written as a matrix that acts on the current, this is the free photon propagator $D^0$: the vector potential produced in vacuum per unit current.6

$$\begin{gathered}A_\alpha(\bq,\omega)=\sum_\beta D^0_{\alpha\beta}(\bq,\omega)\,j_\beta(\bq,\omega),\\ D^0_{\alpha\beta}=D^{0\mathrm L}\,\hat q_\alpha\hat q_\beta\\ \qquad+D^{0\mathrm T}\big(\delta_{\alpha\beta}-\hat q_\alpha\hat q_\beta\big),\\ D^{0\mathrm L}=-\frac{4\pi c}{(\omega+\ii\eta)^2},\\ D^{0\mathrm T}=-\frac{4\pi c}{(\omega+\ii\eta)^2-c^2q^2} .\end{gathered} \tag{X1.5}$$
$\alpha,\beta$
Cartesian components $x,y,z$
$\hat q_\alpha\hat q_\beta$, $\delta_{\alpha\beta}-\hat q_\alpha\hat q_\beta$
projectors onto the longitudinal and transverse parts of a vector
$D^{0\mathrm L}$, $D^{0\mathrm T}$
longitudinal and transverse coefficients of $D^0$ (units: length × time, for example Å s; then $\bA=D^0\bj$ has the units of $\bE$ times length)
$\eta$
positive infinitesimal, $\eta\to0^+$: the field comes after the current that produces it (a retarded function)

With Cartesian indices, $D^{0\mathrm T}_{\alpha\beta}=D^{0\mathrm T}(\delta_{\alpha\beta}-\hat q_\alpha\hat q_\beta)$ is the transverse tensor and $D^{0\mathrm L}_{\alpha\beta}=D^{0\mathrm L}\hat q_\alpha\hat q_\beta$ the longitudinal one. Two features of carry the rest of the section. The transverse coefficient has poles at $\omega=\pm cq$: these are free photons, and the curve $\omega=cq$ in the plane of wavevector and frequency is the light line. The longitudinal coefficient does not depend on $q$ at all: it has no pole on the light line, nothing in it travels, and it is the same at every wavelength. It is the Coulomb interaction, written for currents.

Derivation: $D^0$ is the inverse of the Maxwell operator

  1. Write the Fourier-transformed wave equation of step 2 above in components, as a matrix $\mathsf M$, the Maxwell operator, acting on $\bA$:

    $$\begin{gathered}\sum_\gamma \mathsf M_{\alpha\gamma}A_\gamma=\frac{4\pi}{c}\,j_\alpha,\\ \mathsf M_{\alpha\gamma}=\Big(q^2-\frac{\omega^2}{c^2}\Big)\delta_{\alpha\gamma}-q_\alpha q_\gamma .\end{gathered}$$
    $q^2\bA^{\mathrm T}=q^2\bA-\bq(\bq\cdot\bA)$, whose component $\alpha$ is $\sum_\gamma(q^2\delta_{\alpha\gamma}-q_\alpha q_\gamma)A_\gamma$.

  2. Split $\mathsf M$ with the two projectors, using $q_\alpha q_\gamma=q^2\hat q_\alpha\hat q_\gamma$:

    $$\mathsf M_{\alpha\gamma}=-\frac{\omega^2}{c^2}\,\hat q_\alpha\hat q_\gamma+\Big(q^2-\frac{\omega^2}{c^2}\Big)\big(\delta_{\alpha\gamma}-\hat q_\alpha\hat q_\gamma\big) .$$
    Write $\delta_{\alpha\gamma}=\hat q_\alpha\hat q_\gamma+(\delta_{\alpha\gamma}-\hat q_\alpha\hat q_\gamma)$ and collect the coefficient of each projector.

  3. Invert each coefficient separately and multiply by $4\pi/c$: $D^0=\frac{4\pi}{c}\mathsf M^{-1}$ has the longitudinal coefficient $-4\pi c/\omega^2$ and the transverse coefficient $\frac{4\pi}{c}\big/\big(q^2-\frac{\omega^2}{c^2}\big)=-4\pi c/(\omega^2-c^2q^2)$, which is .

    The projectors multiply like numbers that are 0 or 1: each is its own square and their product vanishes. So $\mathsf M$ times the matrix with the inverted coefficients is the sum of the two projectors, the unit matrix: that matrix is $\mathsf M^{-1}$.

  4. Over a common denominator the two parts combine into one fraction, the form in which the EPP manuscript writes $D^0$:

    $$D^0_{\alpha\beta}=\frac{4\pi c}{\omega^2}\,\frac{-q_\alpha q_\beta+\frac{\omega^2}{c^2}\,\delta_{\alpha\beta}}{q^2-\omega^2/c^2} .$$
    Take out the factor $4\pi c/\omega^2$, multiply the longitudinal term by $(q^2-\omega^2/c^2)/(q^2-\omega^2/c^2)$ and collect the numerator: $-q^2\hat q_\alpha\hat q_\beta+\frac{\omega^2}{c^2}\big[\hat q_\alpha\hat q_\beta+(\delta_{\alpha\beta}-\hat q_\alpha\hat q_\beta)\big]=-q_\alpha q_\beta+\frac{\omega^2}{c^2}\delta_{\alpha\beta}$.

$D^0$ is therefore the Green's function of Maxwell's equations in the temporal gauge: the vector potential of a point current, Fourier component by Fourier component. The steps used a real $\omega$. Replacing it by $\omega+\ii\eta$, as in , moves the poles just below the real frequency axis and selects the solution in which the field comes after the current.

The longitudinal part is the Coulomb line

Two conversions turn the Coulomb interaction of charges into a propagator for currents: the gauge turns a scalar potential into a longitudinal vector potential, and charge conservation turns a charge into a longitudinal current.

  1. In the Coulomb picture a charge density $\rho$ creates the scalar potential $A_0=4\pi\rho/q^2$, and the field $\bE=-\nabla A_0=-\ii\bq A_0$.

    Poisson's equation, $-\nabla^2A_0=4\pi\rho$, in Fourier space. The field is along $\bq$: longitudinal.

  2. In the temporal gauge the same field is $\bE=\frac{\ii\omega}c\bA^{\mathrm L}$, so $\bA^{\mathrm L}=-\frac{c}{\omega}\,\bq\,A_0$.

    Both gauges must give the same $\bE$: $-\ii\bq A_0=\frac{\ii\omega}{c}\bA^{\mathrm L}$.

  3. Charge conservation, $\partial_t\rho+\nabla\cdot\bj=0$, reads $\omega\rho=\bq\cdot\bj$ for a plane wave.

    $-\ii\omega\rho+\ii\bq\cdot\bj=0$. Only the longitudinal part of the current changes the charge.

  4. Combine the three: $\bA^{\mathrm L}=-\frac c\omega\,\bq\,\frac{4\pi}{q^2}\,\frac{\bq\cdot\bj}{\omega}$. In components, with $4\pi/q^2=v(\bq)/e^2$,

    $$D^{0\mathrm L}\,\hat q_\alpha\hat q_\beta=-\frac{c}{\omega^2}\,q_\alpha q_\beta\,\frac{v(\bq)}{e^2} . \tag{X1.6}$$
    $\frac{c}{\omega^2}q^2\frac{4\pi}{q^2}=\frac{4\pi c}{\omega^2}$, which is $-D^{0\mathrm L}$ of . The ratio $v(\bq)/e^2=4\pi/q^2$ is the Coulomb kernel between unit charges, because $v$ includes the factor $e^2$ of two electrons.

The longitudinal photon propagator is therefore the instantaneous Coulomb interaction , rewritten for currents. Its $1/\omega^2$ is a mark of the temporal gauge, not of any dynamics: a static charge produces a constant $\bE=-\frac1c\partial_t\bA$, hence a longitudinal $\bA$ that grows linearly in time, whose Fourier transform has a double pole at $\omega=0$. The coupling of currents to the field carries a factor $1/c$ (Section B.2), and this cancels the $c$ in $D^{0\mathrm L}$: the energy that $D^{0\mathrm L}$ carries between two charge distributions is their Coulomb energy, whatever the speed of light.7

Physical picture: a photon that cannot travel

Each wavevector of the field has three polarizations, two transverse and one longitudinal. A transverse polarization has a magnetic field, which acts as a restoring force: it oscillates at $\omega=cq$ and propagates. These oscillations, quantized, are photons. The longitudinal polarization has no curl and no magnetic field, so nothing pulls it back: it neither oscillates nor propagates. Gauss's law ties it to the charges at every instant, and its field energy is the instantaneous Coulomb energy of the charges. "Instantaneous Coulomb interaction" and "retarded radiation" are two polarizations of the same field.

A second longitudinal propagator that vanishes

The EPP manuscript also derives a free longitudinal propagator equal to zero. That one is the propagator of the quantized longitudinal field when the Coulomb energy has been moved into the Hamiltonian as a separate, instantaneous term: the field alone then has no dynamics, and its propagator vanishes. Both statements hold, for different bookkeeping. Section B.4 shows how Gauss's law turns the longitudinal field energy into the Coulomb interaction, and Section B.5 and Section B.6 show that it re-enters the perturbation expansion exactly as $D^{0\mathrm L}$ of . Drawing both would count the Coulomb interaction twice.

The photon propagator in a medium

In a crystal the electrons and ions answer the field with currents of their own, and these currents produce further field. The photon propagator $D$ is the total vector potential per unit external current, with all of this response included. An external current $\bj^{\mathrm{ext}}$ is a probe that the experimenter controls, for example the current that drives an antenna, or a test current used in a calculation. For a homogeneous medium in Fourier space, $A_\alpha=\sum_\beta D_{\alpha\beta}\,j^{\mathrm{ext}}_\beta$. Quantum mechanically, $D$ is a correlation function of the field: the ground-state average of a product of two field operators taken at two points and two times, in the coupled system of electrons, ions and field:8

$$\begin{gathered}D_{\alpha\beta}(\br,\br';t-t')\\ =\frac{\ii}{\hbar c}\,\big\langle\mathcal T A_\alpha(\br,t)\,A_\beta(\br',t')\big\rangle .\end{gathered} \tag{X1.7}$$

$\langle\dots\rangle$

The prefactor $\ii/\hbar c$ makes $D$ the field per unit external current. An external current couples to the field through the energy $-\frac1c\int\dd^3r\,\bj^{\mathrm{ext}}\cdot\bA$, and first-order perturbation theory (the Kubo formula of the background box) gives $\delta\langle A_\alpha\rangle=\sum_\beta D^{\mathrm R}_{\alpha\beta}\,j^{\mathrm{ext}}_\beta$, where $D^{\mathrm R}$ is the retarded counterpart of . For positive frequencies at zero temperature the two coincide, and the time-ordered form is the one that many-body perturbation theory expands into diagrams.

Without matter, $D$ reduces to the free propagator $D^0$ of . Section B.5 derives the transverse part again from the quantized field and finds the same result; the longitudinal part enters there as the Coulomb interaction, as the caveat above explains.

The sign of the prefactor is opposite to that of the density response $\chi$$\,=-\frac{\ii}{\hbar}\langle\mathcal T\,n\,n\rangle$ of Part A (with the mean subtracted from the number density $n$), because an external potential energy $V^{\mathrm{ext}}$ couples to the density with the opposite sign, $+\int\dd^3r\,V^{\mathrm{ext}}n$.

Response functions, retarded and time ordered skip if familiar

Let a system with Hamiltonian $\hat H$ be perturbed by the energy $-\hat O'f(t)$, where $\hat O'$ is an operator and $f(t)$ a small external function (a force, a potential, a current). To first order in $f$, the change of the expectation value of an operator $\hat O$ is the Kubo formula

$$\delta\langle\hat O(t)\rangle=\frac{\ii}{\hbar}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\dd t'\,\theta(t-t')\,\big\langle[\hat O(t),\hat O'(t')]\big\rangle\,f(t'),$$

with operators in the Heisenberg picture of $\hat H$ and $\theta(t)$ the step function (1 for $t\gt0$, 0 otherwise). The kernel is the retarded response function. The step function enforces causality: the response at $t$ depends only on $f$ at earlier times. After Fourier transformation all its poles lie in the lower half of the complex $\omega$ plane, which is what the replacement $\omega\to\omega+\ii\eta$ produces. Two examples fix the prefactors used in this primer. A potential energy $V^{\mathrm{ext}}$ coupled to the electron density, the energy $\int V^{\mathrm{ext}}n$, is the case $\hat O'=-n$, so $\delta n=-\frac{\ii}{\hbar}\theta\langle[n,n]\rangle\,V^{\mathrm{ext}}$: the density response $\chi$ of Part A. An external current, the energy $-\frac1c\int\bj^{\mathrm{ext}}\cdot\bA$, is the case $\hat O'=A_\beta/c$, so $\delta A_\alpha=\frac{\ii}{\hbar c}\theta\langle[A_\alpha,A_\beta]\rangle\,j^{\mathrm{ext}}_\beta$: the photon propagator.

Diagrammatic perturbation theory works with time-ordered functions instead, $\langle\mathcal T\,\hat O(t)\hat O'(t')\rangle$, because Wick's theorem, which reduces averages of many field operators to products of pair averages, applies to them. For bosonic operators at zero temperature, the time-ordered and retarded functions are equal at positive frequencies and complex conjugates at negative ones. Both sources write time-ordered functions, with the infinitesimal as $\omega^2-\omega_0^2+\ii\eta$ for a resonance at $\omega_0$; this primer writes retarded ones, $(\omega+\ii\eta)^2-\omega_0^2$, which agree for $\omega\gt0$.

The photon self-energy

The medium answers the field with a current. For weak fields the induced current $\bj^{\mathrm{ind}}$ is linear in the field:

$$\begin{gathered}j^{\mathrm{ind}}_\alpha=\sum_\beta\Pi_{\alpha\beta}\,A_\beta,\\ \Pi=\Pi^{\mathrm e}+\Pi^{\mathrm{ion}} .\end{gathered} \tag{X1.8}$$
$A_\beta$
the total vector potential: external plus induced; every quantity is taken at the same $(\bq,\omega)$
$\Pi_{\alpha\beta}$
photon self-energy: current along $\alpha$ per unit total vector potential along $\beta$; units 1/(length × time)
$\Pi^{\mathrm e}$, $\Pi^{\mathrm{ion}}$
parts from the electrons and from the vibrating ions, whose Born-charge dipoles carry a current when they oscillate

$\Pi$ is the response to the total field. In diagrams this means that it is irreducible: it collects the processes that cannot be cut into two separate pieces by cutting a single photon line, because the pieces joined by one photon line are the response to the induced field, which the total field already contains. For the electrons it is a current–current correlation function plus a diamagnetic term, which comes from the dependence of the electron velocity on $\bA$ (Section B.5). The name self-energy is that of the irreducible part of any Dyson equation: $\Pi$ dresses the photon line as the electron self-energy $\Sigma$ dresses the electron line. It plays for currents the role that the irreducible polarizability $\chi^{\mathrm{irr}}$ plays for densities in Part A.9

The familiar response functions are special cases of $\Pi$. With $\bE=\frac{\ii\omega}c\bA$, Ohm's law $\bj=\boldsymbol\sigma\bE$ gives the conductivity $\sigma_{\alpha\beta}=-\frac{\ii c}{\omega}\Pi_{\alpha\beta}$. The induced current is the time derivative of an induced polarization, $\bj=\partial_t\bP=-\ii\omega\bP$, and the dielectric tensor is defined by $\boldsymbol\varepsilon\bE=\bE+4\pi\bP$. Together they give $\varepsilon_{\alpha\beta}=\delta_{\alpha\beta}+\frac{4\pi\ii}{\omega}\sigma_{\alpha\beta}$ and, at long wavelengths,

$$\varepsilon_{\alpha\beta}(\omega)=\delta_{\alpha\beta}+\frac{4\pi c}{\omega^2}\,\Pi_{\alpha\beta}(\omega) . \tag{X1.9}$$

An insulator below its gap, where the electrons give $\varepsilon=\varepsilon_\infty$, therefore has $\Pi^{\mathrm e}=(\varepsilon_\infty-1)\,\omega^2/4\pi c$: per unit vector potential the electrons answer with a current proportional to $\omega^2$, the time derivative of a polarization $(\varepsilon_\infty-1)\bE/4\pi$ that follows the field $\bE=\frac{\ii\omega}{c}\bA$. The vibrating ions add a resonance at the TO frequency (Section B.3).

Two Dyson equations, one structure

Both theories answer the same question. A source is placed in the medium: what is the total field? The medium responds to the total field, its response produces more field, the medium responds to that, and so on. Summing the chain gives a Dyson equation. The steps below derive both equations side by side; in each step Part A comes first and Part B second.

An isotropic medium: the two polarizations separate

In an isotropic medium every tensor is a combination of the two projectors, $X_{\alpha\beta}=X^{\mathrm L}\hat q_\alpha\hat q_\beta+X^{\mathrm T}(\delta_{\alpha\beta}-\hat q_\alpha\hat q_\beta)$, with scalar coefficients $X^{\mathrm L}$ and $X^{\mathrm T}$. The projectors multiply like numbers that are 0 or 1: each is its own square, and their product vanishes. The matrix equation therefore splits into two scalar equations, $D^{\mathrm L}=D^{0\mathrm L}+D^{0\mathrm L}\Pi^{\mathrm L}D^{\mathrm L}$ and the same with T, whose solutions are

$$\begin{gathered}D^{\mathrm L}=\frac{D^{0\mathrm L}}{\varepsilon^{\mathrm L}},\\ D^{\mathrm T}=-\frac{4\pi c}{\omega^2\varepsilon^{\mathrm T}-c^2q^2},\\ \varepsilon^{\mathrm L}=1+\frac{4\pi c}{\omega^2}\Pi^{\mathrm L},\\ \varepsilon^{\mathrm T}=1+\frac{4\pi c}{\omega^2}\Pi^{\mathrm T} .\end{gathered} \tag{X1.13}$$

This holds at any speed of light: in an isotropic medium the longitudinal photon line is $W$, exactly. Everything that is new in Part B sits in the transverse part. The modes of the medium are the poles of $D$. Longitudinal ones satisfy $\varepsilon^{\mathrm L}(\omega)=0$: plasmons (collective oscillations of the electron density), and the longitudinal optical (LO) phonons of Section A.4. Transverse ones satisfy $\omega^2\varepsilon^{\mathrm T}(\omega)=c^2q^2$: light in the medium, which mixes with the TO phonons into polaritons (Section B.6). For an insulator below its gap, $\varepsilon^{\mathrm L}=\varepsilon^{\mathrm T}=\varepsilon_\infty$, and gives the fields of the polarization wave with electrons: the resonance of moves to $cq=\sqrt{\varepsilon_\infty}\,\omega$.

Electrons first, then the lattice

The match between the two columns becomes line by line when the electrons are summed first. Split $\Pi=\Pi^{\mathrm e}+\Pi^{\mathrm{ion}}$ and define the propagator dressed by the electrons alone, $D^{\mathrm e}$:

$$\begin{gathered}D^{\mathrm e}=D^0+D^0\,\Pi^{\mathrm e}\,D^{\mathrm e},\\ D=D^{\mathrm e}+D^{\mathrm e}\,\Pi^{\mathrm{ion}}\,D .\end{gathered} \tag{X1.15}$$

The second line is exact. In inverse matrices reads $D^{-1}=(D^0)^{-1}-\Pi^{\mathrm e}-\Pi^{\mathrm{ion}}$, and the first line gives $(D^{\mathrm e})^{-1}=(D^0)^{-1}-\Pi^{\mathrm e}$, so $D^{-1}=(D^{\mathrm e})^{-1}-\Pi^{\mathrm{ion}}$; multiplying by $D^{\mathrm e}$ on the left and by $D$ on the right gives the second line. The two lines mirror Part A:13

Expanded, a photon dressed by electrons reaches a vibrating ion, which takes it up as a phonon; the phonon propagates and gives the photon back. The size of the light cone

The transverse field of a wave is comparable to the longitudinal one only when the wave pattern moves about as fast as light or faster, that is, when light needs about one period or longer to cross one wavelength. The boundary is the wavevector $q_{\mathrm{light}}$ of light of frequency $\omega$ in a medium of refractive index $\sqrt{\varepsilon_\infty}$, met in the field of a polarization wave. In terms of it, the transverse propagator over the longitudinal one reads

$$\begin{gathered}q_{\mathrm{light}}=\frac{\sqrt{\varepsilon_\infty}\,\omega}{c},\\ \left|\frac{D^{\mathrm T}}{D^{\mathrm L}}\right|=\frac{1}{\big|1-q^2/q_{\mathrm{light}}^2\big|} .\end{gathered} \tag{X1.16}$$
$q_{\mathrm{light}}$
radius of the light cone at frequency $\omega$ (Å⁻¹): the wavevector of light of that frequency in the medium
$|D^{\mathrm T}/D^{\mathrm L}|$
transverse over longitudinal field produced by a current of given size, from with $\varepsilon^{\mathrm L}=\varepsilon^{\mathrm T}=\varepsilon_\infty$ (an insulator below its gap)

The ratio is $\omega^2\varepsilon_\infty/|\omega^2\varepsilon_\infty-c^2q^2|$; dividing numerator and denominator by $\omega^2\varepsilon_\infty$ gives the form above. The time light takes to cross a wavelength $2\pi/q$ is $2\pi\sqrt{\varepsilon_\infty}/cq$, and the period is $2\pi/\omega$; their ratio is $q_{\mathrm{light}}/q$. The name of the light cone comes from the plane of wavevector and frequency. There it is the region $q\lt\sqrt{\varepsilon_\infty}\,\omega/c$, above the light line of the medium, $\omega=cq/\sqrt{\varepsilon_\infty}$; in the space of $\bq$ and $\omega$ it is the inside of the cone $|\bq|=\sqrt{\varepsilon_\infty}\,\omega/c$, and at a fixed frequency it is the inside of a sphere of radius $q_{\mathrm{light}}$ around $\bq=0$. Inside it the transverse and longitudinal fields are comparable. Outside it the transverse field falls off as $(q_{\mathrm{light}}/q)^2$. draws the cone, the Brillouin zone and the phonon branches of GaAs to scale on a logarithmic axis.

The order of limits: $\bq\to0$ against $c\to\infty$

Part A finds that the LO and TO frequencies of a polar crystal differ at Γ, and that the frequency of a polar mode as $\bq\to0$ depends on the direction from which $\bq$ approaches zero: a polarization along $\hat\bq$ feels the field of the bound charges, a polarization across it does not (Section A.4). The light cone shows what finite $c$ does to this. Factor $-4\pi c/\omega^2$ out of :

$$\begin{aligned}D^0_{\alpha\beta}=-\frac{4\pi c}{\omega^2}\Big[&\hat q_\alpha\hat q_\beta\\ &+\frac{\delta_{\alpha\beta}-\hat q_\alpha\hat q_\beta}{1-c^2q^2/\omega^2}\Big] .\end{aligned} \tag{X1.17}$$

Apart from the prefactor, $D^0$ depends on $\bq$ and $c$ only through the direction $\hat\bq$ and the ratio $cq/\omega$. Two limits that might seem interchangeable therefore give different tensors:

$$\begin{gathered}\bq\to0,\ c\ \text{fixed}:\ \ [\dots]\to\delta_{\alpha\beta},\\ c\to\infty,\ \bq\ \text{fixed}:\ \ [\dots]\to\hat q_\alpha\hat q_\beta .\end{gathered} \tag{X1.18}$$

Here $[\dots]$ is the bracket of . Taking $\bq\to0$ first, $cq/\omega\to0$: deep inside the light cone the field does not distinguish longitudinal from transverse currents, and the tensor is isotropic. Taking $c\to\infty$ first, $cq/\omega\to\infty$: only the longitudinal projector survives, which is the Coulomb line and all of Part A. Near the point $\bq=0$, $1/c=0$ the bracket has no single limit: along a path on which $cq/\omega$ tends to a fixed value, the bracket tends to its value at that ratio, so the result depends on the path and $D^0$ is not continuous there. The prefactor itself grows with $c$ because $D$ is defined per unit current; the physical interaction carries an extra $1/c$, and its longitudinal part stays the Coulomb interaction .15

For the polar modes of GaAs the two orders give:

  • $c\to\infty$ first (Part A, valid for $q\gg q_{\mathrm{light}}$). An LO wave feels the field $-4\pi\bP/\varepsilon_\infty$, a TO wave none, so the frequencies $\omega_{\mathrm{LO}}$ and $\omega_{\mathrm{TO}}$ obey $\omega_{\mathrm{LO}}^2-\omega_{\mathrm{TO}}^2=4\pi e^2Z^{*2}/(\OV M_{\mathrm r}\varepsilon_\infty)$, the LO–TO splitting of Section A.4. Here $Z^*$ is the scalar Born charge of a cubic two-atom crystal, $\OV$ the cell volume and $M_{\mathrm r}$ the reduced mass. With $Z^*=2.07$, the TO mode stays at 33.3 meV and the LO mode rises to 35.9 meV (measured: 36.2 meV).
  • $\bq\to0$ at finite $c$ (valid for $q\ll q_{\mathrm{light}}$). A TO wave feels the same field as an LO wave, so all three polar modes sit at 35.9 meV, whatever the direction of approach. The transverse ones are the $\bq\to0$ end of the upper phonon polariton (Section B.7).

The longitudinal frequency is the same in both orders; only the TO modes move. The crossover happens at $q\approx q_{\mathrm{light}}=5.6\times10^{-5}$ Å⁻¹, and already at $q=10\,q_{\mathrm{light}}$ the TO mode lies only about 0.03 meV below $\omega_{\mathrm{TO}}$. So the non-analytic behaviour of Part A at Γ, a limit that depends on the direction of approach, is real, but it describes the range $q_{\mathrm{light}}\ll q\ll$ zone size; finite $c$ smooths it out on the scale $q_{\mathrm{light}}$. Experiments see both regimes. Raman scattering of visible light in backscattering, where the light reverses direction and hands the crystal about twice its own wavevector, probes $q\approx10^{-2}$ Å⁻¹ and finds the split TO and LO of Part A. Infrared spectroscopy and near-forward Raman scattering probe $q\approx q_{\mathrm{light}}$ and find polaritons.

The EPP manuscript's own result at $\bq=0$ is the second limit with the electrons also left out: every polar mode then receives the unscreened shift and lands at 55.6 meV. The difference from 35.9 meV is electronic screening, not the order of limits.16 With electrons, for $D^{\mathrm e}$ gives $D^{\mathrm e}_{\alpha\beta}\to-\frac{c}{\omega^2}q_\alpha q_\beta W^{\mathrm e}/e^2$ as $c\to\infty$: the only survivor is the electron-screened Coulomb interaction, and Section B.9 recovers Part A from it term by term.

Check yourself: three short questions

  1. Visible-light Raman backscattering in GaAs probes TO phonons with $q\approx10^{-2}$ Å⁻¹. How large is their transverse field compared with the longitudinal field of an LO phonon of the same polarization, and which frequencies does the experiment see?

    Answer. $|D^{\mathrm T}/D^{\mathrm L}|\simeq(q_{\mathrm{light}}/q)^2=(5.6\times10^{-5}/10^{-2})^2=3\times10^{-5}$. The TO mode feels no field to this accuracy, so the experiment sees the split pair of Part A: TO at 33.3 meV and LO at 36.2 meV.

  2. Which part of the free photon propagator survives as $c\to\infty$, and why does the Fröhlich coupling of LO phonons not change at finite $c$?

    Answer. The longitudinal part, $D^{0\mathrm L}\hat q_\alpha\hat q_\beta$, which is the Coulomb interaction rewritten for currents . The LO phonon couples to electrons through its longitudinal field, carried by $D^{\mathrm L}$, and in an isotropic medium $D^{\mathrm L}$ is $W$ at every $c$ ; the $c$ in its prefactor cancels against the $1/c$ of the coupling to currents.

  3. The density response $\chi^{\mathrm{irr}}$ is a scalar, the photon self-energy $\Pi$ a $3\times3$ tensor. Which part of $\Pi$ contains the same information as $\chi^{\mathrm{irr}}$, and what is the rest?

    Answer. The longitudinal projection $\sum_{\alpha\beta}q_\alpha\Pi_{\alpha\beta}q_\beta$, fixed by $\chi^{\mathrm{irr}}$ through charge conservation, . The rest describes currents that carry no charge (transverse currents) and mixed longitudinal–transverse responses: the response to magnetic and radiative fields, which the Coulomb picture never needs.
