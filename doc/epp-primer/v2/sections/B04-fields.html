Quantized fields: transverse photons and the Coulomb interaction

Section B.2 wrote one Lagrangian for electrons, ions and the vector potential. This section turns it into a quantum Hamiltonian. Each plane wave of the field has three polarizations. The two perpendicular to the wavevector are oscillators, and their quanta are photons. The third has no restoring force: Gauss's law ties it to the charges, and its energy is exactly the instantaneous Coulomb interaction that Part A takes as given. The section ends with the split $H=H_0+V'$ on which every diagram of Part B is built.

The picture: three polarizations, two photons

In Part A every interaction is the instantaneous Coulomb interaction, written into the Hamiltonian from the start (Section A.5). Part B starts instead from a Lagrangian in which charges never act on each other directly: electrons and ions act on the electromagnetic field, and the field acts back on them. The two descriptions must agree whenever the speed of light can be taken as infinite. This section shows how the Coulomb interaction comes out of the field, and what the field contains besides it.

The key is a single plane wave. Expand the vector potential $\bA$ in plane waves $\ee^{\ii\bq\cdot\br}$. For each wavevector $\bq$ the amplitude is a vector with three components: two perpendicular to $\bq$, the transverse components, and one along $\bq$, the longitudinal component.

  • A transverse wave has a curl, so it carries a magnetic field. The magnetic energy grows as the square of the amplitude, like the energy of a stretched spring, and the electric field supplies the inertia. Each transverse component is therefore an oscillator. Its frequency is $\omega=cq$, and its quanta are photons.
  • A longitudinal wave has no curl: no magnetic field, no spring. Its electric field points along $\bq$ and has a divergence, which Gauss's law ties to the charge density at the same instant. The longitudinal field therefore has no motion of its own. It follows the charges, and its energy is their Coulomb energy.

The plan of the section follows this picture:

  1. derive Maxwell's equations from the field part of the Lagrangian, where Gauss's law appears as a constraint rather than an equation of motion;
  2. pass from the Lagrangian to the Hamiltonian, in which electrons and ions couple to the field in four ways;
  3. write the field energy mode by mode, in a basis of two transverse polarizations and one longitudinal;
  4. quantize the transverse modes, which gives photons;
  5. show that the longitudinal energy is the Coulomb interaction of all charges;
  6. split the Hamiltonian into a free part $H_0$ and an interaction $V'$.

Maxwell's equations from the field Lagrangian

The Lagrangian, collected around the field

Section B.2 built the Lagrangian from three ingredients. The valence electrons are point particles labelled $s$, with positions $\br_s$ and velocities $\mathbf v_s=\dot\br_s$, in the potential energy $V^{\mathrm{en}}_0(\br)=\sum_{\ell\kappa}V_\kappa(\br-\btau_{\ell\kappa})$ of the static crystal, where $V_\kappa$ is the potential of one ion and every ion sits at its equilibrium site $\btau_{\ell\kappa}$. The ions, of mass $M_\kappa$, are displaced by $\bu_{\ell\kappa}$ and held by the short-range force constants $\Phi^{\mathrm{sr}}$; the long-range dipole–dipole part of the force constants is left out because the field regenerates it, as the Coulomb energy below will show. The field is the vector potential $\bA$ in the temporal gauge, where the scalar potential is zero, $\bE=-\frac1c\partial_t\bA$ and $\bB=\nabla\times\bA$, with $c$ the speed of light. Collected so that every term containing $\bA$ sits in one Lagrangian density $\mathcal L$, the Lagrangian reads2

$$\begin{aligned}L={}&\sum_s\Big[\frac{m_{\mathrm e}}{2}\,\mathbf v_s^2-V^{\mathrm{en}}_0(\br_s)\Big]\\&+\sum_{\ell\kappa\alpha}\frac{M_\kappa}{2}\,\dot u_{\ell\kappa\alpha}^2-U^{\mathrm{sr}}\\&+\int\dd^3r\,\mathcal L(\br),\\ U^{\mathrm{sr}}={}&\frac12\sum\Phi^{\mathrm{sr}}_{\ell\kappa\alpha,\ell'\kappa'\beta}\,u_{\ell\kappa\alpha}\,u_{\ell'\kappa'\beta},\end{aligned} \tag{B4.1}$$
$$\begin{aligned}\mathcal L={}&\frac{1}{8\pi c^2}\,(\partial_t\bA)^2-\frac{1}{8\pi}\big(\nabla\times\bA\big)^2\\&+\frac1c\,\big(\bj^{\mathrm e}+\partial_t\bP\big)\cdot\bA .\end{aligned} \tag{B4.2}$$

The first two terms of $\mathcal L$ are the field's own Lagrangian density, $(\bE^2-\bB^2)/8\pi$. The last term couples the field to the total current, that of the electrons and the polarization current $\partial_t\bP$ of the vibrating dipoles. Two features of the couplings matter below. Both are linear in a velocity, $\mathbf v_s$ or $\dot\bu_{\ell\kappa}$. And neither contains $\partial_t\bA$. Section B.2 shows that, up to a total time derivative, the ionic coupling equals $\int\dd^3r\,\bP\cdot\bE$, minus the potential energy $-\int\dd^3r\,\bP\cdot\bE$ of the dipoles in the field; the form with $\partial_t\bP$ is used because it keeps $\partial_t\bA$ out of the couplings.

Ampère's law with two sources

A field as a mechanical system skip if familiar

Cut space into small cells of volume $\Delta V$, centred at points $\br_c$. The values $A_\alpha(\br_c)$, one for each cell and direction, are then ordinary coordinates, and the Lagrangian is a sum over cells, $\sum_c\Delta V\,\mathcal L(\br_c)$, in which a derivative $\partial_\beta A_\alpha$ is a difference between neighbouring cells. Lagrange's equation for the coordinate $A_\alpha(\br_c)$, divided by $\Delta V$, has three kinds of terms: the derivative of $\mathcal L$ with respect to $A_\alpha$; minus the time derivative of $\partial\mathcal L/\partial(\partial_tA_\alpha)$; and, because $A_\alpha(\br_c)$ also enters the differences formed in the neighbouring cells, minus the difference of $\partial\mathcal L/\partial(\partial_\beta A_\alpha)$ across cell $c$, which becomes a derivative as the cells shrink. This gives the Euler–Lagrange equation of a field,

$$\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial A_\alpha}-\partial_t\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial(\partial_tA_\alpha)}-\sum_\beta\partial_\beta\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial(\partial_\beta A_\alpha)}=0 .$$

The momentum conjugate to the coordinate $A_\alpha(\br_c)$ is $\Delta V\,\partial\mathcal L/\partial(\partial_tA_\alpha)$, so $\partial\mathcal L/\partial(\partial_tA_\alpha)$ is a momentum per unit volume, a momentum density. The Hamiltonian, the sum of momentum times velocity over all coordinates minus $L$, becomes an integral of momentum density times $\partial_tA_\alpha$, minus $L$. Quantization makes each coordinate and its own momentum obey the commutator $\ii\hbar$, and different pairs commute: the commutator of $\hat A_\alpha(\br_c)$ with $\Delta V$ times the momentum density in cell $c'$ along $\beta$ is $\ii\hbar\,\delta_{cc'}\delta_{\alpha\beta}$. Dividing by $\Delta V$ and letting the cells shrink turns $\delta_{cc'}/\Delta V$ into the delta function $\delta(\br-\br')$. So a field and its momentum density commute to $\ii\hbar\,\delta_{\alpha\beta}\,\delta(\br-\br')$.

The field has one coordinate $A_\alpha(\br)$ for every point $\br$ and direction $\alpha$. Its equation of motion is the Euler–Lagrange equation of a field, derived in the box above:

$$\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial A_\alpha}-\partial_t\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial(\partial_tA_\alpha)}-\sum_\beta\partial_\beta\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial(\partial_\beta A_\alpha)}=0,$$

where $\partial_\beta=\partial/\partial r_\beta$ and $r_\beta$ are the Cartesian components of $\br$. Only $\mathcal L$ contains the field, so the rest of $L$ drops out. Term by term:

  1. $\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial A_\alpha}=\frac1c\big(j^{\mathrm e}_\alpha+\partial_tP_\alpha\big)$.

    The field itself, without derivatives, appears only in the coupling.

  2. $\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial(\partial_tA_\alpha)}=\frac{\partial_tA_\alpha}{4\pi c^2}=-\frac{E_\alpha}{4\pi c}$, so the second term of the equation is $+\frac{1}{4\pi c}\,\partial_tE_\alpha$.

    Only the electric term contains $\partial_t\bA$, and $\bE=-\frac1c\partial_t\bA$.

  3. The magnetic term. Since $(\nabla\times\bA)^2=\sum_{\alpha\beta}\big[(\partial_\beta A_\alpha)^2-\partial_\beta A_\alpha\,\partial_\alpha A_\beta\big]$,

    $$\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial(\partial_\beta A_\alpha)}=-\frac1{4\pi}\big(\partial_\beta A_\alpha-\partial_\alpha A_\beta\big).$$
    Write out one component, $(\nabla\times\bA)_z=\partial_xA_y-\partial_yA_x$. Squaring and adding the three components gives every $(\partial_\beta A_\alpha)^2$ with $\alpha\neq\beta$ once and every cross product such as $-2\,\partial_xA_y\,\partial_yA_x$ once, which is what the double sum counts; its terms with $\alpha=\beta$ cancel. The derivative of the second sum picks up two equal terms, $\partial_\alpha A_\beta$ from each factor.

  4. The third term of the Euler–Lagrange equation is then

    $$\begin{gathered}-\sum_\beta\partial_\beta\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial(\partial_\beta A_\alpha)}=\frac1{4\pi}\big[\nabla^2A_\alpha-\partial_\alpha(\nabla\cdot\bA)\big]\\ =-\frac1{4\pi}\big[\nabla\times\bB\big]_\alpha .\end{gathered}$$
    The vector identity $\nabla\times(\nabla\times\bA)=\nabla(\nabla\cdot\bA)-\nabla^2\bA$, with $\bB=\nabla\times\bA$.

  5. Add the three terms, set the sum to zero and multiply by $4\pi$:

    $$\begin{gathered}\nabla\times\bB=\frac1c\,\partial_t\bE\\ +\frac{4\pi}{c}\big(\bj^{\mathrm e}+\partial_t\bP\big). \end{gathered}\tag{B4.3}$$
    This is the Ampère–Maxwell law. Its term $\frac1c\partial_t\bE$ is Maxwell's displacement current.3

The law has two sources: the current of the electrons and the polarization current $\partial_t\bP$ of the ions. The combination $\bE+4\pi\bP$ is the displacement field of macroscopic electrodynamics, with only the ions counted as a polarizable medium. The valence electrons enter as free charges and currents; their own polarization reappears in Section B.5 as the electronic photon self-energy $\Pi^{\mathrm e}$.

Gauss's law: a constraint

The Euler–Lagrange equations of $\bA$ give Ampère's law and nothing else. Gauss's law follows from it, as Section B.2 previewed, in four steps:

  1. Take the divergence of : $\ 0=\frac1c\,\partial_t\nabla\cdot\bE+\frac{4\pi}c\big(\nabla\cdot\bj^{\mathrm e}+\partial_t\nabla\cdot\bP\big)$.

    The divergence of a curl vanishes.

  2. The electrons conserve charge: $\partial_t\rho^{\mathrm e}+\nabla\cdot\bj^{\mathrm e}=0$, with the charge density $\rho^{\mathrm e}=-e\,n_{\mathrm e}$ and the number density $n_{\mathrm e}$$(\br)=\sum_s\delta(\br-\br_s)$.

    Each electron carries its charge along its path: $\partial_t\delta\big(\br-\br_s(t)\big)=-\mathbf v_s\cdot\nabla\delta(\br-\br_s)$, which is $-\nabla\cdot\big[\mathbf v_s\,\delta(\br-\br_s)\big]$ because $\mathbf v_s$ does not depend on $\br$.

  3. Insert step 2 into step 1 and multiply by $c$: $\ \partial_t\big[\nabla\cdot\bE-4\pi(\rho^{\mathrm e}+\rho^{\mathrm{ion}})\big]=0$, with $\rho^{\mathrm{ion}}=-\nabla\cdot\bP$.

    $-\nabla\cdot\bP$ is the bound charge of the dipoles: where the polarization is not uniform, the ends of neighbouring dipoles no longer cancel.

  4. The bracket keeps its initial value for ever. Gauss's law states that this value is zero:

    $$\begin{gathered}\nabla\cdot\bE=4\pi\big(\rho^{\mathrm e}+\rho^{\mathrm{ion}}\big),\\ \rho^{\mathrm e}=-e\,n_{\mathrm e},\\ \rho^{\mathrm{ion}}=-\nabla\cdot\bP .\end{gathered} \tag{B4.4}$$
    A nonzero value would be a static charge that belongs to nothing in the model.4

Gauss's law is therefore not an equation of motion. It is a condition on the initial state, which the dynamics then preserves. The reason is the gauge. With a scalar potential in the Lagrangian, Gauss's law is the Euler–Lagrange equation of that potential; the temporal gauge sets the scalar potential to zero and loses that equation, so the law must be imposed by hand. Nothing physical is lost, and this constraint is what will turn the longitudinal field into the Coulomb interaction.

The other two Maxwell equations need no derivation, because they are identities of the potentials: $\nabla\cdot(\nabla\times\bA)=0$, and $\nabla\times\bE=-\frac1c\nabla\times\partial_t\bA=-\frac1c\partial_t\bB$. The full set reads5

$$\begin{gathered}\nabla\cdot\bB=0,\qquad \nabla\times\bE=-\frac1c\,\partial_t\bB,\\ \nabla\cdot\bE=4\pi\rho,\\ \nabla\times\bB=\frac1c\,\partial_t\bE+\frac{4\pi}{c}\,\bj,\\ \rho=\rho^{\mathrm e}+\rho^{\mathrm{ion}},\qquad \bj=\bj^{\mathrm e}+\bj^{\mathrm{ion}} .\end{gathered} \tag{B4.5}$$
$\rho$; $\rho^{\mathrm e}$, $\rho^{\mathrm{ion}}$
charge density ($\rho$) and its electronic and ionic parts,
$\bj$; $\bj^{\mathrm{ion}}=\partial_t\bP$
current density and its ionic part, the polarization current

The ionic parts conserve charge identically, $\partial_t\rho^{\mathrm{ion}}+\nabla\cdot\bj^{\mathrm{ion}}=-\nabla\cdot\partial_t\bP+\nabla\cdot\partial_t\bP=0$. The division of labour is that of a polar insulator seen by an electrodynamicist: the ions are a polarizable background, with bound charge and polarization current, and the electrons are free charges.

Gauss's law in the quantum theory

Once the fields are operators, Gauss's law cannot hold as an operator identity: in the temporal gauge $\hat\bA$ and $\hat\bE$ obey canonical commutation relations, below, which a fixed relation between $\nabla\cdot\hat\bE$ and the charges would contradict. Instead, the operator $\nabla\cdot\hat\bE-4\pi\hat\rho$ commutes with the Hamiltonian, and it generates the gauge transformations that the temporal gauge leaves free: adding to $\hat\bA$ the gradient of a time-independent function, together with the matching change of phase of the electrons and shift of the ions' canonical momenta. Physical states are those it annihilates, which are the gauge-invariant states. On them Gauss's law holds, and it may be used inside the Hamiltonian, as is done below. The Coulomb gauge, $\nabla\cdot\bA=0$, reaches the same physics by another route: it removes the longitudinal part of $\bA$ from the start and writes the energy of the longitudinal field directly as the Coulomb interaction.

The Hamiltonian: four minimal couplings

A Hamiltonian is a function of coordinates and of the momenta conjugate to them. Here there are three kinds of coordinates: the electron positions $\br_s$, the ion displacements $u_{\ell\kappa\beta}$, and the field values $A_\alpha(\br)$.

Three momenta

Each momentum is the derivative of $L$ with respect to the matching velocity:6

  1. Electron $s$:

    $$\begin{gathered}\mathbf p_s=\frac{\partial L}{\partial\mathbf v_s}\\ =m_{\mathrm e}\mathbf v_s-\frac ec\,\bA(\br_s). \end{gathered}\tag{B4.6}$$
    The velocity appears in the kinetic energy and in the coupling, which for the electrons is $\frac1c\int\bj^{\mathrm e}\cdot\bA=-\frac ec\sum_s\mathbf v_s\cdot\bA(\br_s)$. The canonical momentum is not $m_{\mathrm e}\mathbf v_s$: the field adds the charge times $\bA$ over $c$ (Section B.2).

  2. Ion $\ell\kappa$:

    $$\begin{gathered}\bp_{\ell\kappa}=\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot\bu_{\ell\kappa}}=M_\kappa\dot\bu_{\ell\kappa}\\ +\frac ec\,\mathbf Z^{*\mathsf T}_\kappa\cdot\bA(\btau_{\ell\kappa}). \end{gathered}\tag{B4.7}$$
    The coupling $\frac1c\int\partial_t\bP\cdot\bA=\frac ec\sum_{\ell\kappa}(\mathbf Z^*_\kappa\cdot\dot\bu_{\ell\kappa})\cdot\bA(\btau_{\ell\kappa})$ is linear in the ion velocity; in components, $p_{\ell\kappa\beta}=M_\kappa\dot u_{\ell\kappa\beta}+\frac ec\sum_\alpha Z^*_{\kappa,\alpha\beta}A_\alpha(\btau_{\ell\kappa})$. For its displacements the ion behaves as a particle whose charge is the Born tensor $e\mathbf Z^*_\kappa$, and it feels the field at its equilibrium site.

  3. Field at $\br$, direction $\alpha$:

    $$\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial(\partial_tA_\alpha)}=\frac{\partial_tA_\alpha}{4\pi c^2}=-\frac{E_\alpha}{4\pi c}. \tag{B4.8}$$
    Only the first term of contains $\partial_t\bA$. The momentum density conjugate to the vector potential is minus the electric field over $4\pi c$, the longitudinal part of $\bE$ included.

The Hamiltonian is the Legendre transform of the Lagrangian, built in three steps:

  1. Form the sum of momentum times velocity, minus $L$:

    $$\begin{aligned}H={}&\sum_s\mathbf p_s\cdot\mathbf v_s+\sum_{\ell\kappa}\bp_{\ell\kappa}\cdot\dot\bu_{\ell\kappa}\\&+\int\dd^3r\,\Big(-\frac{\bE}{4\pi c}\Big)\cdot\partial_t\bA-L .\end{aligned}$$
    One product of momentum and velocity per coordinate, minus $L$. For the field the sum over coordinates is an integral over points, with the momentum density of .

  2. The couplings cancel.

    They are linear in the velocities. A term $\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf f$ in $L$ adds $\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf f$ to $H$ through the product $\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf v$ and removes it again through $-L$. No energy is lost: the work that the field does on the charges reappears as a change of the field energy, which the next step keeps.

  3. What remains: each kinetic energy keeps its sign, each potential energy changes sign, and the field gives $\frac{\bE^2}{4\pi}-\frac{\bE^2-\bB^2}{8\pi}=\frac{\bE^2+\bB^2}{8\pi}$.

    $\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf v$ supplies $m_{\mathrm e}\mathbf v^2$, twice the kinetic energy, of which $L$ removes one half. For the field, $(-\bE/4\pi c)\cdot\partial_t\bA=\bE^2/4\pi$ because $\partial_t\bA=-c\bE$.

The result is the total energy:7

$$\begin{aligned}H={}&\sum_s\Big[\frac{m_{\mathrm e}}2\,\mathbf v_s^2+V^{\mathrm{en}}_0(\br_s)\Big]\\&+\sum_{\ell\kappa}\frac{M_\kappa}2\,\dot\bu_{\ell\kappa}^2+U^{\mathrm{sr}}\\&+\frac{1}{8\pi}\int\dd^3r\,\big(\bE^2+\bB^2\big).\end{aligned} \tag{B4.9}$$

Kinetic, potential and field energy, and no interaction term at all. The coupling to the field is hidden in the relation between velocities and momenta, and the Coulomb interaction, as shown below, is hidden in $\int\bE^2$. A Hamiltonian must be written with momenta, not velocities. Eliminating the velocities with –, that is $m_{\mathrm e}\mathbf v_s=\mathbf p_s+\frac ec\bA(\br_s)$ and $M_\kappa\dot\bu_{\ell\kappa}=\bp_{\ell\kappa}-\frac ec\mathbf Z^{*\mathsf T}_\kappa\cdot\bA(\btau_{\ell\kappa})$, gives

$$\begin{aligned}H={}&\sum_s\frac{1}{2m_{\mathrm e}}\Big(\mathbf p_s+\frac ec\,\bA(\br_s)\Big)^2\\&+\sum_sV^{\mathrm{en}}_0(\br_s)+U^{\mathrm{sr}}\\&+\sum_{\ell\kappa}\frac{1}{2M_\kappa}\Big(\bp_{\ell\kappa}\\&\qquad-\frac ec\,\mathbf Z^{*\mathsf T}_\kappa\cdot\bA(\btau_{\ell\kappa})\Big)^2\\&+\frac{1}{8\pi}\int\dd^3r\,\big[\bE^2+(\nabla\times\bA)^2\big],\end{aligned} \tag{B4.10}$$

with $\bE=-4\pi c$ times the field momentum density. This is minimal coupling: every canonical momentum enters shifted by its charge times $\bA$ over $c$, $-e$ for an electron and the tensor $e\mathbf Z^*_\kappa$ for a displaced ion. The shifted combinations are the kinetic momenta, mass times velocity.

Every charged species couples twice: linearly through its current, and quadratically through a contact term in $\bA^2$. The linear terms are called paramagnetic and the quadratic ones diamagnetic, after their roles in the magnetic response of atoms. For the ions the charge is the Born tensor, and the current is $e\mathbf Z^*_\kappa\cdot\bp_{\ell\kappa}/M_\kappa$, which to lowest order in the field is the rate of change of the dipole, $e\mathbf Z^*_\kappa\cdot\dot\bu_{\ell\kappa}$. Two details matter in quantum mechanics. The electronic term is symmetrized, because $\mathbf p_s=-\ii\hbar\nabla_s$, with $\nabla_s$ the gradient with respect to $\br_s$, does not commute with $\bA(\br_s)$: $\mathbf p_s\cdot\bA-\bA\cdot\mathbf p_s=-\ii\hbar\,\nabla\cdot\bA$, which is not zero in the temporal gauge. The ionic momentum, in contrast, commutes with $\bA(\btau_{\ell\kappa})$, because the field is taken at the fixed equilibrium site and not at the moving ion. Finally, the ionic $\bA^2$ term contains no ion coordinate at all: it is a contact between two photons at a site, not a two-phonon term of the Debye–Waller kind. draws each coupling as a vertex.

One Fourier mode, three polarizations

The field energy $H^{\mathrm{field}}$ couples neighbouring points through the derivatives in $\bB=\nabla\times\bA$, just as force constants couple neighbouring atoms. Plane waves remove the coupling, as they did for the lattice in Section A.1. As in Section B.2, the field in the Born–von Kármán supercell of volume $\OVSC$ is a sum of plane waves:9

$$\begin{gathered}\bA(\br)=\sum_\bq\bA_\bq\,\ee^{\ii\bq\cdot\br},\\ \bA_\bq=\frac{1}{\OVSC}\int\dd^3r\,\ee^{-\ii\bq\cdot\br}\bA(\br),\\ \bA_{-\bq}=\bA^*_\bq ,\end{gathered} \tag{B4.12}$$

and likewise for $\bE$, $\bB$ and the charge density $\rho$, with amplitudes $\bE_\bq$, $\bB_\bq$ and $\rho_\bq$. The wavevectors are those allowed by the Born–von Kármán condition, $\bq=\sum_j(s_j/N_j)\bb_j$, but now with all integers $s_j$. A lattice wave lives on the atoms, so its wavevectors fold into the first Brillouin zone. The field lives everywhere in space, so it needs every wavevector: equivalently, every $\bq+\bG$ with $\bq$ in the zone. The amplitudes at $\bq$ and $-\bq$ are complex conjugates because $\bA(\br)$ is real. Derivatives become products, $\nabla\times\bA\to\ii\bq\times\bA_\bq$ and $\nabla\cdot\bA\to\ii\bq\cdot\bA_\bq$.

Section B.2 split each amplitude into its part along $\hat\bq=\bq/q$, the longitudinal part $\bA^{\mathrm L}_\bq=\hat\bq\,(\hat\bq\cdot\bA_\bq)$, and the transverse rest, $\bA^{\mathrm T}_\bq=\bA_\bq-\bA^{\mathrm L}_\bq$, and likewise for $\bE$. Summed back into real space, these give a longitudinal field $\bA^{\mathrm L}(\br)$ with no curl ($\bq\times\bA^{\mathrm L}_\bq=0$) and a transverse field $\bA^{\mathrm T}(\br)$ with no divergence ($\bq\cdot\bA^{\mathrm T}_\bq=0$). Every vector field splits in this way, uniquely except for its uniform part $\bq=0$, which has no direction $\hat\bq$; this is the Helmholtz decomposition. Two consequences carry the whole section. The magnetic field comes only from the transverse part, $\bB=\nabla\times\bA^{\mathrm T}$. The charge in Gauss's law sees only the longitudinal part, $\nabla\cdot\bE=\nabla\cdot\bE^{\mathrm L}$. shows the two parts of one Fourier mode.

The polarization triad

To make the split explicit, give each $\bq$ three orthonormal vectors, the polarization triad: two perpendicular to $\bq$ and one along it.

$$\begin{gathered}\be_3(\bq)=\hat\bq,\\ \be_1(\bq),\ \be_2(\bq)\perp\bq,\\ \be_\lambda(\bq)\cdot\be_{\lambda'}(\bq)=\delta_{\lambda\lambda'},\\ \sum_{\lambda=1}^{3}e_{\lambda\alpha}(\bq)\,e_{\lambda\beta}(\bq)=\delta_{\alpha\beta}.\end{gathered} \tag{B4.13}$$

The first two lines fix the directions, the third makes the vectors orthonormal, and the fourth, completeness, says that together they span all of space. Dropping $\lambda=3$ from the sum leaves the transverse projector $\delta_{\alpha\beta}-\hat q_\alpha\hat q_\beta$. The components of the amplitudes in the triad are

$$A_{\bq\lambda}=\be_\lambda(\bq)\cdot\bA_\bq,\qquad E_{\bq\lambda}=\be_\lambda(\bq)\cdot\bE_\bq,\qquad \bA_\bq=\sum_{\lambda=1}^3\be_\lambda(\bq)\,A_{\bq\lambda},$$

so that $\bA^{\mathrm T}_\bq=\sum_{\lambda=1,2}\be_\lambda A_{\bq\lambda}$ and $\bA^{\mathrm L}_\bq=\hat\bq\,A_{\bq3}$. One convention remains: how the triad at $-\bq$ relates to the triad at $\bq$. For the quantized field to be Hermitian, the transverse vectors must satisfy $\be^*_\lambda(-\bq)=\be_\lambda(\bq)$, $\lambda=1,2$. We take them real and the same at $\bq$ and $-\bq$. This is possible because a vector perpendicular to $\bq$ is also perpendicular to $-\bq$: choose the pair for every $\bq$ in one half of reciprocal space and copy it to the other half. Then $A_{-\bq\lambda}=A^*_{\bq\lambda}$ for $\lambda=1,2$. With this convention the triad is right-handed in one half of reciprocal space and left-handed in the other, since $\be_1$ and $\be_2$ are copied while $\be_3$ changes sign; the handedness makes no difference.10

Transverse modes are photons

Each transverse term of is a harmonic oscillator with "mass" $\OVSC/4\pi c^2$ and spring constant $\OVSC q^2/4\pi$: the electric term is the squared momentum, $(\OVSC)^2|E_{\bq\lambda}|^2/16\pi^2c^2$, divided by twice the mass, and the magnetic term is half the spring constant times $|A_{\bq\lambda}|^2$. Its frequency is $\sqrt{\text{spring}/\text{mass}}=cq$, and its zero-point amplitude, $(\hbar/2\cdot\text{mass}\cdot\text{frequency})^{1/2}$, is $(2\pi\hbar c^2/\OVSC\omega_q)^{1/2}$. Derivation: why the longitudinal pair cannot simply be dropped

The longitudinal coordinate carries no photons, so one might discard it and keep only $\hat\bA^{\mathrm T}$. The commutator forbids this. Insert the expansions of $\hat\bA$ and $\hat\bE$ into it, mode by mode with :

  1. The commutator becomes a sum over modes:

    $$\begin{gathered}\big[\hat A_\alpha(\br),\hat E_\beta(\br')\big]=-\frac{4\pi\ii\hbar c}{\OVSC}\\ \times\sum_\bq\Big[\sum_{\lambda=1}^3e_{\lambda\alpha}(\bq)\,e_{\lambda\beta}(\bq)\Big]\ee^{\ii\bq\cdot(\br-\br')} .\end{gathered}$$
    Only pairs with $\bq'=-\bq$ and $\lambda'=\lambda$ survive, and each brings the product of the two polarization components.

  2. With all three polarizations, completeness makes the bracket $\delta_{\alpha\beta}$, and $\frac{1}{\OVSC}\sum_\bq\ee^{\ii\bq\cdot(\br-\br')}=\delta(\br-\br')$: the commutator is local, as it must be.

    The sum runs over all wavevectors of the supercell grid, not only the first zone, so it builds the full delta function.

  3. With $\lambda=1,2$ only, the bracket is $\delta_{\alpha\beta}-\hat q_\alpha\hat q_\beta$, and the sum gives the transverse delta function:

    $$\begin{gathered}\delta^{\mathrm T}_{\alpha\beta}(\br)=\tfrac23\,\delta_{\alpha\beta}\,\delta(\br)-\frac{\delta_{\alpha\beta}-3\hat r_\alpha\hat r_\beta}{4\pi r^3},\\ \delta^{\mathrm L}_{\alpha\beta}(\br)=\delta_{\alpha\beta}\delta(\br)-\delta^{\mathrm T}_{\alpha\beta}(\br)=-\partial_\alpha\partial_\beta\frac{1}{4\pi r}.\end{gathered}$$
    $\int\frac{\dd^3q}{(2\pi)^3}\frac{4\pi}{q^2}\ee^{\ii\bq\cdot\br}=\frac1r$ (Poisson's equation, $\nabla^2\frac1r=-4\pi\delta(\br)$), so the longitudinal part $\int\frac{\dd^3q}{(2\pi)^3}\hat q_\alpha\hat q_\beta\ee^{\ii\bq\cdot\br}$ is $-\partial_\alpha\partial_\beta\frac{1}{4\pi r}$. Differentiating twice gives the dipolar term and a contact term $\frac13\delta_{\alpha\beta}\delta(\br)$. Here $r=|\br|$ and $\hat r_\alpha=r_\alpha/r$.

The transverse delta function has a tail falling as $1/r^3$: a field built from transverse modes alone would make $\hat\bA$ at one point fail to commute with $\hat\bE$ far away. Only the longitudinal part cancels that tail. So in the temporal gauge the longitudinal pair must stay in the theory as operators, although it is not an oscillator and carries no quanta.13 The same tensor $-\partial_\alpha\partial_\beta\frac1r$ returns below as the interaction between two ionic dipoles.

The longitudinal mode is the Coulomb interaction

The longitudinal pair has no oscillator to quantize. What fixes it is Gauss's law. In Fourier space, $\nabla\cdot\bE=4\pi\rho$ reads $\ii\bq\cdot\bE_\bq=4\pi\rho_\bq$. Only the longitudinal component has a divergence, $\ii\bq\cdot\bE_\bq=\ii q\,E_{\bq3}$, so, as Section B.2 found,

$$E_{\bq3}=-\frac{4\pi\ii}{q}\,\rho_\bq .$$

The longitudinal electric field is a function of the charge density at the same instant; it has no motion of its own. It is the field that a static charge distribution produces: a static field has no curl, so it is purely longitudinal, and the energy stored in it is the familiar electrostatic energy of the charges. shows where that energy sits for two charges.

From field energy to Coulomb energy

Start from the longitudinal part of and use Gauss's law:

  1. Insert the longitudinal field:

    $$H^{\mathrm L}=\frac{\OVSC}{8\pi}\sum_\bq|E_{\bq3}|^2=\frac{\OVSC}{2}\sum_{\bq\neq0}\frac{4\pi}{q^2}\,\rho^*_\bq\,\rho_\bq .$$

  2. Return to real space:

    $$\rho^*_\bq\rho_\bq=\frac{1}{(\OVSC)^2}\int\dd^3r\int\dd^3r'\,\rho(\br)\,\rho(\br')\,\ee^{\ii\bq\cdot(\br-\br')} .$$
    The definition of $\rho_\bq$, as in , used once for each factor; $\rho(\br)$ is real.

  3. Do the sum over $\bq$: in a large supercell $\frac1{\OVSC}\sum_\bq\to\int\frac{\dd^3q}{(2\pi)^3}$ over all wavevectors, and $\int\frac{\dd^3q}{(2\pi)^3}\frac{4\pi}{q^2}\ee^{\ii\bq\cdot(\br-\br')}=\frac{1}{|\br-\br'|}$.

    $4\pi/q^2$ is the Fourier transform of $1/r$, because $\nabla^2\frac1r=-4\pi\delta(\br)$. The sum covers all of reciprocal space, so the transform is complete.

The result is the central identity of the section:14

$$\begin{gathered}H^{\mathrm L}=\frac{1}{8\pi}\int\dd^3r\,\big(\bE^{\mathrm L}\big)^2\\ =\frac12\int\dd^3r\int\dd^3r'\,\frac{\rho(\br)\,\rho(\br')}{|\br-\br'|},\\ \rho=-e\,n_{\mathrm e}+\rho^{\mathrm{ion}} .\end{gathered} \tag{B4.20}$$

The energy of the longitudinal field is exactly the instantaneous Coulomb energy of all charges. No Coulomb term was written into the Lagrangian. It was inside $\frac1{8\pi}\int\bE^2$ all along, and Gauss's law brings it out. "Instantaneous" is literal: the longitudinal field at time $t$ is fixed by the charges at the same time $t$, so acts without delay at any distance. Since $\rho$ is a charge density, the kernel is $1/|\br-\br'|$; between two electrons it becomes the bare Coulomb interaction $v$ of Part A, $v(\bq)=4\pi e^2/q^2$.

Physical picture: instantaneous, yet causal

An interaction that acts at once over any distance seems to contradict relativity. It does not, because is not the whole field. When a charge moves, its current also drives the transverse field. The transverse part of a current is spread over all of space, even where the current itself is zero, and the transverse field it produces contains an instantaneous piece that cancels the instantaneous change of the Coulomb field outside the light cone. The measurable field $\bE=\bE^{\mathrm L}+\bE^{\mathrm T}$ spreads at the speed of light. The split into an instantaneous longitudinal part and a retarded transverse part is a choice of bookkeeping, and an excellent one for solids: nearly all the action is at $q\gg\omega/c$, where the transverse correction is tiny.

Three interactions in one term

The charge density has an electronic part $-e\,n_{\mathrm e}$ and an ionic part $\rho^{\mathrm{ion}}=-\nabla\cdot\bP$. Squaring their sum in gives three interactions, each of which Part B dresses:

  • Electron–ion, $\int\!\!\int\rho^{\mathrm e}(\br)\,\rho^{\mathrm{ion}}(\br')/|\br-\br'|$. Second quantization: field operators, densities and currents skip if familiar

The field operator $\hat\Psi(\br)$ removes an electron at the point $\br$, and $\hat\Psi^\dagger(\br)$ creates one. Expanded in a complete set of orbitals, here the Bloch states $\psi_{n\bk}$, it reads $\hat\Psi(\br)=\sum_{n\bk}\psi_{n\bk}(\br)\,c_{n\bk}$, where $c_{n\bk}$ removes an electron from the state $n\bk$. With spin suppressed, $\hat\Psi(\br)\hat\Psi^\dagger(\br')+\hat\Psi^\dagger(\br')\hat\Psi(\br)=\delta(\br-\br')$. Three rules translate first-quantized expressions:

  • a one-electron operator $h(\br,\mathbf p)$ summed over the electrons, $\sum_s h(\br_s,\mathbf p_s)$, becomes $\int\dd^3r\,\hat\Psi^\dagger(\br)\,h(\br,-\ii\hbar\nabla)\,\hat\Psi(\br)$; in the basis of eigenstates of $h$ it is the sum of the eigenvalues times $c^\dagger c$;
  • the number density $\sum_s\delta(\br-\br_s)$ becomes $\hat n_{\mathrm e}(\br)=\hat\Psi^\dagger(\br)\hat\Psi(\br)$;
  • the Coulomb interaction $\frac12\sum_{s\neq s'}e^2/|\br_s-\br_{s'}|$ becomes $\frac12\int\!\!\int\hat\Psi^\dagger(\br)\hat\Psi^\dagger(\br')$ $\times\frac{e^2}{|\br-\br'|}\,\hat\Psi(\br')\hat\Psi(\br)$, with every creator to the left of every annihilator. This normal order excludes the self term $s=s'$, which $\frac12\int\!\!\int\hat n_{\mathrm e}\hat n_{\mathrm e}\,e^2/|\br-\br'|$ would contain.

Apply the first rule to the electronic paramagnetic coupling of , and move the derivative off $\bA\hat\Psi$ in the first term by an integration by parts:

$$\begin{gathered}\frac{e}{2m_{\mathrm e}c}\int\dd^3r\,\hat\Psi^\dagger\big[(-\ii\hbar\nabla)\cdot\bA+\bA\cdot(-\ii\hbar\nabla)\big]\hat\Psi\\ =-\frac{\ii e\hbar}{2m_{\mathrm e}c}\int\dd^3r\,\bA\cdot\big[\hat\Psi^\dagger\nabla\hat\Psi-(\nabla\hat\Psi^\dagger)\hat\Psi\big]=-\frac1c\int\dd^3r\,\hat\bj^{\mathrm{para}}\cdot\bA .\end{gathered}$$

The diamagnetic term becomes $\frac{e^2}{2m_{\mathrm e}c^2}\int\hat n_{\mathrm e}\bA^2$ by the second rule.

Free part and interaction

The longitudinal energy is the Coulomb interaction, so it belongs to the interaction rather than to the free Hamiltonian. Moving it there leaves a free Hamiltonian of electrons, phonons and transverse photons, and an interaction that holds every coupling:20

The free part has three kinds of independent quanta: electrons in Kohn–Sham bands, phonons of the short-range lattice, without LO–TO splitting, and photons with $\omega_q=cq$. Its first two lines are the first line of Part A's electron–phonon Hamiltonian (Section A.2); the third is new. The interaction has five terms: two electron–photon vertices, two ion–photon vertices, and the Coulomb energy.

What $V'$ lacks matters as much. It has no term of the form $g\,c^\dagger c\,\phi$, the electron–phonon vertex of Part A, and no Debye–Waller term. Electrons and phonons interact only through the field. They do so electrostatically, through the electron–ion part of the Coulomb term, which is the bare Fröhlich vertex once $\hat\bu$ is expanded in phonons, and electrodynamically, by exchanging a photon between the electronic paramagnetic vertex and the ionic one. The transverse photons bring retardation in the sense of a finite speed of light, which is not the frequency dependence of electronic screening that Part A also calls retardation (Section X.1).

One remark concerns the longitudinal part of $\hat\bA$ in $V'$. Once $H^{\mathrm L}$ is written with the charges, no term of $H$ contains the momentum conjugate to $\hat A_{\bq3}$, so the longitudinal coordinate never changes. It is a gauge variable, which may be set to zero; then every coupling to it vanishes and only $\hat\bA^{\mathrm T}$ remains in $V'$, which is the Hamiltonian of the Coulomb gauge. Diagrammatic perturbation theory sees the same fact in another form: under $H_0$ the longitudinal coordinate does not evolve, and Section B.5 shows that its free propagator vanishes, while the Coulomb interaction it used to carry sits in $V'$.21

What $H_0$ already contains

The Kohn–Sham energies $E_{n\bk}$ already include the Hartree and exchange–correlation mean field of the valence electrons, and the Coulomb term of $V'$ adds the bare electron–electron interaction on top. The force constants must be short-range, because the dipole–dipole part is regenerated by the ion–ion Coulomb term; and if the Born charges and $\Phi^{\mathrm{sr}}$ come from density-functional theory, they already contain the screening of the ions by the valence electrons. The EPP manuscript flags the double counting without resolving it here. Section B.1 sets out the rule: start from bare vertices and let the diagrams screen, or start from screened inputs and never screen twice.22

Check yourself: three short questions

  1. One plane wave of the vector potential has its amplitude $\bA_\bq$ at 30° to $\bq$. Which part of it costs magnetic energy, and what fraction of $|\bA_\bq|^2$ is that part?

    Answer. Only the transverse part, $\bA^{\mathrm T}_\bq$, has a curl; its length is $|\bA_\bq|\sin30^\circ$, so it holds $\sin^230^\circ=\frac14$ of $|\bA_\bq|^2$, and the magnetic energy of the mode is proportional to $q^2|\bA^{\mathrm T}_\bq|^2$. The longitudinal three quarters cost no magnetic energy at all: nothing pulls them back.

  2. What is the energy of a photon of wavevector $10^{-4}$ Å⁻¹? What wavevector does a photon need to carry the GaAs LO energy, 36.2 meV, and how does it compare with the Brillouin zone?

    Answer. $\hbar cq=1973.27\ \text{eV Å}\times10^{-4}\ \text{Å}^{-1}=0.197$ eV. For 36.2 meV, $q=0.0362/1973.27=1.8\times10^{-5}$ Å⁻¹, about $1.7\times10^{-5}$ of the distance $2\pi/a=1.11$ Å⁻¹ from $\Gamma$ to the zone boundary. Light and lattice waves of equal energy meet only in a minute region around $\Gamma$.

  3. An LO and a TO phonon have the same small wavevector $\bq$. Which appears in the ion–ion Coulomb term, and which can couple to transverse photons?

    Answer. In the LO mode $\bP\parallel\bq$, so its bound charge, of Fourier amplitude $-\ii\bq\cdot\bP_\bq$ with $\bP_\bq$ the amplitude of $\bP$, is nonzero: the ion–ion term stiffens it by $(\omega^{\mathrm{ion}}_{\mathrm p})^2$ before electronic screening. In the TO mode $\bP\perp\bq$, so it carries no charge and is absent from the Coulomb term; its polarization current $\partial_t\bP$ is transverse, and it couples to transverse photons through the ionic paramagnetic vertex. That coupling makes the phonon polaritons of Sections B.6 and B.7.

EPP: $a_{\bq\alpha}$, $a^+_{\bq\alpha}$ in Eqs. (208)–(217) and §4.7; $b_{\bq\sigma}$, $b^+_{\bq\sigma}$ from Eq. (219).
$\hat\bA(\br)$; $\omega_q$
field operator : photons in the transverse part with amplitude $(2\pi\hbar c^2/\OVSC\omega_q)^{1/2}$, plus the longitudinal coordinate; photon frequency $\omega_q=cq$. EPP: Eqs. (216), (244), with longitudinal ladder operators at the transverse scale; $\omega_q=qc$.
$\delta^{\mathrm T}_{\alpha\beta}(\br)$, $\delta^{\mathrm L}_{\alpha\beta}(\br)$
transverse and longitudinal delta functions, the projectors $\delta_{\alpha\beta}-\hat q_\alpha\hat q_\beta$ and $\hat q_\alpha\hat q_\beta$ in real space; each has a dipolar $1/r^3$ tail, and their sum is $\delta_{\alpha\beta}\delta(\br)$ (Å⁻³). EPP: the kernel in braces of Eq. (227) is $\delta^{\mathrm L}$.
$\hat\Psi(\br)$, $\hat n_{\mathrm e}(\br)$
electron field operator, $\sum_{n\bk}\psi_{n\bk}(\br)c_{n\bk}$, spin suppressed (Å$^{-3/2}$), and the number density operator $\hat\Psi^\dagger\hat\Psi$ (Å⁻³). EPP: $\Psi(\br)$ with Kohn–Sham orbitals $\phi_{n\bk}$ (Eq. 240); $\rho_{\rm e}=\Psi^+\Psi$ (Eq. 239), used also where the charge density is meant.
$\hat\bj^{\mathrm{para}}(\br)$
paramagnetic current density of the electrons, ; the current they carry is $\hat\bj^{\mathrm e}=\hat\bj^{\mathrm{para}}-\frac{e^2}{m_{\mathrm e}c}\hat n_{\mathrm e}\hat\bA$. EPP: $\bj_{\rm e}$ of Eq. (238), not that of Eq. (166).
$H_0$, $V'$
free part of the Hamiltonian (Kohn–Sham electrons, phonons of $\Phi^{\mathrm{sr}}$, transverse photons) and the interaction (four minimal couplings and the Coulomb energy), –. EPP: $H_0$ (Eq. 236), $V'$ (Eq. 237), after the intermediate $H'_0$, $V$ (Eq. 186).
