Light absorption, transport and superconductivity

The electron–phonon coupling built in the previous sections enters three classic measurements. Silicon absorbs light below its direct gap only because a phonon supplies the missing crystal momentum; carriers drift through a field at a speed set by how often phonons scatter them; and in many metals the exchange of phonons binds electrons into pairs that carry current without resistance. This section derives the formula behind each measurement from the matrix element, with numbers for real materials, and closes with the assumptions that all of Part A made, which Part B relaxes.

What you need: the electron–phonon matrix element $g_{mn\nu}(\bk,\bq)$ and the Hamiltonian $\hat H^{\mathrm{e\text{-}ph}}$ of Section A.2; for the last two subsections, the screened vertex of density-functional perturbation theory (Section A.3) and the Fröhlich vertex of polar crystals (Section A.4); the phonon part $W^{\mathrm{ph}}$ of the screened interaction and the phonon propagator $F^0$ of Section A.5; the Eliashberg function and the coupling strength $\lambda_{\mathrm{ep}}$ of Section A.7, and the Matsubara frequencies taught in a background box there; and the carrier lifetimes and temperature-dependent gaps of Section A.8. From optics only the absorption coefficient of a medium is needed, and it is defined below. A background box recaps Fermi's golden rule at second order.1

The picture: one coupling, three measurements

Each measurement uses the matrix element in its own way:

  • Indirect absorption. $g$ appears once, next to the coupling of electrons to light, in a two-step amplitude. Light supplies the energy, about 1 eV; the phonon supplies the crystal momentum, a wavevector of about 1 Å⁻¹ in silicon.
  • Transport. $|g|^2$ appears in the rate at which a carrier near a band edge is scattered. The mobility is set by how fast these collisions destroy the current that a field builds up.
  • Superconductivity. $|g|^2$ appears for electrons at the Fermi level, divided by the phonon energy. That ratio is the strength of the attraction that phonon exchange produces between two electrons.

In all three, charges interact only through the instantaneous Coulomb interaction, screened by the electrons and by the moving ions, and light, where it enters, is a classical field imposed from outside. The last subsection collects these assumptions; Part B relaxes them.

Indirect absorption: energy from the photon, momentum from a phonon

In silicon the top of the valence band lies at the zone centre $\Gamma$, at the wavevector $\bk_{\mathrm v}=0$. The bottom of the conduction band lies on the line from $\Gamma$ to the point X, the centre of a square face of the zone, at $|\bk_{\mathrm c}|\approx0.85\times2\pi/a\approx0.98$ Å⁻¹, with $a=5.43$ Å the lattice constant (literature values). The smallest gap, 1.17 eV at low temperature, therefore separates states of different crystal momentum: it is an indirect gap. The smallest gap between states of the same wavevector, the direct gap, is about 3.4 eV, at $\Gamma$. A photon of 1.2 eV carries almost no momentum. Inside the crystal its wavevector is $q_{\mathrm L}=n_{\mathrm r}\omega/c\approx2\times10^{-3}$ Å⁻¹, where $\omega$ is the angular frequency of the light, $c$ the speed of light and $n_{\mathrm r}\approx3.5$ the refractive index of silicon; that is about 500 times smaller than $|\bk_{\mathrm c}|$.

Between 1.1 and 3.4 eV, light can therefore lift an electron across the gap only if something else supplies the crystal momentum $\hbar(\bk_{\mathrm c}-\bk_{\mathrm v})$. A phonon of wavevector $\bq\approx\bk_{\mathrm c}-\bk_{\mathrm v}$ can, by being absorbed or emitted in the same process. Its energy, 20 to 60 meV in silicon, is small next to the gap, so the photon provides nearly all the energy and the phonon nearly all the momentum. The process is weak. At 1.5 eV the intensity of light in silicon falls by a factor $\ee$ over about 10 μm, an absorption coefficient of about $10^3$ cm⁻¹ (defined below). Just above the gap of a direct semiconductor such as GaAs the absorption coefficient is about $10^4$ cm⁻¹, and above 3.4 eV silicon itself reaches about $10^6$ cm⁻¹. This is why silicon solar cells are much thicker than GaAs ones.

Light as an external field

In Part A light is a classical electromagnetic wave imposed from outside. Its wavelength inside silicon, 0.3 μm at 1.2 eV, spans more than 500 unit cells, so over the region where an electron changes its state the field is uniform. Setting the photon wavevector to zero in this way is the electric-dipole approximation; it holds up to photon energies of a few eV. The wave can be described by a vector potential $\bA$ alone, with the scalar potential set to zero (the temporal gauge, which Part B uses throughout):

$$\begin{gathered}\bA(t)=A_{\mathrm L}\,\be_\lambda\cos\omega t,\\ \bE(t)=-\frac1c\,\frac{\partial\bA}{\partial t}=\frac{\omega A_{\mathrm L}}{c}\,\be_\lambda\sin\omega t .\end{gathered} \tag{A9.1}$$
$\bA(t)$, $A_{\mathrm L}$
vector potential of the light wave, uniform in space, and its amplitude (in Gaussian units an electric field times a length)
$\be_\lambda$
polarization of the light: a unit vector perpendicular to the direction of propagation; $\lambda=1,2$ labels the two independent polarizations
$\omega$
angular frequency of the light; $\hbar\omega$ is the photon energy (eV)
$\bE(t)$
electric field of the wave, of amplitude $E_{\mathrm L}=\omega A_{\mathrm L}/c$

An electron of charge $-e$ in this field has the kinetic energy $\big(\hat{\mathbf p}+\frac ec\bA\big)^2/2m_{\mathrm e}$, the rule of minimal coupling, where $\hat{\mathbf p}$ is the momentum operator and $m_{\mathrm e}$ the electron mass. Expanding the square gives three terms. The first is the kinetic energy without light. The last, $e^2A^2/2m_{\mathrm e}c^2$, is the same number for every state when $\bA$ is uniform, so it causes no transitions. The middle term couples the electrons to the light. Written with the velocity operator $\hat{\mathbf v}=\hat{\mathbf p}/m_{\mathrm e}$, and on the second line with the electron operators $c^\dagger_{m\bk}$, $c_{n\bk}$ and the Bloch states $\psi_{n\bk}$ of Section A.2, it reads2

$$\begin{gathered}\hat H^{\mathrm{e\text{-}L}}(t)=\frac ec\,\bA(t)\cdot\hat{\mathbf v}\\ =\frac ec\,\bA(t)\cdot\sum_{\bk mn}\mathbf v_{mn}(\bk)\,c^\dagger_{m\bk}c_{n\bk},\\ \mathbf v_{mn}(\bk)=\langle\psi_{m\bk}|\hat{\mathbf v}|\psi_{n\bk}\rangle .\end{gathered} \tag{A9.2}$$
$\hat H^{\mathrm{e\text{-}L}}(t)$
coupling of the electrons to the light wave, to first order in $\bA$ (eV)
$\hat{\mathbf v}$
velocity operator: $\hat{\mathbf p}/m_{\mathrm e}$ for a local potential; in general the time derivative of the position, which with the nonlocal pseudopotentials of practical calculations acquires an extra term
$\mathbf v_{mn}(\bk)$
velocity matrix element between two Bloch states of the same $\bk$ (m/s). The diagonal element is the band velocity of Section A.7, $\mathbf v_{n\bk}=\mathbf v_{nn}(\bk)=\hbar^{-1}\nabla_{\bk}E_{n\bk}$, the slope of the band $E_{n\bk}$

The sum in connects only states with the same $\bk$. The operator $\hat{\mathbf v}$ commutes with every lattice translation, as the crystal Hamiltonian does, so it conserves crystal momentum: this is the selection rule of Section A.2 with $\bq=0$. To first order, Fermi's golden rule gives the rate of transitions from a full state $n\bk$ to an empty state $m\bk$ under the part of that oscillates as $\ee^{-\ii\omega t}$, which absorbs the energy $\hbar\omega$ (since $\cos\omega t=\frac12\ee^{-\ii\omega t}+\frac12\ee^{\ii\omega t}$, its amplitude is $eA_{\mathrm L}/2c$):

$$\frac{2\pi}{\hbar}\,\Big|\frac{eA_{\mathrm L}}{2c}\,\be_\lambda\cdot\mathbf v_{mn}(\bk)\Big|^2\,\delta\big(E_{m\bk}-E_{n\bk}-\hbar\omega\big).$$

The final state has the same $\bk$ as the initial one. On a band diagram the transition is a vertical arrow, a direct transition. In silicon no vertical transition between 1.1 and 3.4 eV conserves energy, so the first-order rate vanishes there. (The part that oscillates as $\ee^{+\ii\omega t}$ makes the reverse transitions: stimulated emission of light.)

What is measured is the absorption coefficient $\alpha_{\mathrm{abs}}$: the power absorbed per unit volume divided by the intensity $I$ of the light, the energy it carries across unit area per unit time. A beam then decays as $\ee^{-\alpha_{\mathrm{abs}}z}$ along its path $z$. A medium absorbs from a field of amplitude $E_{\mathrm L}$ the power $\omega\varepsilon_2E_{\mathrm L}^2/8\pi$ per unit volume, where $\varepsilon_2$ is the imaginary, absorptive part of its dielectric function, and the intensity in a medium of refractive index $n_{\mathrm r}$ is $I=c\,n_{\mathrm r}E_{\mathrm L}^2/8\pi$. Hence3

$$\begin{gathered}\alpha_{\mathrm{abs}}(\omega)=\frac{\omega\,\varepsilon_2(\omega)}{c\,n_{\mathrm r}(\omega)},\\ I=\frac{n_{\mathrm r}\,\omega^2A_{\mathrm L}^2}{8\pi c} .\end{gathered} \tag{A9.3}$$
$\alpha_{\mathrm{abs}}(\omega)$
absorption coefficient (cm⁻¹); $1/\alpha_{\mathrm{abs}}$ is the depth over which the intensity falls by a factor $\mathrm e$
$\varepsilon_2(\omega)=\mathrm{Im}\,\varepsilon(\omega)$
absorptive part of the macroscopic dielectric function $\varepsilon(\omega)$ of the medium at the frequency of the light (dimensionless)
$n_{\mathrm r}(\omega)$
refractive index, the real part of $\sqrt{\varepsilon(\omega)}$; about 3.5 for silicon near its gap
$I$
intensity of the light inside the medium (energy per unit area and time)

Two steps through a virtual state

With both couplings present, the light term and the electron–phonon term $\hat H^{\mathrm{e\text{-}ph}}$ of , an electron can go from $n\bk$ to $m\,\bk+\bq$ in two steps: one with the light and one with a phonon, in either order. Between the steps the electron occupies a real band state, but with the wrong energy: the energy available differs from the energy of that state. Such an intermediate state is called virtual. Quantum mechanics tolerates it for a time of order $\hbar$ divided by the energy mismatch, and the mismatch appears in the amplitude as an energy denominator.

Fermi's golden rule at second order skip if familiar

Let a system with eigenstates $|\mathrm{in}\rangle$, $|\mathrm{mid}\rangle$, $|\mathrm{fin}\rangle$ of energies $\mathcal E_{\mathrm{in}}$, $\mathcal E_{\mathrm{mid}}$, $\mathcal E_{\mathrm{fin}}$ be perturbed by a weak, time-independent perturbation $\hat H'$, switched on slowly. To first order, the rate of transitions from $|\mathrm{in}\rangle$ to $|\mathrm{fin}\rangle$ is Fermi's golden rule,

$$\frac{2\pi}{\hbar}\,\big|\langle\mathrm{fin}|\hat H'|\mathrm{in}\rangle\big|^2\,\delta\big(\mathcal E_{\mathrm{fin}}-\mathcal E_{\mathrm{in}}\big).$$

When the first-order matrix element vanishes, the next order of time-dependent perturbation theory gives the same formula with the matrix element replaced by the two-step amplitude

$$\mathcal M_{\mathrm{fin},\mathrm{in}}=\sum_{\mathrm{mid}}\frac{\langle\mathrm{fin}|\hat H'|\mathrm{mid}\rangle\,\langle\mathrm{mid}|\hat H'|\mathrm{in}\rangle}{\mathcal E_{\mathrm{in}}-\mathcal E_{\mathrm{mid}}} .$$

Energy is conserved between the initial and the final state only, by the δ function. The intermediate states can have any energy, but a large mismatch $\mathcal E_{\mathrm{mid}}-\mathcal E_{\mathrm{in}}$ suppresses their contribution. Different intermediate states that lead to the same final state add as amplitudes, before squaring, so they can interfere.

A classical wave that oscillates as $\ee^{-\ii\omega t}$ fits this scheme if the photon is counted as part of the system: it is present, with energy $\hbar\omega$, in every state reached before it is absorbed, and absent afterwards. The rule then needs no change, and the result is the same as that of time-dependent perturbation theory with the classical wave.

The derivation of the absorption coefficient follows the golden rule step by step.

  1. Fix the initial and the final state. Initially the electron is in $n\bk$, the photon is present, and mode $\bq\nu$ holds $n_{\bq\nu}$ phonons; in thermal equilibrium their mean number is the Bose–Einstein occupation $n_{\bq\nu}$ of Section A.1. Finally the electron is in $m\,\bk+\bq$, the photon is gone, and the lattice has either one phonon less in mode $\bq\nu$ or one phonon more in mode $-\bq\nu$. Label the two cases $s=-1$ (phonon absorption) and $s=+1$ (phonon emission). With phonon energies measured from the initial state of the lattice,

    $$\mathcal E_{\mathrm{in}}=E_{n\bk}+\hbar\omega,\qquad \mathcal E_{\mathrm{fin}}=E_{m\bk+\bq}+s\hbar\omega_{\bq\nu} .$$
    Crystal momentum is conserved: the electron gains $\hbar\bq$, the photon brings almost none, and the lattice loses $\hbar\bq$ by absorbing $\bq\nu$ or by emitting $-\bq\nu$. The phonon field $\hat\phi_{\bq\nu}=\hat a_{\bq\nu}+\hat a^\dagger_{-\bq\nu}$ in $\hat H^{\mathrm{e\text{-}ph}}$ does both, with the same $g_{mn\nu}(\bk,\bq)$, and the two modes have the same frequency, $\omega_{-\bq\nu}=\omega_{\bq\nu}$ (Section A.1).

  2. Photon first. The light term takes $n\bk$ vertically to a state $n'\bk$ of any band $n'$; the phonon term then takes $n'\bk$ to $m\,\bk+\bq$. In the intermediate state the photon is gone and the phonons are unchanged, so $\mathcal E_{\mathrm{mid}}=E_{n'\bk}$, and this path contributes

    $$\begin{gathered}\frac{g_{mn'\nu}(\bk,\bq)}{\sqrt{N_\ell}}\sqrt{n_{\bq\nu}+\tfrac12+\tfrac s2}\\ \times\frac{(eA_{\mathrm L}/2c)\,\be_\lambda\cdot\mathbf v_{n'n}(\bk)}{E_{n\bk}+\hbar\omega-E_{n'\bk}} .\end{gathered}$$
    The matrix element of $\hat H^{\mathrm{e\text{-}ph}}$ is $g$ divided by $\sqrt{N_\ell}$ ($N_\ell$ is the number of cells) times that of the phonon field between states of definite phonon number. The ladder operators of Section A.1 give $\sqrt{n_{\bq\nu}}$ for absorption and $\sqrt{n_{\bq\nu}+1}$ for emission; in thermal equilibrium mode $-\bq\nu$ holds as many phonons as mode $\bq\nu$. Both cases are $\sqrt{n_{\bq\nu}+\frac12+\frac s2}$.

  3. Phonon first. The phonon term takes $n\bk$ to $n'\,\bk+\bq$; the light term then takes $n'\,\bk+\bq$ vertically to $m\,\bk+\bq$. In the intermediate state the photon is still present and the phonon has already been absorbed or emitted, $\mathcal E_{\mathrm{mid}}=E_{n'\bk+\bq}+\hbar\omega+s\hbar\omega_{\bq\nu}$, so this path contributes

    $$\begin{gathered}\frac{(eA_{\mathrm L}/2c)\,\be_\lambda\cdot\mathbf v_{mn'}(\bk+\bq)}{E_{n\bk}-E_{n'\bk+\bq}-s\hbar\omega_{\bq\nu}}\\ \times\frac{g_{n'n\nu}(\bk,\bq)}{\sqrt{N_\ell}}\sqrt{n_{\bq\nu}+\tfrac12+\tfrac s2} .\end{gathered}$$
    The photon energy cancels between $\mathcal E_{\mathrm{in}}$ and $\mathcal E_{\mathrm{mid}}$. In this path the light is absorbed last, so the mismatch is the energy needed to reach the band state $n'\,\bk+\bq$ with the phonon alone.

  4. Both paths end in the same final state, so their amplitudes add. Summing over the intermediate bands and taking out the common factor $\frac{eA_{\mathrm L}}{2c}\big[(n_{\bq\nu}+\frac12+\frac s2)/N_\ell\big]^{1/2}$ leaves the reduced amplitude of the process, below.

    Both denominators are now written as the energy of the intermediate band state minus the energy available. This flips the overall sign, which drops out of $|\mathcal M|^2$.
$$\begin{gathered}\mathcal M^s_{mn\nu}(\bk,\bq)=\sum_{n'}\bigg[\\ \frac{g_{mn'\nu}(\bk,\bq)\,\be_\lambda\cdot\mathbf v_{n'n}(\bk)}{E_{n'\bk}-E_{n\bk}-\hbar\omega}\;+\\ \frac{\be_\lambda\cdot\mathbf v_{mn'}(\bk+\bq)\;g_{n'n\nu}(\bk,\bq)}{E_{n'\bk+\bq}-E_{n\bk}+s\hbar\omega_{\bq\nu}}\bigg]\end{gathered} \tag{A9.4}$$

Two more steps turn the amplitude into the absorption coefficient:

  1. Square, multiply by $2\pi/\hbar$ and impose energy conservation. The rate of the transition is

    $$\begin{gathered}\frac{2\pi}{\hbar}\Big(\frac{eA_{\mathrm L}}{2c}\Big)^2\,\frac{n_{\bq\nu}+\frac12+\frac s2}{N_\ell}\,\big|\mathcal M^s_{mn\nu}(\bk,\bq)\big|^2\\ \times\delta\big(E_{m\bk+\bq}-E_{n\bk}-\hbar\omega+s\hbar\omega_{\bq\nu}\big).\end{gathered}$$
    This is the golden rule at second order (background box), with $\mathcal E_{\mathrm{fin}}-\mathcal E_{\mathrm{in}}$ from the first step of the list above. The phonon factor is $n_{\bq\nu}+1$ for emission, nonzero even at zero temperature (spontaneous emission), and $n_{\bq\nu}$ for absorption, which needs phonons to be present.

  2. Add the rates of all transitions, each weighted by the probability that its initial state is full and its final state empty, and subtract the reverse transitions, in which the light is emitted again. Multiply by $\hbar\omega$, divide by the volume of the crystal, $\OVSC=N_\ell\OV$ with $\OV$ the volume of the unit cell, and divide by $I$ from . A factor 2 counts the two spin orientations, and the sums over the $N_\ell$ wavevectors become averages over the zone, $\frac1{N_\ell}\sum_{\bk}\to\int\dd^3k/\OBZ$, where $\OBZ=(2\pi)^3/\OV$ is the volume of the Brillouin zone.

    The amplitude $A_{\mathrm L}^2$ cancels between the absorbed power and $I$. The factor $1/N_\ell$ of the rate and the factor $1/N_\ell$ of the volume turn the double sum over $\bk$ and $\bq$ into two zone averages. The occupations of a transition and of its reverse combine into $(f_{n\bk}-f_{m\bk+\bq})(n_{\bq\nu}+\frac12+\frac s2)$, where $f_{n\bk}$ is the Fermi–Dirac occupation, up to a term $s\,f_{m\bk+\bq}(1-f_{n\bk})$ that vanishes when the conduction band is empty.

The result is the phonon-assisted absorption coefficient of Hall, Bardeen and Blatt (1954):45

$$\begin{gathered}\alpha_{\mathrm{abs}}(\omega)=\frac{8\pi^2e^2}{c\,n_{\mathrm r}(\omega)\,\omega\,\OV}\\ \times\sum_{mn\nu}\sum_{s=\pm1}\int\!\!\int\frac{\dd^3k\,\dd^3q}{\OBZ^2}\\ \times\big(f_{n\bk}-f_{m\bk+\bq}\big)\big(n_{\bq\nu}+\tfrac12+\tfrac s2\big)\\ \times\big|\mathcal M^s_{mn\nu}(\bk,\bq)\big|^2\\ \times\delta\big(E_{m\bk+\bq}-E_{n\bk}-\hbar\omega+s\hbar\omega_{\bq\nu}\big) .\end{gathered} \tag{A9.5}$$
$\mathcal M^s_{mn\nu}(\bk,\bq)$
reduced amplitude of the transition $n\bk\to m\,\bk+\bq$ with absorption ($s=-1$) or emission ($s=+1$) of a phonon of branch $\nu$ (units of a velocity, m/s)
$n'$
band of the virtual intermediate state
$s$
$+1$ for phonon emission, $-1$ for phonon absorption (not the grid integers $s_j$ of Section A.1)
$\OBZ$
volume of the Brillouin zone, $(2\pi)^3/\OV$ (Å⁻³)

Read for the transitions from the top of the valence band to the bottom of the conduction band, where $E_{m\bk+\bq}-E_{n\bk}$ equals the gap $E_{\mathrm g}$ between the band extrema, with clamped nuclei as in Section A.8. The δ function then requires $\hbar\omega=E_{\mathrm g}+s\hbar\omega_{\bq\nu}$. With a phonon absorbed, light of energy $E_{\mathrm g}-\hbar\omega_{\bq\nu}$, below the gap, is already absorbed; with a phonon emitted, the light must supply $E_{\mathrm g}+\hbar\omega_{\bq\nu}$. Each phonon branch therefore opens two thresholds, $\hbar\omega_{\bq\nu}$ below and above the gap, with the weights $n_{\bq\nu}$ and $n_{\bq\nu}+1$. The denominators of are large. In silicon the photon-first path passes through the conduction states at $\Gamma$, about 2.2 eV above the energy available at the edge, and the phonon-first path through valence states near $\bk_{\mathrm c}$, between 2 and 3 eV below it. These virtual states last about $\hbar/(2\ \mathrm{eV})\approx0.3$ fs.

Physical picture: borrowing energy for a fraction of a femtosecond

In the photon-first path the electron absorbs 1.2 eV and finds itself at the same wavevector, where the nearest band state lies 2.2 eV higher. It cannot stay there: within about 0.3 fs it either falls back or is scattered by a phonon to the conduction-band minimum at $\bk_{\mathrm c}$, where the energy fits. The phonon need not supply energy, only momentum. The two orders of the steps cannot be told apart in the final state, so they interfere, and $\mathcal M$ adds them before squaring.

What leaves out

Light is a classical field in the electric-dipole approximation. The electrons are independent particles, so excitons (an excited electron and the hole it leaves, bound by their Coulomb attraction) and local-field effects (the response to the variation of the field within a cell) are missing; the Bethe–Salpeter equation adds both, but not within this formula. The band energies in the δ function and in both denominators are those of the crystal with clamped nuclei, which the next subsection corrects. Light acts on the electrons only: the ions of a polar crystal, which absorb infrared light strongly near $\omega_{\mathrm{TO}}$ through their Born charges, are absent, and so is the medium's own response to the light, which enters only through the number $n_{\mathrm r}$, put in by hand. The review notes that a systematic route exists, the electronic dielectric function of Section A.5 built from Green's functions dressed by the electron–phonon self-energy; it quotes the second-order result instead.

Temperature: two slopes and a moving edge

Temperature changes the edge in two ways. The phonon occupations set the weights of the thresholds, which the first subsection works out for the simplest bands. The band energies themselves shift with temperature, which moves the thresholds; the second subsection shows how to include that shift.

The edge of two parabolic bands

Near the edge only states close to the two band extrema take part, and reduces to a closed form. Take a full valence band with its maximum at $\bk_{\mathrm v}$ and an empty conduction band with its minimum at $\bk_{\mathrm c}$, both parabolic. The phonons involved all have $\bq\approx\bk_{\mathrm c}-\bk_{\mathrm v}$; neglect the dispersion of each branch there, writing $\omega_\nu$ for its frequency, and treat $|\mathcal M|^2$ and $n_{\mathrm r}$ as constants.

  1. Measure the energy of the hole below the valence maximum by $x\ge0$ and that of the electron above the conduction minimum by $y\ge0$. Then $E_{m\bk+\bq}-E_{n\bk}=E_{\mathrm g}+x+y$, and the δ function of becomes $\delta(x+y-\Delta E_s)$ with

    $$\Delta E_s=\hbar\omega-E_{\mathrm g}-s\hbar\omega_\nu .$$
    $\Delta E_s$ is the energy left over beyond the threshold, to be shared between the hole and the electron.

  2. For each $\nu$ and $s$, change the integration variable from $\bq$ to the final wavevector $\bk'=\bk+\bq$. The two integrals then run independently, one over the valence states and one over the conduction states.

    With $|\mathcal M|^2$ and $\omega_\nu$ constant, nothing else in the integrand depends on how $\bk$ and $\bk'$ are related.

  3. Turn each integral into an integral over energy. Near a parabolic extremum in three dimensions the number of states within $\dd x$ grows as $\sqrt x\,\dd x$, so

    $$\begin{gathered}\int_0^\infty\!\!\dd x\int_0^\infty\!\!\dd y\,\sqrt x\,\sqrt y\;\delta(x+y-\Delta E_s)\\ =\frac\pi8\,(\Delta E_s)^2\,\Theta(\Delta E_s) .\end{gathered}$$
    The δ function sets $y=\Delta E_s-x$, and the integrand $\sqrt{x(\Delta E_s-x)}$ traces a semicircle of radius $\Delta E_s/2$, whose area is $\pi(\Delta E_s)^2/8$. The step function $\Theta$ is 1 for a positive argument and 0 otherwise: below the threshold there are no final states.

  4. Collect all constants into one number $C$ and a relative weight $w_\nu$ for each branch, and keep the phonon factors and the factor $1/\omega$ of :

    $$\begin{gathered}\alpha_{\mathrm{abs}}(\omega)=\frac{C}{\hbar\omega}\sum_\nu w_\nu\Big[\\ (n_\nu+1)\,(\Delta E_+)^2\,\Theta(\Delta E_+)\\ +\,n_\nu\,(\Delta E_-)^2\,\Theta(\Delta E_-)\Big] .\end{gathered} \tag{A9.6}$$
    $\Delta E_\pm$ is $\Delta E_s$ for $s=\pm1$, and $n_\nu=n_{\mathrm B}(\omega_\nu)$ is the Bose–Einstein occupation of branch $\nu$, with $n_{\mathrm B}$ the Bose–Einstein function of Section A.1. The emission term carries the weight $n_\nu+1$, the absorption term $n_\nu$.
$C$
a constant that collects $|\mathcal M|^2$, $n_{\mathrm r}$, the band masses and the prefactor of (cm⁻¹ eV⁻¹)
$w_\nu$
relative strength of branch $\nu$ (dimensionless)
$\Theta$
unit step function of Section A.8: $\Theta(x)=1$ for $x\gt0$ and $0$ otherwise

Each branch contributes two parabolas: one that starts at $E_{\mathrm g}-\hbar\omega_\nu$, fed by phonon absorption, and one that starts at $E_{\mathrm g}+\hbar\omega_\nu$, fed by emission. Plotted as $\sqrt{\alpha_{\mathrm{abs}}}$ against $\hbar\omega$, each is nearly a straight line (the factor $1/\hbar\omega$ varies slowly), of slope proportional to $\sqrt{n_\nu}$ and $\sqrt{n_\nu+1}$. These are the two slopes of the indirect edge. The squared ratio of the slopes is $n_\nu/(n_\nu+1)=\ee^{-\hbar\omega_\nu/\kB T}$ at the temperature $T$, and the thresholds lie $\hbar\omega_\nu$ below and above the gap, so such plots measure the phonons that assist the transitions. Macfarlane and co-workers used them in the 1950s; in silicon the main assisting phonons are a transverse acoustic (TA) phonon of about 18 meV and a transverse optical (TO) phonon of about 58 meV (literature values). On cooling, the absorption part freezes out. At 296 K the occupations are $n_{\mathrm{TA}}=0.95$ and $n_{\mathrm{TO}}=0.12$, at 78 K they are $0.07$ and $2\times10^{-4}$: the absorption below the gap nearly disappears, while the emission part survives with $n_\nu+1\to1$.

Why the edge moves

The band energies in belong to the crystal with its nuclei clamped at equilibrium, so the thresholds do not move with temperature. The measured edge of silicon does: between 78 and 296 K it moves down by about 40 meV, because the gap shrinks on heating, as Section A.8 showed. In the one-oscillator form of that section, , the renormalized gap is $E^{\mathrm{qp}}_{\mathrm g}(T)=E_{\mathrm g}-|\Delta E^{\mathrm{ZPR}}_{\mathrm g}|\,\big[1+2n_{\mathrm B}(\omega_0)\big]$. Here $E_{\mathrm g}$ is the gap with clamped nuclei, $\Delta E^{\mathrm{ZPR}}_{\mathrm g}$ its zero-point renormalization, $\omega_0$ the frequency of the single phonon of the model, and $n_{\mathrm B}$ the Bose–Einstein function at temperature $T$. For silicon, $E_{\mathrm g}=1.23$ eV, $\Delta E^{\mathrm{ZPR}}_{\mathrm g}=-60$ meV and $\hbar\omega_0=30$ meV give $E^{\mathrm{qp}}_{\mathrm g}=1.17$ eV at 0 K and 1.12 eV at 296 K (literature values, illustrative). The first first-principles calculation with , by Noffsinger and co-workers (2012) for silicon, therefore had to be shifted rigidly to the measured onset at each temperature.6

Two remedies exist. The first puts the temperature-dependent band energies of Section A.8 into , but not everywhere. Patrick and Giustino (2014) and Zacharias and Giustino (2016) worked out where they belong: in the δ function and in the first denominator of , the one of the photon-first path, but not in the second. The review sums up the rule as follows: the renormalization changes the energies of real transitions but leaves those of virtual transitions unchanged. Zacharias, Patrick and Giustino (2015) took a second route. In a crystal whose atoms are displaced from equilibrium, crystal momentum is no longer conserved, so an indirect transition becomes an ordinary first-order one. Averaging the clamped-nuclei $\varepsilon_2$ over the displacements of the vibrating lattice, an idea due to Williams and Lax, gives

$$\varepsilon_2(\omega;T)=\big\langle\varepsilon_2\big(\omega;\{\bu_{\ell\kappa}\}\big)\big\rangle_T . \tag{A9.7}$$

$\langle\cdots\rangle_T$

Transport: from lifetimes to mobility

A uniform, static electric field $\bE$ accelerates every carrier: the crystal momentum of an electron changes as $\hbar\dot\bk=-e\bE$. Phonons scatter the carriers to other states and randomize their direction of motion. In a steady state the two effects balance, and the carriers drift with a mean velocity $\mathbf v_{\mathrm d}$ proportional to the field. The mobility $\mu_{\mathrm{mob}}$ is the drift speed per unit field, $\mathbf v_{\mathrm d}=-\mu_{\mathrm{mob}}\bE$ for electrons, quoted in cm²/(V s). At room temperature, pure silicon has an electron mobility of about 1400 cm²/(V s) and GaAs about 8500 cm²/(V s) (literature values). In pure crystals near room temperature the phonons set these values; impurities take over at low temperature or high doping.

The simplest model: one relaxation time

Suppose every carrier is scattered at random, independent times, on average once per interval $\tau$, and that each collision leaves it with a random velocity whose average is zero. Between collisions a carrier of effective mass $m^*$ gains the velocity $-e\bE t/m^*$ in a time $t$. On average a carrier was last scattered a time $\tau$ ago, so the drift velocity is $-e\tau\bE/m^*$ and

$$\mu_{\mathrm{mob}}=\frac{e\,\tau}{m^*} . \tag{A9.8}$$
$\mu_{\mathrm{mob}}$
mobility, the drift speed per unit field (cm²/(V s)); not the chemical potential $\mu$
$\tau$
relaxation time, the mean time between collisions that destroy the drift (ps)
$m^*$
effective mass of the carriers, set by the curvature of the band, $1/m^*=\hbar^{-2}\,\partial^2E_{n\bk}/\partial k^2$, as in Sections A.4 and A.8; quoted in units of the electron mass $m_{\mathrm e}$

This is Drude's formula with the band mass. Inverted with the measured mobilities, $\tau=m^*\mu_{\mathrm{mob}}/e$ gives about 0.2 ps for silicon, with $m^*=0.26\,m_{\mathrm e}$ (the conductivity mass of its six conduction valleys), and about 0.3 ps for GaAs, with $m^*=0.067\,m_{\mathrm e}$. Two points remain open. First, every state $n\bk$ has its own lifetime $\tau_{n\bk}$, the one Section A.8 computed from the imaginary part of the self-energy, and the formula does not say which average of these lifetimes plays the role of $\tau$. Second, not every collision destroys the drift: a collision that turns the velocity by a small angle leaves most of it in its original direction. The Boltzmann equation settles both points. shows the steady state in $\bk$ space and how much of the drift a single collision removes.

The Boltzmann equation, linearized

The semiclassical picture treats each carrier as a wave packet with a definite band and wavevector, moving with the band velocity $\mathbf v_{n\bk}$ of . The state of the carriers is described by the occupations $f_{n\bk}$, now allowed to differ from the Fermi–Dirac occupations of equilibrium, which from here on are written $f^0_{n\bk}$. Each carrier carries the charge $-e$ at the velocity $\mathbf v_{n\bk}$, so the current density $\bj$, the charge that crosses unit area per unit time, is8

$$\bj=-\frac{2e}{\OVSC}\sum_{n\bk}\mathbf v_{n\bk}\,f_{n\bk} . \tag{A9.9}$$

The factor 2 counts spin, and $\OVSC$ is the volume of the crystal. In equilibrium the current vanishes: $f^0_{n\bk}$ depends on $\bk$ only through the energy, which is the same at $\bk$ and $-\bk$, while the velocity changes sign.

Two things change the occupations. The field moves every carrier through $\bk$ space at the rate $\dot\bk=-e\bE/\hbar$, and phonons scatter carriers from state to state. In a steady state the two changes cancel:

$$\dot\bk\cdot\nabla_{\bk}f_{n\bk}=\Big(\frac{\partial f_{n\bk}}{\partial t}\Big)_{\mathrm{coll}} .$$

The left side is the change of the occupations along the path that the field imposes. The right side, the collision term, is the net rate at which collisions fill state $n\bk$. The field is weak, so $f_{n\bk}=f^0_{n\bk}+\delta f_{n\bk}$ with $\delta f$ proportional to $\bE$, and both sides are expanded to first order in $\bE$:

  1. On the left only $f^0$ is needed, and $\nabla_{\bk}f^0_{n\bk}=\hbar\,\mathbf v_{n\bk}\,\partial f^0_{n\bk}/\partial E_{n\bk}$:

    $$\dot\bk\cdot\nabla_{\bk}f_{n\bk}\simeq-e\bE\cdot\mathbf v_{n\bk}\,\frac{\partial f^0_{n\bk}}{\partial E_{n\bk}} .$$
    $\dot\bk$ is already proportional to $\bE$, so the correction $\delta f$ would add a term of second order. $f^0$ depends on $\bk$ through $E_{n\bk}$, whose gradient is $\hbar\mathbf v_{n\bk}$.

  2. The collision term counts transitions out of $n\bk$ minus transitions into it. The golden-rule rate from $n\bk$ to $m\,\bk+\bq$, by absorbing a phonon $\bq\nu$ or emitting a phonon $-\bq\nu$, is $R_{n\bk\to m\bk+\bq}/N_\ell$, with

    $$\begin{gathered}R_{n\bk\to m\bk+\bq}=\frac{2\pi}{\hbar}\sum_\nu|g_{mn\nu}(\bk,\bq)|^2\\ \times\big[n_{\bq\nu}\,\delta_++(n_{\bq\nu}+1)\,\delta_-\big],\\ \delta_\pm=\delta\big(E_{m\bk+\bq}-E_{n\bk}\mp\hbar\omega_{\bq\nu}\big).\end{gathered}$$

    Each transition needs a full initial state and an empty final state:

    $$\begin{gathered}\Big(\frac{\partial f_{n\bk}}{\partial t}\Big)_{\mathrm{coll}}=-\frac1{N_\ell}\sum_{m\bq}\Big[\\ R_{n\bk\to m\bk+\bq}\,f_{n\bk}\big(1-f_{m\bk+\bq}\big)\\ -R_{m\bk+\bq\to n\bk}\,f_{m\bk+\bq}\big(1-f_{n\bk}\big)\Big].\end{gathered}$$
    $\delta_+$ puts the final state one phonon energy above the initial one (absorption), $\delta_-$ one phonon energy below (emission). The phonons are assumed to stay in equilibrium. The reverse rate $R_{m\bk+\bq\to n\bk}$ has the same $|g|^2$, because the Hamiltonian is Hermitian, but it emits where the forward rate absorbs, so the factors $n_{\bq\nu}$ and $n_{\bq\nu}+1$ are exchanged.

  3. Linearize in $\delta f$. The terms proportional to $\delta f_{n\bk}$ add up to $-\delta f_{n\bk}/\tau_{n\bk}$, where $1/\tau_{n\bk}$ is exactly the golden-rule scattering rate of Section A.8, : the zone average of $\frac{2\pi}{\hbar}|g_{mn\nu}(\bk,\bq)|^2$ times $(n_{\bq\nu}+f^0_{m\bk+\bq})\,\delta_+$ plus $(n_{\bq\nu}+1-f^0_{m\bk+\bq})\,\delta_-$, summed over $m$ and $\nu$.

    For a final state above $n\bk$, the forward term contributes $n(1-f^0_m)$ and the reverse term $(n+1)f^0_m$, which add up to $n+f^0_m$. For a final state below, $(n+1)(1-f^0_m)+nf^0_m=n+1-f^0_m$. These are the factors of the golden-rule lifetime: Pauli blocking and stimulated emission combined.

  4. The terms proportional to $\delta f_{m\bk+\bq}$ describe carriers scattered into $n\bk$. Their factors combine in the same way, now with the occupation of $n\bk$, into $n_{\bq\nu}+1-f^0_{n\bk}$ when $m\,\bk+\bq$ lies above $n\bk$ and $n_{\bq\nu}+f^0_{n\bk}$ when it lies below. Collecting both sides gives the linearized Boltzmann equation, below.

    Two terms contain $\delta f_{m\bk+\bq}$: carriers arriving from $m\,\bk+\bq$, weighted by $1-f^0_{n\bk}$, and carriers leaving $n\bk$ for a target that is more or less occupied, weighted by $f^0_{n\bk}$. When $m\,\bk+\bq$ lies above, the arrival is an emission and the departure an absorption: $(n+1)(1-f^0_n)+nf^0_n=n+1-f^0_n$. When it lies below, $n(1-f^0_n)+(n+1)f^0_n=n+f^0_n$.
$$\begin{gathered}-e\bE\cdot\mathbf v_{n\bk}\,\frac{\partial f^0_{n\bk}}{\partial E_{n\bk}}\\ =-\frac{\delta f_{n\bk}}{\tau_{n\bk}}\\ +\sum_{m\nu}\int\frac{\dd^3q}{\OBZ}\,\mathcal K_{mn\nu}\,\delta f_{m\bk+\bq},\\ \mathcal K_{mn\nu}(\bk,\bq)=\frac{2\pi}{\hbar}|g_{mn\nu}(\bk,\bq)|^2\\ \times\big[(n_{\bq\nu}+1-f^0_{n\bk})\,\delta_+\\ +(n_{\bq\nu}+f^0_{n\bk})\,\delta_-\big] .\end{gathered} \tag{A9.10}$$
$\delta f_{n\bk}$
deviation of the occupation from equilibrium, first order in the field
$\mathcal K_{mn\nu}(\bk,\bq)$
in-scattering kernel: the rate at which a deviation in $m\,\bk+\bq$ feeds state $n\bk$ through branch $\nu$ (s⁻¹)

The left side is the drift. On the right, the first term is the scattering out of $n\bk$ and the sum is the scattering into $n\bk$ from all states $m\,\bk+\bq$.9 There is one such linear equation for each state, coupled to all the others through $\mathcal K$.

The current follows by inserting the solution into , where only $\delta f$ contributes. It is proportional to the field, $j_\alpha=\sum_\beta\sigma_{\alpha\beta}E_\beta$, which defines the conductivity tensor $\sigma_{\alpha\beta}$; here the field components $E_\beta$ carry a Cartesian index, the band energies $E_{n\bk}$ a band and a wavevector. The mobility is the conductivity per unit carrier charge density, $(\mu_{\mathrm{mob}})_{\alpha\beta}=\sigma_{\alpha\beta}/(e\,n_{\mathrm c})$, where $n_{\mathrm c}$ is the number of carriers per unit volume.

Solving it: a relaxation time, or iteration

SERTA. Drop the scattering-in term of . Each state then relaxes on its own, with the lifetime of Section A.8, and $\delta f_{n\bk}=e\,\tau_{n\bk}\,(\partial f^0_{n\bk}/\partial E_{n\bk})\,\mathbf v_{n\bk}\cdot\bE$. Because $\tau_{n\bk}$ is the lifetime given by the self-energy, this is called the self-energy relaxation-time approximation (SERTA); the review calls it the energy relaxation-time approximation. Inserted in , it gives the mobility

$$\begin{gathered}(\mu_{\mathrm{mob}})_{\alpha\beta}=-\frac{2e}{n_{\mathrm c}\OV}\sum_n\int\frac{\dd^3k}{\OBZ}\\ \times\frac{\partial f^0_{n\bk}}{\partial E_{n\bk}}\,v_{n\bk\alpha}\,v_{n\bk\beta}\,\tau_{n\bk} .\end{gathered} \tag{A9.11}$$
$(\mu_{\mathrm{mob}})_{\alpha\beta}$
mobility tensor: the drift velocity along $\alpha$ per unit field along $\beta$ (cm²/(V s)); in a cubic crystal it is $\mu_{\mathrm{mob}}\,\delta_{\alpha\beta}$
$n_{\mathrm c}$
density of carriers, electrons in the conduction band or holes in the valence band (cm⁻³)
$v_{n\bk\alpha}$
Cartesian components of the band velocity $\mathbf v_{n\bk}$
$\partial f^0_{n\bk}/\partial E_{n\bk}$
derivative of the Fermi–Dirac occupation, negative and peaked within a few $\kB T$ of the chemical potential; in a nondegenerate semiconductor it weights the states within a few $\kB T$ of the band edge

For a parabolic band with a constant lifetime $\tau_{n\bk}=\tau$, gives exactly the Drude result : since $(\partial f^0/\partial E)\,v_\alpha=\hbar^{-1}\partial f^0/\partial k_\alpha$, integrating by parts in $\bk$ turns $-(\partial f^0/\partial E)\,v_\alpha v_\beta$ into $f^0\delta_{\alpha\beta}/m^*$, and the remaining zone integral counts the carriers. In general, is an average of the lifetimes over the states near the band edge, or near the chemical potential in a metal, weighted by the square of the velocity.

The iterative solution. With the scattering-in term kept, is a linear integral equation for $\delta f$. It can be solved by iteration: start from the SERTA solution, insert it in the sum, solve for a new $\delta f$, and repeat until it no longer changes. This is the iterative Boltzmann transport equation (IBTE); the mobility then follows from with the converged $\delta f$. The scattering-in term matters when collisions are anisotropic. A carrier scattered through a small angle keeps most of its velocity, and with it most of its contribution to the drift, so it barely reduces the current, yet SERTA counts the collision as a full loss. For elastic scattering in an isotropic band the iteration converges to a closed form: $\tau_{n\bk}$ in is replaced by the momentum relaxation time $\tau_{\mathrm{tr}}$, in which each collision is weighted by the fraction $1-\cos\theta$ of the velocity it removes, with $\theta$ the angle between the initial and the final wavevector:

$$\begin{gathered}\frac1\tau=\frac1{N_\ell}\sum_{\bk'}R_{\bk\to\bk'},\\ \frac1{\tau_{\mathrm{tr}}}=\frac1{N_\ell}\sum_{\bk'}R_{\bk\to\bk'}(1-\cos\theta) .\end{gathered} \tag{A9.12}$$

Isotropic scattering gives $\tau_{\mathrm{tr}}=\tau$, since $\cos\theta$ averages to zero; scattering concentrated at small angles gives $\tau_{\mathrm{tr}}\gg\tau$. The momentum relaxation-time approximation, which the review shows next to the full solution for silicon, carries this weight over to any band, usually with $\mathbf v_{n\bk}\cdot\mathbf v_{m\bk+\bq}/|\mathbf v_{n\bk}|^2$ in place of $\cos\theta$. takes a parabolic band in two dimensions, for clarity, and a squared matrix element proportional to $1/(q^2+q_0^2)$, where the wavevector $q_0$ sets how strongly small angles are favoured: a small $q_0$ mimics the polar coupling of GaAs, a large one the nearly isotropic scattering in silicon.

Silicon and GaAs. For $n$-type silicon, the calculations of Li (2015) shown in the review give the same mobility within a few percent with SERTA, with the momentum relaxation-time approximation and with the full solution, from 100 to 300 K: about $2.3\times10^4$ cm²/(V s) at 100 K and about 1850 cm²/(V s) at 300 K. The approximations agree because the dominant collisions, with acoustic phonons and between the six conduction valleys, randomize the direction of motion. The calculated values lie at or above the measured ones: at 300 K they agree with one set of measurements (about 1800 cm²/(V s)) and exceed another (about 1350 cm²/(V s)); near 150 K they exceed both, by a factor of about 1.4 to 1.8. The review suggests the DFT matrix elements as one cause (see the subsection on the DFT vertex below).10 In GaAs, carriers near room temperature are scattered mostly by LO phonons, through the long-range Fröhlich vertex of Section A.4, , whose $|g|^2\propto1/q^2$ favours small $q$ and hence small scattering angles. These collisions shorten the lifetime far more than they relax the current. They are also inelastic, since $\hbar\omega_{\mathrm{LO}}$$\;=36$ meV is comparable to the thermal energy, so no single relaxation time exists; Rode's iterative method, around 1970, was developed for exactly this case. The same small-$q$ behaviour of the polar vertex makes the adiabatic band renormalization diverge (Section A.8).

What the Boltzmann equation leaves out

The semiclassical picture needs carriers whose energy is sharper than the thermal energy, $\hbar/\tau_{n\bk}\ll\kB T$; a lifetime of 0.1 ps gives $\hbar/\tau=7$ meV against $\kB T=26$ meV at room temperature, which is adequate but not by a wide margin. Only one-phonon processes enter the rates, the phonons stay in equilibrium, and impurities and other carriers, which add their own scattering, are left out. The results also depend on very fine $\bk$ and $\bq$ grids near the band edges, obtained by Wannier interpolation (Section A.4), and on the effective masses, since the rates scale with the density of final states; some calculations therefore take the band energies from the GW approximation of Section A.5, which corrects the gaps and masses of DFT. In metals the same equation, with the states at the Fermi level, gives the resistivity, which is easier to converge than the mobility of a semiconductor.

Superconductivity: from the Eliashberg function to $T_{\mathrm c}$

The picture: a delayed attraction

Below a critical temperature $T_{\mathrm c}$ many metals carry current without resistance and expel magnetic fields: aluminium below 1.2 K, lead below 7.2 K, MgB₂ below 39 K, and H₃S, compressed to about 155 GPa, below about 200 K (literature values). In these superconductors, called conventional, the cause is the electron–phonon coupling. An electron moving through the lattice pulls the positive ions toward its path. The ions respond on the time scale of a phonon period, a few tenths of a picosecond, by which time the electron, moving at the Fermi velocity of about $10^6$ m/s, is hundreds of nanometres away. A second electron that passes later is attracted by the displaced ions, the wake of the first. The two electrons attract each other without ever being close at the same time, so their direct Coulomb repulsion, which acts instantly and at short range, is largely avoided.

Cooper (1956) showed that any net attraction, however weak, binds two electrons of opposite wavevector and spin near the Fermi surface, the surface in $\bk$ space where $E_{n\bk}$ equals the Fermi energy $E_{\mathrm F}$, into a Cooper pair; Bardeen, Cooper and Schrieffer (BCS, 1957) showed that the pairs condense into one coherent state. Breaking a pair costs at least $2\Delta$, where $\Delta$ is the superconducting gap in the excitation spectrum: about 0.18 meV in aluminium and 1.4 meV in lead. BCS modelled the attraction as a constant acting between electrons within a phonon energy $\hbar\omega_{\mathrm{ph}}$ of $E_{\mathrm F}$, and found

$$\begin{gathered}\kB T_{\mathrm c}=1.13\,\hbar\omega_{\mathrm{ph}}\,\ee^{-1/\lambda_{\mathrm{BCS}}},\\ 2\Delta=3.53\,\kB T_{\mathrm c} .\end{gathered} \tag{A9.13}$$
$T_{\mathrm c}$
superconducting critical temperature (K)
$\Delta$
superconducting gap at zero temperature (meV): the minimum energy of an electron excitation, half the cost of breaking a pair
$\omega_{\mathrm{ph}}$
a typical, or the largest, phonon frequency of the metal
$\lambda_{\mathrm{BCS}}$
dimensionless strength of the net attraction: the attraction between two electrons at the Fermi energy times the density of states $N_{\mathrm F}$ there (defined below)

The exponential explains why $T_{\mathrm c}$ lies far below the phonon temperature $\hbar\omega_{\mathrm{ph}}/\kB$ of a few hundred kelvin, and why it is so sensitive to the coupling. A first-principles theory must compute the number in the exponent. The next subsections do so: the attraction from $g$, the repulsion from the screened Coulomb interaction, and the corrections that strong coupling brings.

The coupling on the Fermi surface

Pairing involves electrons within a phonon energy of the Fermi energy, a shell much thinner than the width of the bands. The matrix elements that matter are therefore those between two states on the Fermi surface, averaged over the surface. For any quantity $X_{n\bk,m\bk'}$ that depends on two states, define the double average over the Fermi surface:11

$$\begin{gathered}\langle\!\langle X\rangle\!\rangle_{\mathrm{FS}}=\frac{1}{N_{\mathrm F}^2}\sum_{nm}\int\!\!\int\frac{\dd^3k\,\dd^3k'}{\OBZ^2}\\ \times\delta(E_{n\bk}-E_{\mathrm F})\,\delta(E_{m\bk'}-E_{\mathrm F})\\ \times X_{n\bk,m\bk'},\\ N_{\mathrm F}=\sum_n\int\frac{\dd^3k}{\OBZ}\,\delta(E_{n\bk}-E_{\mathrm F}) .\end{gathered} \tag{A9.14}$$
$N_{\mathrm F}$
density of electronic states at the Fermi energy, per spin and per unit cell (states/eV), as in Section A.7; about 0.2 eV⁻¹ per atom for aluminium
$\langle\!\langle X\rangle\!\rangle_{\mathrm{FS}}$
average of $X$ over all pairs of states $n\bk$ and $m\bk'$ on the Fermi surface

The isotropic Eliashberg function $\alpha^2F$ of Section A.7, , has both states on the Fermi surface and counts the phonons that connect them, resolved by phonon energy. In the notation of it reads

$$\begin{gathered}\alpha^2F(\omega)=N_{\mathrm F}\big\langle\!\big\langle\sum_\nu|g_{mn\nu}(\bk,\bq)|^2\\ \times\delta\big(\hbar\omega-\hbar\omega_{\bq\nu}\big)\big\rangle\!\big\rangle_{\mathrm{FS}},\\ \bq=\bk'-\bk .\end{gathered} \tag{A9.15}$$

It is dimensionless: $|g|^2$ is an energy squared, the δ function an inverse energy, and $N_{\mathrm F}$ an inverse energy. It resembles the phonon density of states, with each phonon weighted by how strongly it couples electrons on the Fermi surface; its peaks reach a few tenths in weakly coupled metals such as aluminium and 1 to 2 in strongly coupled ones such as lead. Why this function controls pairing becomes clear from the interaction it produces:

  1. Two electrons exchange a phonon $\bq\nu$: one emits it and the other absorbs it. Between Bloch states this is the phonon part $W^{\mathrm{ph}}$ of the screened interaction, , the vertex squared times the free phonon propagator :

    $$\begin{gathered}\frac{1}{N_\ell}\big|g_{mn\nu}(\bk,\bq)\big|^2\,F^0_\nu(\bq,\omega),\\ F^0_\nu(\bq,\omega)=\frac1\hbar\,\frac{2\omega_{\bq\nu}}{\omega^2-\omega^2_{\bq\nu}} .\end{gathered}$$
    $\hbar\omega$ is the energy transferred between the electrons. The factor $1/N_\ell$ comes from the two vertices of $\hat H^{\mathrm{e\text{-}ph}}$; it disappears in the next steps, when the partner states are summed.

  2. Electrons that pair exchange energies of order $\kB T_{\mathrm c}$, far below the phonon energy. For $\omega\ll\omega_{\bq\nu}$ the propagator tends to $-2/\hbar\omega_{\bq\nu}$, so the interaction is $-2|g|^2/(N_\ell\hbar\omega_{\bq\nu})$: negative, an attraction.

    The sign changes at $\omega=\omega_{\bq\nu}$. The lattice follows the electrons, and attracts, only at frequencies below its own: this is the delay of the picture above.

  3. Multiply by the number of partner states per unit energy, $N_\ell N_{\mathrm F}$ for the whole crystal, change the sign so that an attraction counts as positive, and average over the Fermi surface. The factor $N_\ell$ cancels, and the result is the dimensionless coupling strength:

    $$\begin{gathered}\lambda_{\mathrm{ep}}=2N_{\mathrm F}\\ \times\Big\langle\!\!\Big\langle\sum_\nu\frac{|g_{mn\nu}(\bk,\bq)|^2}{\hbar\omega_{\bq\nu}}\Big\rangle\!\!\Big\rangle_{\mathrm{FS}}\\ =2\int_0^\infty\dd\omega\,\frac{\alpha^2F(\omega)}{\omega} .\end{gathered} \tag{A9.16}$$
    The second form follows from , since $\int\dd\omega\,\delta(\hbar\omega-\hbar\omega_{\bq\nu})/\omega=1/\hbar\omega_{\bq\nu}$. It is the moment of $\alpha^2F$ that defines $\lambda_{\mathrm{ep}}$ in Section A.7, , where the same number set the mass enhancement $1+\lambda_{\mathrm{ep}}$.

The weight $1/\omega$ makes soft phonons count more: a phonon of half the frequency, with the same $|g|^2$, contributes twice as much to $\lambda_{\mathrm{ep}}$. Values range from about 0.4 in aluminium to about 1.6 in lead and 2 in H₃S. The characteristic phonon frequency of pairing is the logarithmic average

$$\begin{gathered}\ln\omega_{\log}=\frac{2}{\lambda_{\mathrm{ep}}}\\ \times\int_0^\infty\dd\omega\,\frac{\alpha^2F(\omega)}{\omega}\,\ln\omega .\end{gathered} \tag{A9.17}$$

By the weights $2\alpha^2F(\omega)/(\lambda_{\mathrm{ep}}\omega)$ integrate to 1, so $\omega_{\log}$ is a geometric mean of the phonon frequencies, each weighted by its share of $\lambda_{\mathrm{ep}}$; the unit chosen for $\omega$ inside the logarithm cancels. For narrow peaks at frequencies $\omega_i$ that contribute $\lambda_i$ each to $\lambda_{\mathrm{ep}}=\sum_i\lambda_i$, the average is $\ln\omega_{\log}=\sum_i(\lambda_i/\lambda_{\mathrm{ep}})\ln\omega_i$.

The McMillan–Allen–Dynes formula

McMillan (1968) solved Eliashberg's equations, described below, numerically, with the phonon spectrum of niobium as a model, and fitted the resulting critical temperatures with a formula of the BCS form; Allen and Dynes (1975), who tested it against many other spectra, gave its prefactor the form now used:12

$$\begin{gathered}\kB T_{\mathrm c}=\frac{\hbar\omega_{\log}}{1.2}\,\times\\ \exp\bigg[-\frac{1.04(1+\lambda_{\mathrm{ep}})}{\lambda_{\mathrm{ep}}-\mu^*(1+0.62\lambda_{\mathrm{ep}})}\bigg]\end{gathered} \tag{A9.18}$$
$\mu^*$
Coulomb pseudopotential: the dimensionless repulsion between the electrons of a pair, reduced by the delay of the attraction (a traditional name, unrelated to the chemical potential $\mu$); typically 0.10 to 0.15
$\omega_{\log}$
logarithmic average phonon frequency ; $\hbar\omega_{\log}$ in meV

Each part of can be read against the BCS result :

  • the prefactor $\hbar\omega_{\log}/1.2$ replaces $1.13\,\hbar\omega_{\mathrm{ph}}$, with the phonon frequency averaged as pairing needs;
  • for weak coupling, $\lambda_{\mathrm{ep}}\ll1$, the exponent tends to $-1.04/(\lambda_{\mathrm{ep}}-\mu^*)$: the net attraction $\lambda_{\mathrm{BCS}}$ is the phonon attraction minus the Coulomb repulsion;
  • the factor $1+\lambda_{\mathrm{ep}}$ is the mass enhancement of Section A.7: the coupling that pairs the electrons also makes them heavier and slower, which works against pairing and limits the growth of $T_{\mathrm c}$ at strong coupling;
  • $T_{\mathrm c}$ vanishes when the denominator does, for $\lambda_{\mathrm{ep}}\le\mu^*/(1-0.62\mu^*)$, which is 0.13 for $\mu^*=0.12$.

With $\mu^*=0.12$ and the model spectra of , aluminium ($\lambda_{\mathrm{ep}}\approx0.44$, $\hbar\omega_{\log}\approx25$ meV) gives 1.3 K against 1.2 K measured, and lead ($\lambda_{\mathrm{ep}}\approx1.6$, $\hbar\omega_{\log}\approx5.6$ meV) gives 7.4 K against 7.2 K. MgB₂ treated as isotropic gives 18 to 27 K for $\mu^*$ between 0.15 and 0.10, far below the measured 39 K, and H₃S gives about 150 K against about 200 K. The formula assumes an isotropic superconductor with a single gap, and $\mu^*$ is usually adjusted within 0.1 to 0.2. The review stresses that this makes $T_{\mathrm c}$ uncertain at moderate coupling, where the exponent is most sensitive.

The Coulomb pseudopotential comes from the repulsion between the two electrons of a pair: the electronic screened interaction $W^{\mathrm e}$ of Section A.5 between the pair states $(\bk,-\bk)$ and $(\bk',-\bk')$, averaged over the Fermi surface and multiplied by $N_{\mathrm F}$. This number, $\mu_{\mathrm C}$, is about 0.3 to 0.5. The repulsion acts on the electronic time scale, of order the inverse of the plasma frequency $\omega_{\mathrm p}$, while the attraction is delayed by a phonon period. This delay of one interaction with respect to the other is called retardation. Morel and Anderson (1962) showed that the repeated Coulomb scattering of the pair at energies between $\hbar\omega_{\mathrm{ph}}$ and the plasma energy $\hbar\omega_{\mathrm p}$ reduces the repulsion to13

$$\mu^*=\frac{\mu_{\mathrm C}}{1+\mu_{\mathrm C}\ln(\omega_{\mathrm p}/\omega_{\mathrm{ph}})} . \tag{A9.19}$$
$\mu_{\mathrm C}$
dimensionless Coulomb repulsion on the Fermi surface before the reduction by retardation: $N_{\mathrm F}$ times the average of the screened $W^{\mathrm e}$ between pair states
$\omega_{\mathrm p}$
electronic plasma frequency (Section A.5); $\hbar\omega_{\mathrm p}$ is about 10 eV in simple metals

Physical picture: why $\mu^*$ is small

The two electrons of a pair attract through the lattice without being at the same place at the same time: the second arrives after the first has left. Their instantaneous repulsion is largely avoided, and the logarithm counts the decades between the electronic and the phonon time scales. With $\mu_{\mathrm C}=0.4$, $\hbar\omega_{\mathrm p}=10$ eV and $\hbar\omega_{\mathrm{ph}}=50$ meV, $\ln(\omega_{\mathrm p}/\omega_{\mathrm{ph}})=5.3$ and $\mu^*=0.13$. This is retardation in the sense of the review: the frequency dependence of an interaction that is still electrostatic. The finite speed of light plays no role.

Beyond one number: Eliashberg theory

McMillan's formula compresses the coupling into three numbers. Eliashberg's theory (1960), which builds on Migdal's treatment of the electron–phonon self-energy (1958), keeps its full dependence on frequency and its variation over the Fermi surface. It is formulated with the finite-temperature (Matsubara) Green's functions of Section A.7, whose background box teaches the technique. Three facts from there are all this subsection needs:

  • the propagators are evaluated at imaginary frequencies, and an electron propagator only at the fermionic Matsubara frequencies $\omega^{\mathrm F}_p=(2p+1)\pi\kB T/\hbar$, with $p$ an integer; the difference of two of them is a bosonic frequency $\omega^{\mathrm B}_p=2p\pi\kB T/\hbar$;
  • results on the real frequency axis follow by analytic continuation, replacing $\ii\omega$ by $\omega+\ii\eta$, with $\eta$ a positive infinitesimal;
  • on the imaginary axis the free phonon propagator of Section A.5 is real and negative, $F^0_\nu(\bq,\ii\omega)=-\frac1\hbar\,\frac{2\omega_{\bq\nu}}{\omega^2+\omega^2_{\bq\nu}}$.

On that axis the coupling between two states on the Fermi surface is

The coupling is $N_{\mathrm F}|g|^2$ times minus the phonon propagator on the imaginary axis: the phonon-mediated interaction $W^{\mathrm{ph}}$ of Section A.5, projected onto pairs of states on the Fermi surface. At zero frequency its average is $\lambda_{\mathrm{ep}}$; at frequencies above those of the phonons it dies away. Eliashberg's equations determine, at each temperature, two functions for every state on the Fermi surface: the gap function $\Delta_{n\bk}(\ii\omega^{\mathrm F}_p)$ and a mass renormalization function $\mathcal Z_{n\bk}(\ii\omega^{\mathrm F}_p)$, which in the normal state at low frequency tends to $1+\lambda_{\mathrm{ep}}$. Each is a sum over all other states and all fermionic frequencies, weighted by the coupling at the difference of the two frequencies; the gap equation also contains the Coulomb repulsion, as $\mu^*$. $T_{\mathrm c}$ is the highest temperature at which a nonzero gap exists, and the gaps on the real frequency axis follow by analytic continuation.14

The isotropic Eliashberg equations

Average the gap and the renormalization over the Fermi surface, and write them at the fermionic frequencies as $\Delta_p=\Delta(\ii\omega^{\mathrm F}_p)$ and $\mathcal Z_p=\mathcal Z(\ii\omega^{\mathrm F}_p)$; write the averaged coupling at the difference of two of them as $\lambda_{pp'}=\lambda(\ii\omega^{\mathrm F}_p-\ii\omega^{\mathrm F}_{p'})$. Two coupled equations remain:

$$\begin{gathered}\mathcal Z_p=1+\frac{\pi\kB T}{\hbar\omega^{\mathrm F}_p}\sum_{p'}\frac{\hbar\omega^{\mathrm F}_{p'}\,\lambda_{pp'}}{\sqrt{(\hbar\omega^{\mathrm F}_{p'})^2+\Delta_{p'}^2}},\\ \mathcal Z_p\Delta_p=\pi\kB T\sum_{p'}\frac{\Delta_{p'}\,(\lambda_{pp'}-\mu^*)}{\sqrt{(\hbar\omega^{\mathrm F}_{p'})^2+\Delta_{p'}^2}} .\end{gathered}$$

The sums run up to a cutoff of several times the largest phonon frequency, and $\mu^*$ refers to that cutoff. In the normal state, $\Delta_p=0$, each term of the first sum becomes $\pm\lambda_{pp'}$, with the sign of $\omega^{\mathrm F}_{p'}$, and at low temperature and low frequency the sum gives $\mathcal Z\to1+\lambda_{\mathrm{ep}}$: $\mathcal Z$ is the inverse of the quasiparticle weight $Z^{\mathrm{qp}}$ of Section A.7, not the weight itself. At $T_{\mathrm c}$ the second equation, linear in $\Delta$ there, first has a nonzero solution. The anisotropic equations that the review gives replace $\lambda$ by $\lambda_{n\bk,m\bk'}$ and keep a gap for every state; they describe superconductors with several gaps.

For MgB₂, Margine and Giustino (2013) solved the anisotropic equations with couplings from Wannier interpolation. They found two distinct gaps at low temperature: about 7.5 to 9 meV on the sheets of the Fermi surface that come from the in-plane σ bonds of boron, and 1 to 2.5 meV on the sheets that come from its out-of-plane π bonds, both closing at $T_{\mathrm c}=50$ K against 39 K measured; the empirical $\mu^*$ accounts for part of the difference. A formula built for one isotropic gap cannot describe this, which is one reason why falls well short for MgB₂.15

Density-functional theory for superconductors (Lüders, Marques and co-workers, 2005) removes the empirical $\mu^*$. It generalizes the Hohenberg–Kohn theorem to three densities, the electron density, the density of Cooper pairs and the nuclear density, and maps the system onto noninteracting but superconducting Kohn–Sham electrons. Its gap equation resembles that of BCS theory, with kernels built from $g$, the phonons and the screened Coulomb interaction, so no adjustable $\mu^*$ is needed. The gap has no explicit frequency dependence; the delay of the attraction enters through the kernels.16

Integrals on the Fermi surface

In metals both the Boltzmann equation and the Eliashberg equations couple states on or near the Fermi surface, and their zone integrals can need up to $10^6$ wavevectors to converge. Besides Wannier interpolation (Section A.4), the review describes Allen's Fermi-surface harmonics, which trade three-dimensional zone integrals for one-dimensional integrals over energy. Any quantity that depends on the state is expanded as

$$X_{n\bk}=\sum_LX_L(E_{n\bk})\,Y_L(\bk),$$

where the functions $Y_L(\bk)$, labelled by an index $L$, are orthonormal on every surface of constant energy, with the density of states as the weight, and the $X_L(E)$ are the expansion coefficients. A linear equation over all states, such as the linearized Boltzmann equation, then becomes a small matrix equation in $L$ and the energy, provided a few harmonics suffice. Allen built the $Y_L$ from polynomials in the band velocity, without showing that the set is complete. Eiguren and Gurtubay (2014) made it complete by defining the harmonics as eigenfunctions of a Laplacian on the Fermi surface; for a circular Fermi line with constant speed they reduce to the Fourier modes of the angle around the circle.17

DFT underestimates band gaps, and a material with smaller gaps screens more: $\chi^0$ is too large, the screening too strong, and $g$ too small. The evidence that the review collects is scattered but consistent:19

  • in graphene and graphite, the GW approximation raises the coupling of the A1′ phonon at K and the E2g phonon at $\Gamma$ by almost 40%, and the GW slopes of the phonon dispersion near K agree with inelastic x-ray scattering;
  • in C₆₀, the hybrid functional PBE0, which mixes a fraction of exact exchange into DFT and opens the gaps, raises $\lambda_{\mathrm{ep}}$ by 42%, and GW by 48%;
  • in Ba₁₋ₓKₓBiO₃ the hybrid functional HSE triples $\lambda_{\mathrm{ep}}$, and then gives 31 K against 32 K measured; in β-ZrNCl $\lambda_{\mathrm{ep}}$ rises by 50% (18 K against 16 K), in MgB₂ only slightly;
  • in H₃S, PBE0 raises the predicted $T_{\mathrm c}$ from 201–217 K to 253–270 K;
  • in diamond, GW raises the zero-point renormalization of the gap by 40%, and in silicon by a similar amount, but in LiF, MgO and TiO₂ by only about 5%;
  • in MnO and CaCuO₂, DFT phonon energies are too low by up to 15 meV, and a Hubbard correction (LSDA+U) restores agreement; for a Cu–O stretching mode of CaCuO₂, 65.3 meV in DFT and 80.9 meV with the correction, against 80.1 meV measured.

Not every property suffers equally, as a scaling argument shows:

  1. The band renormalization of Section A.8 contains two screened vertices, so it scales as $\varepsilon_\infty^{-2}$.

    Each $g$ carries one factor $(\varepsilon^{\mathrm e})^{-1}$, which for a semiconductor is set by the electronic dielectric constant $\varepsilon_\infty$ of Section A.4.

  2. An adiabatic phonon frequency carries the screening only through a term that scales as $\varepsilon_\infty^{-1/2}$.

    The frequency is the square root of a force constant, and the screened part of the force constant scales as $\varepsilon_\infty^{-1}$, as in the LO–TO splitting of Section A.4.

  3. An error of 20% in $\varepsilon_\infty$ therefore means, to first order, an error of about 40% in the band renormalization and of about 10% in the phonon frequencies.

    Relative errors scale with the exponents: $2\times20\%$ and $\frac12\times20\%$.20

For a practitioner of GW, contains the same $(\varepsilon^{\mathrm e})^{-1}$ as the screened interaction $W$. Faber and co-workers (2015) showed that a "constant screening" approximation, which varies the Green's function $G$ in a frozen-phonon calculation but keeps the $W$ of the undistorted crystal, reproduces full GW couplings within 10% for diamond, graphene and C₆₀.

What Part A assumed

Four assumptions run through Part A. Part B relaxes each of them, at least in part:

  1. One interaction. Every interaction between charges is the instantaneous, longitudinal Coulomb interaction $v$, screened. Even phonon exchange, which pairs electrons in a superconductor, is a second channel of the same screened interaction, $W=W^{\mathrm e}+W^{\mathrm{ph}}$, .
  2. One kind of retardation. The only delay is the frequency dependence of screening: that of the electrons, $\varepsilon^{\mathrm e}(\omega)$, and the slow phonon propagator inside $W^{\mathrm{ph}}$, which makes $\mu^*$ small. The finite speed of light never appears.21
  3. Classical light. Photons enter, as in , as an external classical field that couples to the electrons through $\be_\lambda\cdot\hat{\mathbf v}$. The refractive index is an input, and the ions do not couple to light at all.
  4. Polar coupling added by hand. The Fröhlich vertex and the LO–TO splitting of Section A.4 are added analytically, from Born charges and the dielectric constant $\varepsilon_\infty$ computed separately.

Section X.1 shows that the first two assumptions are the limit $c\to\infty$ of one electrodynamic theory, in which light becomes a propagator and matter, electronic and ionic alike, a single self-energy.

Where these objects go next

In the EPP formalism of Part B the photon is a quantized field with its own propagator (Sections X.1 and B.4), not a prescribed potential. Electrons couple to it through their current, ions through their Born dipoles. Absorption then follows from the imaginary part of one photon self-energy with an electronic and an ionic part (Section B.5), and the dielectric function, hence $n_{\mathrm r}$, comes from the same object instead of being put in by hand (Section B.3). The ionic part contains what lacks: the one-phonon infrared absorption of a polar crystal near $\omega_{\mathrm{TO}}$. Phonon-assisted absorption would be a phonon correction inside the electronic part; the EPP manuscript does not work it out.

Transport and pairing should stay electrostatic. The electrodynamic corrections of Part B act at wavevectors inside the light cone, $q\lesssim\sqrt{\varepsilon_\infty}\,\omega_{\mathrm{LO}}/c\approx6\times10^{-5}$ Å⁻¹ in GaAs, while thermal carriers at 300 K have wavevectors of about 0.02 Å⁻¹ and scattering across the Fermi surface of a metal reaches twice the Fermi wavevector, 3.5 Å⁻¹ in aluminium. The exchange of transverse photons between moving electrons would add terms smaller by roughly the square of the ratio of the Fermi velocity to $c$ (our estimate). Part B does generalize the screened vertex , replacing $(\varepsilon^{\mathrm e})^{-1}v$ by the photon propagator dressed by the electrons (Section B.8).

Check yourself: three short questions

  1. Silicon at 296 K has its indirect gap at 1.12 eV. Where are the four thresholds of the edge due to the TA (18.4 meV) and TO (57.8 meV) phonons, and which of them keep their weight on cooling to 4 K?

    Answer. At $E^{\mathrm{qp}}_{\mathrm g}\mp\hbar\omega_\nu$, with the renormalized gap in the δ function: 1.062 and 1.102 eV (phonon absorption), 1.138 and 1.178 eV (phonon emission). At 4 K the absorption thresholds lose their weight, $n_{\mathrm{TA}}\approx7\times10^{-24}$ and $n_{\mathrm{TO}}$ far smaller, while emission keeps $n_\nu+1\approx1$. The gap itself rises to about 1.17 eV and carries the emission thresholds with it.

  2. A metal has $\lambda_{\mathrm{ep}}=0.5$ and $\hbar\omega_{\log}=20$ meV. Estimate $T_{\mathrm c}$ from for $\mu^*=0.10$ and for $\mu^*=0.15$.

    Answer. $\hbar\omega_{\log}/1.2\kB=193$ K. The exponent is $-1.56/0.369=-4.23$ for $\mu^*=0.10$ and $-1.56/0.304=-5.14$ for $\mu^*=0.15$, so $T_{\mathrm c}\approx2.8$ K and 1.1 K. Changing $\mu^*$ by 0.05 changes $T_{\mathrm c}$ by a factor of 2.5.

  3. Why does SERTA give the mobility of silicon almost exactly, but not that of GaAs?

    Answer. SERTA counts every collision as a full loss of the drift. In silicon the dominant collisions randomize the direction of motion, so this is nearly right and $\tau_{\mathrm{tr}}\approx\tau$. In GaAs the Fröhlich coupling, $|g|^2\propto1/q^2$, favours small-angle collisions, which shorten the lifetime but remove little of the drift, so $\tau_{\mathrm{tr}}\gg\tau$ and the full, iterative solution of is needed.
