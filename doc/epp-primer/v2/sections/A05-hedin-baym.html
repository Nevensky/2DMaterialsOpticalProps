Hedin–Baym: electrons and nuclei with one Coulomb interaction

Sections A.2–A.4 computed the electron–phonon coupling with density-functional theory. This section asks which exact theory those calculations approximate. For electrons and nuclei that interact only through the Coulomb interaction, Hedin and Baym derived a closed set of equations in which the screened interaction contains the lattice: an electron-screened part plus the exchange of phonons between two screened vertices. The vertex turns out to be the screened potential change of a phonon, the coupling that DFPT computes, and the phonons turn out to be nuclei that interact through the electron-screened Coulomb interaction.

What you need: the phonons of Section A.1, in particular the displacement operator ; the Bloch states, the electron operators and the matrix element $g_{mn\nu}(\bk,\bq)$ of Section A.2; the screened vertex and the electronic dielectric function of density-functional perturbation theory (DFPT) in Section A.3; and the Born charges, dielectric constants and Fröhlich coupling of Section A.4. You should be at ease with the Heisenberg picture of quantum mechanics. Background boxes teach the electron propagator and its poles, Dyson equations, and Hedin's equations with the GW approximation; a derivation box explains the functional derivatives they use.1

The picture: two screening clouds

The electron–phonon calculations of Sections A.2–A.4 rest on three simplifications. The coupling $g$ is computed from the Kohn–Sham potential, so it inherits the approximate exchange–correlation functional. The phonons are computed in the Born–Oppenheimer approximation of Section A.1, which assumes that the electrons follow the nuclei instantly; this is doubtful when an electronic excitation costs as little as a phonon, as in metals and doped semiconductors. And the electrons screen the potential of a moving nucleus as if the nucleus were frozen. Beyond these, electrons and phonons change each other: the phonons dress the electrons and the electrons dress the phonons, so an exact theory must be self-consistent.

The review answers with a field theory of electrons and nuclei, built with the functional-derivative method of Schwinger, in the form given by Baym (1961) and by Hedin and Lundqvist (1969). Its central object is the screened Coulomb interaction, and its central result is that the lattice lives inside it.

Physical picture: two screening clouds

Place a test charge inside a polar crystal such as GaAs. Within about a femtosecond the electrons rearrange around it and reduce its field by the electronic dielectric constant, $\varepsilon_\infty$$\,=10.9$. The interaction reduced in this way is the screened interaction of a GW calculation. If the charge stays in place for longer than a period of the optical phonons, about 0.1 ps, the ions shift as well, and the field drops further, to $1/\varepsilon_{\mathrm s}$ of its bare value, with the static dielectric constant $\varepsilon_{\mathrm s}=12.9$. A charge that moves or varies in time therefore carries a fast electronic cloud and a slow ionic one. The theory of this section puts both clouds into one screened interaction and treats the slow ionic cloud as a propagating quantum object: the phonon.

The plan of the section:

  1. write the Hamiltonian of electrons and nuclei, coupled by one instantaneous Coulomb interaction;
  2. see in three simple models what moving ions do to the interaction between electrons;
  3. define the electron propagator and its self-energy, and derive the equation for the screened interaction;
  4. split the screened interaction into an electronic part and a lattice part, and write the lattice part with phonons;
  5. derive the equation of motion of the phonons;
  6. collect everything into the closed set of Hedin–Baym equations.

One Hamiltonian, one instantaneous interaction

The electrons are identical fermions. They are described by a field operator $\hat\psi(\br)$, which removes an electron at the point $\br$, and its conjugate $\hat\psi^\dagger(\br)$, which adds one. In terms of the Bloch states $\psi_{n\bk}$ and the operators $c_{n\bk}$ of Section A.2, $\hat\psi(\br)=\sum_{n\bk}\psi_{n\bk}(\br)\,c_{n\bk}$. The electron density operator is $\hat n^{\rme}(\br)=\hat\psi^\dagger(\br)\hat\psi(\br)$. Spin is suppressed: every electronic operator carries an implied sum over the two spin directions, and a factor 2 is written where that sum is carried out.

The nuclei are treated differently. In a solid each nucleus stays near its own site, so the nuclei can be told apart and labelled by cell and basis atom, $\ell\kappa$, exactly as in Section A.1. Their equilibrium positions $\btau_{\ell\kappa}$ are external parameters, taken from experiment or from a structural relaxation. The dynamical variables are the displacement operators $\hat\bu_{\ell\kappa}$ and their conjugate momenta $\hat\bp_{\ell\kappa}$. Phonons, the quanta of the displacements, appear later. The charge of nucleus $\kappa$ is $Z_\kappa e$, with $Z_\kappa$ the atomic number, or the ionic valence when the core electrons are frozen into a pseudopotential.

It is convenient to count all charges in units of the electron's charge $-e$: an electron counts $+1$, a nucleus $-Z_\kappa$. The density of charge in these units is

$$\begin{aligned}\hat n&=\hat n^{\rme}+\hat n^{\ion},\\ \hat n^{\ion}&=-\sum_{\ell\kappa}Z_\kappa\,\delta(\br-\btau_{\ell\kappa}-\hat\bu_{\ell\kappa}),\end{aligned} \tag{A5.1}$$

and the charge density itself is $-e\hat n$. The nuclear part $\hat n^{\ion}$ is negative and depends on the displacements: its delta functions sit at the instantaneous positions of the nuclei. With this bookkeeping, $\int\dd^3r'\,v(\br-\br')\,\langle\hat n(\br')\rangle$ is the potential energy of an electron at $\br$ in the field of all charges, where $v(\br)=e^2/|\br|$ is the bare Coulomb interaction of Section A.3.

The Hamiltonian is the kinetic energy of the electrons and of the nuclei plus the Coulomb energy of every pair of charges:2

$$\begin{aligned}\hat H={}&\hat T^{\rme}+\hat T^{\ion}\\&+\frac12\int\dd^3r\,\dd^3r'\,v(\br-\br')\\&\quad\times\hat n^{\rme}(\br)\big[\hat n^{\rme}(\br')-\delta(\br-\br')\big]\\&+\int\dd^3r\,\hat n^{\rme}(\br)\,\hat V^{\mathrm{en}}(\br)\\&+\frac12\sum_{\ell\kappa\neq\ell'\kappa'}Z_\kappa Z_{\kappa'}\,v\big(\btau_{\ell\kappa}+\hat\bu_{\ell\kappa}\\&\qquad-\btau_{\ell'\kappa'}-\hat\bu_{\ell'\kappa'}\big).\end{aligned} \tag{A5.2}$$
$\hat T^{\rme}$
kinetic energy of the electrons, $-(\hbar^2/2m_{\mathrm e})\int\dd^3r\,\hat\psi^\dagger\nabla^2\hat\psi$, with $m_{\mathrm e}$ the electron mass
$\hat T^{\ion}$
kinetic energy of the nuclei, $\sum_{\ell\kappa}\hat\bp_{\ell\kappa}^2/2M_\kappa$, with $M_\kappa$ the mass of nucleus $\kappa$
$\hat V^{\mathrm{en}}(\br)$
potential energy of an electron in the field of all nuclei, $\int\dd^3r'\,v(\br-\br')\,\hat n^{\ion}(\br')$ $=-\sum_{\ell\kappa}Z_\kappa e^2/|\br-\btau_{\ell\kappa}-\hat\bu_{\ell\kappa}|$: the $V^{\mathrm{en}}$ of Section A.2 with the nuclei at their instantaneous positions, hence an operator (eV)

The three Coulomb lines are one interaction, $v$, between the electrons, between electrons and nuclei, and between the nuclei. The term $-\delta(\br-\br')$ removes the interaction of each electron with itself, and the nuclei, which are distinguishable, are counted pair by pair. Together the three lines are $\frac12\int\dd^3r\,\dd^3r'\,v(\br-\br')\,\hat n(\br)\hat n(\br')$ with all self-interactions removed. Nothing else couples the particles: there is no vector potential, no magnetic force and no photon.

The formulas below involve functions of two or more points in space and time. A number stands for a point and its time, $1\equiv(\br_1,t_1)$; an integral $\int\dd1$ runs over $\dd^3r_1\,\dd t_1$, and $\int\dd(34)$ is short for $\int\dd3\,\dd4$; and $1^+\equiv(\br_1,t_1+\eta)$ is the point $1$ an instant later, with $\eta\to0^+$. In this notation the Coulomb interaction is

$$v(12)=v(\br_1-\br_2)\,\delta(t_1-t_2). \tag{A5.3}$$

The delta function in time says that the interaction is instantaneous: a charge moved at time $t_1$ is felt everywhere at that same time. Every bare interaction line in this section is this one; Part A therefore describes only the electrostatic, longitudinal field. The screened interactions built from it below are not instantaneous, because the screening clouds take time to form.

Moving ions in three simple models

Before the general theory, three models show what the motion of the nuclei does to the interaction between electrons. They share three quantities. The screened interaction $W$ is the potential energy that an electron feels because of a unit charge of the electron's sign at another point, including the response of all other charges. $W^{\rme}$ is the same quantity when only the electrons respond and the nuclei stay at their sites. Their difference, $W^{\ph}=W-W^{\rme}$, is what the moving nuclei add. The precise definitions follow in the subsections on screening.3

Two electrons and a row of ions

Take a row of ions of charge $Ze$ and mass $M$, a distance $a$ apart. Each ion is held near its site by a spring of frequency $\omega_0$ and moves independently of the others: a set of Einstein oscillators, the simplest model of an optical branch without dispersion. Two electrons move along a line parallel to the row. Electrons and ions interact through the electron-screened interaction $W^{\rme}$, here in the screened Coulomb form $W^{\rme}(r)=(e^2/r)\,\ee^{-k_{\mathrm{TF}}r}$: the other electrons cut the interaction off beyond a screening length $1/k_{\mathrm{TF}}$, where $k_{\mathrm{TF}}$ is the Thomas–Fermi wavevector of Section A.3 (about 1 Å⁻¹ in a simple metal).

Electron 1 at $x_1$ attracts ion $\ell$ with a force $\bF_\ell(x_1)$. Let the charge at $x_1$ oscillate in time as $\cos\omega t$; any time dependence, such as an electron passing by, is a superposition of such components. Ion $\ell$ then moves like a driven oscillator, with displacement $\bF_\ell(x_1)/\big[M(\omega_0^2-\omega^2)\big]$: in phase with the force below resonance and against it above. When ion $\ell$ moves by $\bu_\ell$, the interaction energy of electron 2 with that ion changes by $-\bF_\ell(x_2)\cdot\bu_\ell$, because the force on the ion is minus the gradient of their interaction energy with respect to the ion's position. Summing over the ions gives the extra potential energy of electron 2 per unit charge of electron 1:

$$\begin{aligned}&W^{\ph}(x_2,x_1;\omega)\\&\quad=\sum_\ell\frac{\bF_\ell(x_2)\cdot\bF_\ell(x_1)}{M\,(\omega^2-\omega_0^2)} .\end{aligned} \tag{A5.4}$$
$x_1$, $x_2$
positions of the two electrons along the row (Å)
$\bF_\ell(x)$
force on ion $\ell$ exerted by an electron at $x$, through the screened interaction (eV/Å)
$\omega_0$
frequency of each ion's spring; $\omega$ is the frequency at which the charge at $x_1$ oscillates

The formula has three factors: the force of electron 1 on an ion, the displacement of the ion per unit force at frequency $\omega$, and the change of the energy of electron 2 per unit displacement of the ion. This anatomy, a coupling at each end and an oscillator in between, is the anatomy of the general result .

The sign follows from the driven oscillator. When electron 2 is close to electron 1, the two forces on a nearby ion point the same way and the numerator is positive. Below resonance, $\omega<\omega_0$, the ions move toward electron 1 and gather positive charge near it, so $W^{\ph}<0$: the moving ions make the electrons attract. Above resonance the ions move against the force and $W^{\ph}>0$. For $\omega\gg\omega_0$ the ions cannot follow and $W^{\ph}\to0$. Whether the total $W=W^{\rme}+W^{\ph}$ is attractive depends on the size of $W^{\ph}$ against the screened repulsion. With the parameters of (sodium mass, $\hbar\omega_0=15$ meV, illustrative), the ions reduce the static repulsion at 2 Å from 0.97 to 0.54 eV, and at $\omega=0.8\,\omega_0$ they reverse it, to $-0.24$ eV.

A polar insulator

At long wavelengths a crystal acts as a homogeneous medium. A test charge whose density varies as $\ee^{\ii(\bq\cdot\br-\omega t)}$ then creates a potential reduced by the dielectric function $\varepsilon(\bq,\omega)$, so that $W(\bq,\omega)=v(q)/\varepsilon(\bq,\omega)$, with $v(q)=4\pi e^2/q^2$. The electrons contribute their own dielectric function, $\varepsilon^{\rme}$ of Section A.3; the ions add a term, found in three steps for a cubic crystal with two atoms per cell:

  1. In an optical mode at $\bq\to0$ the two sublattices move against each other, with relative displacement $\bu=\bu_1-\bu_2$. Each cell then carries the dipole $eZ^*\bu$, with $Z^*$ the Born effective charge of Section A.4, and responds to the macroscopic electric field $\bE$ as one oscillator of reduced mass $M_{\mathrm r}$: $M_{\mathrm r}\big(\ddot\bu+\omega_0^2\bu\big)=eZ^*\bE$.

    The frequency $\omega_0$ is that of the short-range restoring force alone, without the macroscopic field: the transverse optical frequency $\omega_{\mathrm{TO}}$, since a transverse mode creates no macroscopic field (Section A.4). The Born charge already includes the electrons that follow the ions, so the force on the cell is the Born charge times the macroscopic field.

  2. For a field that oscillates at $\omega$, $\bu=eZ^*\bE/\big[M_{\mathrm r}(\omega_0^2-\omega^2)\big]$, and the ionic polarization is $\bP^{\ion}=eZ^*\bu/\OV$.

    The polarization is the dipole per unit volume, one dipole per cell of volume $\OV$.

  3. The dielectric function is defined by $\varepsilon\,\bE=\varepsilon^{\rme}\bE+4\pi\bP^{\ion}$, which gives

    $$\begin{gathered}\varepsilon=\varepsilon^{\rme}+\frac{(\omega_{\mathrm p}^{\ion})^2}{\omega_0^2-\omega^2},\\ (\omega_{\mathrm p}^{\ion})^2=\frac{4\pi e^2Z^{*2}}{M_{\mathrm r}\OV}.\end{gathered} \tag{A5.5}$$
    The electrons' polarization is contained in $\varepsilon^{\rme}$; the ions add $4\pi\bP^{\ion}$ (Gaussian units).

$\omega_{\mathrm p}^{\ion}$ is the ionic plasma frequency: the frequency at which the ionic charge would oscillate if only the Coulomb force acted on it. For free ions of charge $Ze$ and mass $M$, one per volume $\OV$, it is $(\omega_{\mathrm p}^{\ion})^2=4\pi Z^2e^2/(M\OV)$; jellium below uses this form.4 For the polar crystal, $\omega_0=\omega_{\mathrm{TO}}$, and the electrons, whose gap lies far above the phonon energies, screen statically: $\varepsilon^{\rme}=$ $\varepsilon_\infty$. Then can be written as

$$\begin{aligned}\varepsilon(\omega)&=\varepsilon_\infty\,\frac{\omega_{\mathrm{LO}}^2-\omega^2}{\omega_{\mathrm{TO}}^2-\omega^2},\\ \omega_{\mathrm{LO}}^2&=\omega_{\mathrm{TO}}^2+\frac{(\omega_{\mathrm p}^{\ion})^2}{\varepsilon_\infty},\\ \varepsilon_{\mathrm s}&=\varepsilon(0)=\varepsilon_\infty\,\frac{\omega_{\mathrm{LO}}^2}{\omega_{\mathrm{TO}}^2}.\end{aligned} \tag{A5.6}$$

The second relation is the LO–TO splitting of Section A.4, , and the third is the Lyddane–Sachs–Teller relation between the static dielectric constant $\varepsilon_{\mathrm s}$ and the two frequencies $\omega_{\mathrm{LO}}$, $\omega_{\mathrm{TO}}$. With $W^{\rme}=v/\varepsilon_\infty$ and $W=v/\varepsilon(\omega)$, the lattice part follows in one line: $1/\varepsilon-1/\varepsilon_\infty=(\omega_{\mathrm{LO}}^2-\omega_{\mathrm{TO}}^2)/[\varepsilon_\infty(\omega^2-\omega_{\mathrm{LO}}^2)]$, and by the Lyddane–Sachs–Teller relation $(\omega_{\mathrm{LO}}^2-\omega_{\mathrm{TO}}^2)/\varepsilon_\infty=(1/\varepsilon_\infty-1/\varepsilon_{\mathrm s})\,\omega_{\mathrm{LO}}^2$. So

$$\begin{aligned}&W^{\rme}=\frac{v(q)}{\varepsilon_\infty},\qquad W=\frac{v(q)}{\varepsilon(\omega)},\\&W^{\ph}=v(q)\Big(\frac1{\varepsilon_\infty}-\frac1{\varepsilon_{\mathrm s}}\Big)\\&\qquad\quad\times\frac{\omega_{\mathrm{LO}}^2}{\omega^2-\omega_{\mathrm{LO}}^2}.\end{aligned} \tag{A5.7}$$

Four features matter.

  • A pole at LO, a zero at TO. $W$ is the interaction between charges, which acts through the longitudinal field, so it resonates with the longitudinal mode: its pole is at $\omega_{\mathrm{LO}}$, where $\varepsilon=0$, and it vanishes at $\omega_{\mathrm{TO}}$, where $\varepsilon$ diverges.
  • The lattice screens statically. At $\omega=0$, $W=v/\varepsilon_{\mathrm s}$ is smaller than $W^{\rme}=v/\varepsilon_\infty$, by 15 % in GaAs ($\varepsilon_{\mathrm s}=12.9$) and by 70 % in MgO ($\varepsilon_\infty=2.96$, $\varepsilon_{\mathrm s}=9.7$).
  • Attraction below LO. For $\omega<\omega_{\mathrm{LO}}$, $W^{\ph}<0$, as in the row of ions: the ionic cloud reduces the repulsion. Between TO and LO, in the Reststrahlen band, even the total $W$ is negative.
  • Two spectra. The energy lost by a fast charge, $-\mathrm{Im}\,(1/\varepsilon)$, peaks at $\omega_{\mathrm{LO}}$, while the infrared absorption, $\mathrm{Im}\,\varepsilon$, peaks at $\omega_{\mathrm{TO}}$.

The residue of the pole of in $\omega$ is $v(q)(1/\varepsilon_\infty-1/\varepsilon_{\mathrm s})\,\omega_{\mathrm{LO}}/2$, since $\omega^2-\omega_{\mathrm{LO}}^2\simeq2\omega_{\mathrm{LO}}(\omega-\omega_{\mathrm{LO}})$ near the pole. It turns out to be $\OV/\hbar$ times the square of Fröhlich's matrix element: a later subsection shows that $W^{\ph}$ is Fröhlich's coupling squared times the propagator of an LO phonon.5

Jellium with mobile ions

A simple metal such as sodium can be modelled as jellium: an electron gas of uniform density $n^{\rme}_0$ in a uniform positive background. Here the background consists of ions that can move, with $Z$ electrons per ion and one ion per volume $\OV=Z/n^{\rme}_0$. Two frequencies set the scales. The electron gas displaced against the ions oscillates at the electronic plasma frequency $\omega_{\mathrm p}$; the ions displaced against the electrons, with nothing else to hold them, would oscillate at the ionic plasma frequency:

$$\begin{gathered}\omega_{\mathrm p}^2=\frac{4\pi n^{\rme}_0e^2}{m_{\mathrm e}},\\ (\omega_{\mathrm p}^{\ion})^2=\frac{4\pi Z^2e^2}{M\OV}.\end{gathered} \tag{A5.8}$$

In sodium ($Z=1$, $M=22.99$ amu, $\OV=37.7$ Å³), $\hbar\omega_{\mathrm p}=6.05$ eV and $\hbar\omega_{\mathrm p}^{\ion}=29.6$ meV. Since $n^{\rme}_0=Z/\OV$, their ratio is $(Zm_{\mathrm e}/M)^{1/2}=0.005$: the mass ratio separates the electronic and ionic time scales.

At phonon frequencies the electrons cross a wavelength faster than one period, $\omega\ll v_{\mathrm F}q$, with $v_{\mathrm F}$ the Fermi velocity, so they screen statically. The Thomas–Fermi model gives $\varepsilon^{\rme}(q)=1+k_{\mathrm{TF}}^2/q^2$, with $k_{\mathrm{TF}}^2=6\pi n^{\rme}_0e^2/E_{\mathrm F}=4k_{\mathrm F}/(\pi a_0)$ in terms of the Fermi energy $E_{\mathrm F}$, the Fermi wavevector $k_{\mathrm F}$ and the Bohr radius $a_0=0.529$ Å ($k_{\mathrm{TF}}=1.49$ Å⁻¹ in sodium). The ions are free, $\omega_0=0$ in :

$$\varepsilon(q,\omega)=1+\frac{k_{\mathrm{TF}}^2}{q^2}-\frac{(\omega_{\mathrm p}^{\ion})^2}{\omega^2}. \tag{A5.9}$$

Writing $\varepsilon=\varepsilon^{\rme}(q)\big[1-\omega_{\mathrm{LA}}^2(q)/\omega^2\big]$ and subtracting $W^{\rme}=v/\varepsilon^{\rme}$ gives

$$\begin{aligned}W&=\frac{v(q)}{\varepsilon^{\rme}(q)}\,\frac{\omega^2}{\omega^2-\omega_{\mathrm{LA}}^2(q)},\\ W^{\ph}&=\frac{v(q)}{\varepsilon^{\rme}(q)}\,\frac{\omega_{\mathrm{LA}}^2(q)}{\omega^2-\omega_{\mathrm{LA}}^2(q)},\\ \omega_{\mathrm{LA}}^2(q)&=\frac{(\omega_{\mathrm p}^{\ion})^2}{\varepsilon^{\rme}(q)}.\end{aligned} \tag{A5.10}$$

The pole $\omega_{\mathrm{LA}}(q)$ is the frequency of the one branch of jellium, a longitudinal one; transverse waves of a uniform charge meet no restoring force. Electronic screening turns the ionic plasma oscillation into sound. At small $q$, $\varepsilon^{\rme}\simeq k_{\mathrm{TF}}^2/q^2$ and

$$\begin{aligned}\omega_{\mathrm{LA}}(q)&\simeq v_{\mathrm s}q,\\ v_{\mathrm s}&=\frac{\omega_{\mathrm p}^{\ion}}{k_{\mathrm{TF}}}=v_{\mathrm F}\Big(\frac{Zm_{\mathrm e}}{3M}\Big)^{1/2},\end{aligned} \tag{A5.11}$$

the Bohm–Staver sound velocity $v_{\mathrm s}$. It is 3.0 km/s for sodium, close to the measured value, and 9.1 km/s for aluminium, against a measured 6.4 km/s: jellium ignores the lattice and the ion cores. The second form follows from $k_{\mathrm{TF}}^2=6\pi n^{\rme}_0e^2/E_{\mathrm F}$ and $E_{\mathrm F}=m_{\mathrm e}v_{\mathrm F}^2/2$.

Below $\omega_{\mathrm{LA}}(q)$ the total interaction is negative: two electrons that exchange less energy than a phonon attract each other, because the slow ionic cloud overscreens them. For $\omega\to0$, $W\to0$: free ions screen a static charge completely. This frequency-dependent attraction is the effective interaction of Bardeen and Pines (1955), and it is the pairing interaction of superconductors (Section A.9). With the full Lindhard dielectric function in place of Thomas–Fermi, the dispersion acquires the Kohn anomaly at $q=2k_{\mathrm F}$ (Section A.6).6

In all three models $W=W^{\rme}+W^{\ph}$, and $W^{\ph}$ has one anatomy: a squared coupling times the propagator of an oscillator, with a pole at a frequency of the ions. For the Einstein ions it is their spring frequency $\omega_0$; in the two media, where the ions interact through their own field, it is the frequency of their longitudinal motion, $\omega_{\mathrm{LO}}$ or $\omega_{\mathrm{LA}}(q)$. The rest of the section shows that this structure is exact.

The electron propagator and its self-energy

The theory is built around the time-ordered electron propagator, or Green's function, in the exact ground state $|0\rangle$ of the coupled electrons and nuclei, at zero temperature:

$$G(12)=-\frac{\ii}{\hbar}\,\langle\mathcal T\,\hat\psi(1)\,\hat\psi^\dagger(2)\rangle . \tag{A5.12}$$
$\langle\cdots\rangle$
expectation value in the ground state $|0\rangle$
$\hat\psi(1)$
the field operator in the Heisenberg picture, $\ee^{\ii\hat H\,t_1/\hbar}\hat\psi(\br_1)\,\ee^{-\ii\hat H\,t_1/\hbar}$
$\mathcal T$
time ordering: puts the later operator to the left, with a minus sign when two fermion operators are exchanged
$G(12)$

For $t_1>t_2$, $\ii\hbar G(12)$ is the amplitude for an electron added at $\br_2$ to be found at $\br_1$ a time $t_1-t_2$ later; for $t_1<t_2$ it describes a hole. Its poles in frequency are the energies needed to add or remove an electron: the band energies, shifted and broadened by the interactions. Every electron–phonon renormalization of Sections A.7 and A.8 is read off $G$.

The electron propagator and its poles skip if familiar

For independent electrons in Bloch states the propagator is diagonal in $n\bk$. An empty state ($E_{n\bk}>\mu$) gives, for $t>0$, $G^0_{n\bk}(t)=-(\ii/\hbar)\,\ee^{-\ii E_{n\bk}t/\hbar}$, and its Fourier transform is

$$G^0_{n\bk}(\omega)=\frac{1}{\hbar\omega-E_{n\bk}+\ii\eta\,\mathrm{sgn}(E_{n\bk}-\mu)} ,$$

where the sign of the infinitesimal $\eta$ comes from the time ordering: an occupied state ($E_{n\bk}<\mu$, a hole) propagates backward in time, and its pole lies just above the real axis. Either way, the pole sits at the band energy $E_{n\bk}$, measured with the chemical potential $\mu$ as the dividing line.

Interactions change two things. The poles move, because adding an electron now also rearranges the others; and they leave the real axis, because the added electron can decay into other excitations. An excitation that lives for a time $\tau$ has its pole at $\hbar\omega=E-\ii\hbar/2\tau$, since its amplitude decays as $\ee^{-t/2\tau}$ and its probability as $\ee^{-t/\tau}$. Poles close to the real axis are quasiparticles; the rest of the spectral weight forms a broad background or satellites (Section A.7).

The retarded propagator describes the causal response to a perturbation: it vanishes for $t_1<t_2$, and all its poles lie below the real axis, $\hbar\omega\to\hbar\omega+\ii\eta$ for every state. At zero temperature the time-ordered and retarded functions share their real parts and differ only in the sign of the imaginary part below $\mu$, so a formula derived for one converts into the other. Field theory uses the time-ordered function because it is the one with simple diagram rules.

$G$ follows from a simpler propagator through a Dyson equation:7

$$\begin{aligned}&G=G^0+G^0\Sigma G,\\&G(12)=G^0(12)\\&\;+\int\dd(34)\,G^0(13)\Sigma(34)G(42).\end{aligned} \tag{A5.13}$$
$G^0$
propagator of one electron moving in the static total potential $V^{\mathrm{tot}}$ of below, the Hartree potential of all charges
$\Sigma(12)$
the electron self-energy: everything beyond that potential, namely exchange, correlation and the coupling to the moving nuclei

In the Bloch basis, with $\Sigma$ diagonal, reads $G_{n\bk}(\omega)=1/[\hbar\omega-E^0_{n\bk}-\Sigma_{n\bk}(\omega)]$, where $E^0_{n\bk}$ is a band energy of the reference propagator: the real part of $\Sigma_{n\bk}$ shifts the pole and the imaginary part gives its lifetime. In practice the reference is often the Kohn–Sham band structure of Section A.2, whose potential already contains the exchange–correlation potential $V^{\mathrm{xc}}$; $\Sigma$ is then replaced by $\Sigma-V^{\mathrm{xc}}$.

Dyson equations skip if familiar

Let an electron propagate freely, and let some process scatter it from point 4 to point 3 with amplitude $\Sigma(34)$. The full propagator from 2 to 1 is the free one, plus free propagation from 2 to 4, one scattering and free propagation from 3 to 1, plus two scatterings, and so on:

$$\begin{aligned}G&=G^0+G^0\Sigma G^0+G^0\Sigma G^0\Sigma G^0+\dots\\&=G^0+G^0\Sigma\big(G^0+G^0\Sigma G^0+\dots\big)\\&=G^0+G^0\Sigma G,\end{aligned}$$

where every product is an integral over the internal points, as written out in . The series is geometric, and in the Bloch basis, with $\Sigma$ diagonal, it sums to $1/(\hbar\omega-E^0_{n\bk}-\Sigma_{n\bk})$, since $G^0_{n\bk}=1/(\hbar\omega-E^0_{n\bk})$. The self-energy must contain only processes that cannot be cut in two by cutting a single $G^0$: such processes are irreducible. A reducible piece is already a repetition of irreducible ones and would be counted twice.

The same structure returns for every propagator in this primer: the screened interaction $W$, built from the bare $v$ and the polarizability, below; the phonon propagator in Section A.6; the photon propagator in Part B. In Feynman diagrams a propagator is a line, a self-energy is a blob, a product is a chain, and the internal points are integrated over.

A probe that acts on every charge

To find $\Sigma$, the review follows Schwinger. Add a weak external potential energy $V^{\mathrm{ext}}(\br,t)$ that acts on every charge, through the term $\int\dd^3r\,\hat n(\br)\,V^{\mathrm{ext}}(\br,t)$ in the Hamiltonian; follow how everything responds to it; and set it to zero at the end. Because $\hat n$ contains electrons and nuclei, two quantities include the nuclei from the start. The first is the total potential, the potential energy of an electron in the mean field of all charges plus the probe:

$$\begin{aligned}V^{\mathrm{tot}}(1)={}&V^{\mathrm{ext}}(1)\\&+\int\dd2\,v(12)\,\langle\hat n(2)\rangle .\end{aligned} \tag{A5.14}$$

The second is the inverse dielectric function, which says how much of a change of the probe survives in the total potential:8

$$\varepsilon^{-1}(12)=\frac{\delta V^{\mathrm{tot}}(1)}{\delta V^{\mathrm{ext}}(2)} . \tag{A5.15}$$

Here $\delta/\delta V^{\mathrm{ext}}(2)$ is a functional derivative: the change of the left side per unit change of $V^{\mathrm{ext}}$ at the single space-time point $2$. Since the nuclei respond to the probe as well, $\varepsilon^{-1}$ contains the screening by the lattice together with the screening by the electrons. The third ingredient is the vertex function,

$$\Lambda(123)=-\frac{\delta G^{-1}(12)}{\delta V^{\mathrm{tot}}(3)} , \tag{A5.16}$$

where $G^{-1}$ is the inverse of $G$ as an integral kernel, $\int\dd2\,G(12)\,G^{-1}(23)=\delta(13)$. The vertex says how the propagator responds to a change of the total potential at point 3. For an electron that responds only to the potential at its own position it would be $\Lambda(123)=\delta(12)\delta(13)$; the corrections describe how the change of the potential also rearranges the other electrons. With these definitions the equation of motion of $G$ takes the Dyson form , with

$$\begin{aligned}&\Sigma(12)=\ii\hbar\int\dd(34)\,G(13)\\&\qquad\times\Lambda(324)\,W(41^+),\\&W(12)=\int\dd3\,\varepsilon^{-1}(13)\,v(32).\end{aligned} \tag{A5.17}$$

Hedin's equations, the RPA and GW skip if familiar

For electrons in the field of clamped nuclei, Hedin (1965) wrote the exact many-electron problem as five coupled relations: $G$ from $\Sigma$ by the Dyson equation ; $\Sigma=\ii\hbar\,G\Lambda W$; the polarizability $\chi^{\mathrm{irr}}=-2\ii\hbar\,GG\Lambda$; $W=v+v\chi^{\mathrm{irr}}W$; and the vertex $\Lambda=1+(\delta\Sigma/\delta G)\,GG\Lambda$. Each quantity is built from the next one around a loop, often drawn as a pentagon.

Start the loop from $\Sigma=0$. The vertex is then $\Lambda(123)=\delta(12)\delta(13)$; the polarizability is the bubble $\chi^{\mathrm{irr}}(12)=-2\ii\hbar\,G(12)G(21^+)$, which with Kohn–Sham propagators is the independent-particle response $\chi^0$ of Section A.3, the random-phase approximation (RPA); $W$ is the RPA screened interaction; and $\Sigma(12)=\ii\hbar\,G(12)W(21^+)$ is the GW approximation, named after its two factors. GW codes compute quasiparticle band structures in this way, and the Bethe–Salpeter equation for excitons uses the same $W$. Corrections to $\Lambda$ beyond the first term add exchange and correlation to the screening and to the self-energy.

The frequency dependence of the screening is what the review calls retardation, following Bardeen and Pines (1955) and Baym (1961). Hedin and Lundqvist replaced $(\varepsilon^{\rme})^{-1}(\omega)$ by its static value, which amounts to the Born–Oppenheimer approximation for the vertex. In insulators the difference is small, because the electronic excitations cost much more than a phonon. In metals the screening of a slow lattice wave is not static; Section A.6 shows the consequences for the phonons.17

Which "retardation"

Three ideas share the word. A retarded function is a causal response, with $\omega+\ii\eta$. The review's retardation effects are the frequency dependence of the electronic screening: the electrons need time to respond. The EPP manuscript's retardation is the finite speed of light. In this section the speed of light is infinite, because $v(12)$ is instantaneous; this primer calls the second idea dynamical screening, and Section X.1 takes up the third.

Both ends of the phonon line in carry screened vertices, since an electron feels the phonon through the screened potential and the phonon is pushed by the screened field of the electron. This matters later. The phonon self-energy of Section A.6 turns out to contain one bare and one screened vertex, and using two screened vertices there would count the screening twice.

The polar insulator again: Fröhlich's coupling

The general result reproduces the polar model of the first part, and explains its residue, in three steps:

  1. At long wavelength in a polar crystal, the vertex of the LO phonon is dominated by the macroscopic potential of its dipole wave, which is a plane wave: $\mathcal V_{\bq,\mathrm{LO}}(\br)\simeq g^{\mathrm{lr}}(\bq)\,\ee^{\ii\bq\cdot\br}$, with $g^{\mathrm{lr}}$ the long-range vertex of Section A.4 in its isotropic, one-mode form, Fröhlich's , whose modulus depends only on $q=|\bq|$.

    The dipole wave carries the polarization charge density $-\ii\bq\cdot\bP^{\ion}$, whose potential is $4\pi/q^2$ times that charge density: for a longitudinal wave it grows as $1/q$ and dominates every other part of the vertex as $q\to0$. The electrons screen it with $\varepsilon_\infty$.

  2. Insert into :

    $$\begin{aligned}&W^{\ph}(\br_1,\br_2;\omega)\\&\;=\frac1{N_\ell}\sum_\bq|g^{\mathrm{lr}}(q)|^2F^0_{\mathrm{LO}}(\omega)\,\ee^{\ii\bq\cdot(\br_1-\br_2)} .\end{aligned}$$
    The second vertex is $\mathcal V_{-\bq,\mathrm{LO}}=\mathcal V^*_{\bq,\mathrm{LO}}$ (static screening), so $g^{\mathrm{lr}}(-\bq)=g^{\mathrm{lr}}(\bq)^*$; and at long wavelength the LO propagator has its pole at $\omega_{\mathrm{LO}}$.

  3. Read off the Fourier component per unit volume: $W^{\ph}(\bq,\omega)=\OV\,|g^{\mathrm{lr}}(q)|^2\,F^0_{\mathrm{LO}}(\omega)$.

Phonons are nuclei interacting through $W^{\rme}$

The phonon propagator needs an equation of its own. Its content is Newton's law for the nuclei: mass times acceleration equals the Coulomb force of all other charges. That force has to be expressed through the quantities of the theory. Take two different nuclei, $\ell\kappa$ and $\ell'\kappa'$, and move the second one:

  1. Displacing nucleus $\ell'\kappa'$ by $\bu_{\ell'\kappa'}(t')$ adds a point dipole to the nuclear charge, $\delta n^{\ion}(\br,t')=Z_{\kappa'}u_{\ell'\kappa'\beta}(t')\,\partial_\beta\delta(\br-\btau_{\ell'\kappa'})$.

    This is the linear term of .

  2. Its potential, screened by the electrons, is

    $$\begin{aligned}&\delta V^{\mathrm{tot}}(\br,t)=-Z_{\kappa'}\int\dd t'\\&\;\times\partial'_\beta W^{\rme}(\br,\btau_{\ell'\kappa'};t-t')\,u_{\ell'\kappa'\beta}(t'),\end{aligned}$$

    where $\partial'_\beta$ acts on the second argument.

    By step 3 of the derivation of , the potential of a nuclear charge with its screening cloud is $W^{\rme}$ acting on that charge; an integration by parts moves the derivative from the delta function to $W^{\rme}$. The cloud forms over time, so the potential at $t$ depends on the displacement at earlier times $t'$.

  3. Nucleus $\ell\kappa$, of charge $-Z_\kappa$ in units of $-e$, has the energy $-Z_\kappa\,\delta V^{\mathrm{tot}}(\btau_{\ell\kappa},t)$, so the force on it is $F_{\ell\kappa\alpha}=Z_\kappa\,\partial_\alpha\delta V^{\mathrm{tot}}(\btau_{\ell\kappa},t)$.

    A force is minus the gradient of the energy with respect to the position of the particle.

  4. Compare with the force constants of Section A.1, whose force law is $F_{\ell\kappa\alpha}$$\,=-\sum$$\Phi$$\,u$. At frequency $\omega$ the convolution in time becomes a product, and the force constant between the two nuclei is $Z_\kappa Z_{\kappa'}\,\partial_\alpha\partial'_\beta W^{\rme}(\btau_{\ell\kappa},\btau_{\ell'\kappa'};\omega)$.

    Steps 2 and 3 give $F_{\ell\kappa\alpha}=-Z_\kappa Z_{\kappa'}\int\dd t'\,\partial_\alpha\partial'_\beta W^{\rme}(t-t')\,u_{\ell'\kappa'\beta}(t')$.

  5. The block of a nucleus with itself follows from the sum rule. When the whole crystal is moved slowly, $\omega\to0$, no force may arise, so each row of the static force constants sums to zero, as in . This fixes the static part of the on-site block as minus the sum of the static couplings to all other nuclei.

    The on-site block also contains the frequency-dependent drag of a nucleus's own screening cloud. Baym obtained the complete expression from the equation of motion of the displacements, with an external force on the nuclei as the probe; the sum rule fixes the part that comes from the static charge distribution.

The equations of this section now close on themselves. Written symbolically, with products standing for integrals over internal points, they are

The electronic equations are Hedin's (1965), the nuclear ones Baym's (1961), and the review collects them as the Hedin–Baym equations. shows how they feed each other.23

Three properties make the set the reference for everything that follows.

  • It is not perturbative. The equations fix the exact propagators self-consistently; nothing is expanded in powers of a coupling constant.
  • It does not assume Born–Oppenheimer. $\Phi(\omega)$ and the vertex keep their frequency dependence, so the same equations cover insulators, doped semiconductors and metals.
  • It contains Hedin's equations. Setting $F=0$ removes $W^{\ph}$ and the smearing of the nuclei, and what remains is Hedin's equations for clamped nuclei, the framework of GW. With the nuclei free, electrons and nuclei meet in only three places: through $\varepsilon^{\rme}$ and $W^{\rme}$, which build the vertex $\mathcal V$ of $W^{\ph}$ and the force constants $\Phi(\omega)$; through $W^{\ph}$ in $\Sigma$; and through $\langle\hat n^{\ion}\rangle$ in $V^{\mathrm{tot}}$. The set contains no separate coupling constant: the coupling is the electronic screening of the nuclear charge. This is why the review calls the electronic dielectric function "absolutely central".

What the Hedin–Baym equations leave out

The set is harmonic: it keeps the expansion to second order, and it keeps the one-phonon term inside $\chi^{\ion}$. Going beyond the harmonic approximation is possible in principle but leads to a formalism of forbidding complexity (Gillis, 1970). The set is written at zero temperature; finite temperatures need the Matsubara formalism, and Sections A.7 and A.8 use its results. The interaction is the instantaneous Coulomb interaction, so there are no transverse photons and the speed of light is infinite. Finally, nobody solves the set as it stands: Sections A.6–A.8 introduce the approximations of practice, namely the GW vertex, static screening and DFPT phonons.

How the review writes these objects

The review works in SI units with spin labels, writes the probes as $\varphi$, $J$ and $\mathbf F_{\kappa p}$, the polarizations as $P$ and $P_{\rm e}$, the vertex as $\Gamma$, and the screened interactions as $W_{\rm e}$ and $W_{\rm ph}$.
