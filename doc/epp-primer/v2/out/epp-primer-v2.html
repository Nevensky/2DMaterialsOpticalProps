How symbols are written

  • Vectors and Cartesian tensors are bold, their components italic with indices: the displacement $\bu_{\ell\kappa}$ has components $u_{\ell\kappa\alpha}$.
  • Labels that abbreviate a word are set upright as a whole: $k_{\mathrm B}$ (Boltzmann), $\omega_{\mathrm{LO}}$ (longitudinal optical, Section A.4), and superscripts such as ion (ionic), ph (phonon) and e (electrons only) on quantities of later sections, for example $\Pi^{\ion}$. Running indices stay italic.
  • Euler's number, the imaginary unit and the differential are upright, $\ee^{\ii\bq\cdot\bR_\ell}$ and $\dd^3r$, so that the exponential is never confused with the elementary charge $e$ or with the phonon eigenvector components $e_{\kappa\alpha,\nu}$.
  • A tilde means mass-scaled: $\tPhi$ is the force-constant matrix divided by square roots of masses.
  • Hats mark operators only where an operator and an ordinary number of the same name appear together. A hat also marks the unit vector of a vector, $\hat\bq=\bq/q$; a vector that is a unit vector by definition, such as a bond direction $\bn$, needs none.
  • A comma separates index groups with different roles: in $e_{\kappa\alpha,\nu}$ the pair $\kappa\alpha$ labels a component and $\nu$ the mode; in $\Phi_{\ell\kappa\alpha,\ell'\kappa'\beta}$ the two groups label the two atoms.

Part A

Electron–phonon interactions from first principles

Part A builds the standard first-principles theory of electrons coupled to the vibrations of a crystal lattice. It starts in this section with the vibrations themselves. The later sections couple them to electrons, compute the coupling with density-functional perturbation theory, and treat both with many-body perturbation theory.

One thread runs through all of Part A. Every interaction in it is the instantaneous Coulomb interaction, screened by the electrons and, through the vibrations, by the ions. Light enters only as an external field. Part B replaces this picture with the full electromagnetic field.

Lattice dynamics and phonons

Atoms in a crystal sit near the bottom of an energy landscape and vibrate about it. This section builds the language the primer uses for those vibrations, from the ground up: how atoms and their displacements are labelled, what force constants are and why they obey a sum rule, why waves with a wavevector are the natural description of the motion, and how quantizing the waves gives phonons.

What you need: Newton's equations for masses on springs, complex numbers, and the eigenvalues of Hermitian matrices. A background box recaps the quantum harmonic oscillator where it is needed. No electromagnetism appears in this section.1

The picture: atoms on springs

A nucleus is between about 1800 (hydrogen) and 430,000 (uranium) times heavier than an electron. When the nuclei move, the electrons have time to readjust, and they stay in their ground state for the current positions of the nuclei. This is the Born–Oppenheimer approximation. It turns the crystal into a mechanical problem. For every arrangement of the nuclei there is one energy $U$: the ground-state energy of the electrons plus the Coulomb repulsion of the nuclei, all held fixed in that arrangement. Seen as a function of all nuclear positions, $U$ is the Born–Oppenheimer energy surface. A density-functional code computes it for any frozen arrangement of atoms. The force on a nucleus is minus the gradient of $U$ with respect to that nucleus's position.2

At equilibrium every force vanishes, so the atoms sit at a stationary point of $U$; for a stable crystal it is a minimum. Close to a minimum any smooth function looks like a paraboloid, and a parabolic energy is the energy of a set of springs. This is the harmonic approximation. It holds because the vibrations are small: at room temperature atoms move by several hundredths of an ångström up to about a tenth (silicon: about 0.08 Å root mean square along each direction), a few percent of bond lengths of 1.4 to 3.5 Å; hydrogen, at about ten percent of its bond, is the exception. Figure A1.7 puts numbers on single modes.

The plan of the section follows from this picture:

  1. label every atom and its displacement;
  2. expand $U$ to second order, which defines the spring constants of the crystal, the force constants;
  3. find the patterns of motion in which all atoms oscillate at one frequency, the normal modes;
  4. quantize the normal modes, which gives phonons.

Labelling a crystal

A crystal is a pattern repeated by translations. The repeated unit is the unit cell, the parallelepiped spanned by three primitive vectors $\ba_1$, $\ba_2$, $\ba_3$, chosen so that every translation that maps the crystal onto itself is an integer combination of them. Cells are labelled by an index $\ell$ that stands for a triple of integers $(\ell_1,\ell_2,\ell_3)$. The corner of cell $\ell$ sits at the lattice vector

$$\bR_\ell=\ell_1\ba_1+\ell_2\ba_2+\ell_3\ba_3 .$$

Every cell contains the same $N_\kappa$ atoms, the basis, labelled $\kappa=1,\dots,N_\kappa$. Atom $\kappa$ has mass $M_\kappa$ and sits at $\btau_\kappa$, measured from the corner of its cell. Atom $\kappa$ of cell $\ell$ therefore has its equilibrium position at $\btau_{\ell\kappa}$, and as it vibrates it is displaced from there by $\bu_{\ell\kappa}(t)$:3

$$\begin{gathered}\btau_{\ell\kappa}=\bR_\ell+\btau_\kappa,\\ \text{position at time }t=\btau_{\ell\kappa}+\bu_{\ell\kappa}(t).\end{gathered} \tag{A1.1}$$

$\kappa$

In a sheet only $\alpha=x,y$ are needed for motion within the plane.

A finite crystal without surfaces

A real crystal has about $10^{23}$ atoms, and its surfaces break the repetition. A standard device removes both problems. Take a block of $N_1\times N_2\times N_3$ cells, the Born–von Kármán (BvK) supercell, and require the motion to repeat from one block to the next:

$$\begin{gathered}\bu_{\ell\kappa}=\bu_{\ell'\kappa}\\ \text{whenever }\bR_{\ell'}=\bR_\ell+N_j\ba_j\ \ (j=1,2,3).\end{gathered} \tag{A1.2}$$

An atom near the right face of the block now has neighbours across that face: the atoms near the left face of the next copy, which move exactly like those of the block. No cell is special and there are no surfaces. The supercell holds $N_\ell=N_1N_2N_3$ cells and $3N_\kappa N_\ell$ coordinates. Bulk properties come out of the limit $N_\ell\to\infty$, where the choice of block no longer matters.

The energy to second order: force constants

Expand the energy surface $U$ in powers of all $3N_\kappa N_\ell$ displacements about equilibrium, the way one expands a function of one variable, $f(x)=f(0)+f'(0)\,x+\tfrac12f''(0)\,x^2+\dots$, term by term:

  1. The zeroth-order term is the equilibrium energy $U_0$.

    A constant shifts all energies equally and exerts no force, so it plays no role in the motion.

  2. The first-order terms, $\sum_{\ell\kappa\alpha}(\partial U/\partial u_{\ell\kappa\alpha})\,u_{\ell\kappa\alpha}$, vanish.

    $-\partial U/\partial u_{\ell\kappa\alpha}$ is the force on atom $\ell\kappa$ along $\alpha$, and every force is zero at equilibrium.

  3. The second-order terms are kept; third and higher orders are dropped.

    Each further order is smaller by roughly one more factor of displacement over bond length, a few percent. These anharmonic terms let phonons scatter off each other and cause thermal expansion; this primer leaves them out.

The result is the harmonic energy

$$\begin{gathered}U\simeq U_0+\frac12\sum_{\ell\kappa\alpha}\ \sum_{\ell'\kappa'\beta}\Phi_{\ell\kappa\alpha,\ell'\kappa'\beta}\,u_{\ell\kappa\alpha}\,u_{\ell'\kappa'\beta},\\ \Phi_{\ell\kappa\alpha,\ell'\kappa'\beta}=\left.\frac{\partial^2U}{\partial u_{\ell\kappa\alpha}\,\partial u_{\ell'\kappa'\beta}}\right|_{\text{equilibrium}} .\end{gathered} \tag{A1.3}$$
$\Phi_{\ell\kappa\alpha,\ell'\kappa'\beta}$
interatomic force constant that couples the motion of atom $\ell\kappa$ along $\alpha$ to the motion of atom $\ell'\kappa'$ along $\beta$ (eV/Å²)
$\alpha,\beta$
Cartesian directions $x,y,z$
$U_0$
energy at equilibrium (eV)

The comma in $\Phi_{\ell\kappa\alpha,\ell'\kappa'\beta}$ separates the two atoms involved: the labels before it name one atom and one direction, those after it the other. Since there are $3N_\kappa N_\ell$ coordinates, $\Phi$ is a $3N_\kappa N_\ell\times3N_\kappa N_\ell$ matrix whose rows and columns are each labelled by a triple: cell, atom, direction.4

What a force constant means

Differentiating (A1.3) gives the force on any atom. Along $\alpha$, atom $\ell\kappa$ feels

$$F_{\ell\kappa\alpha}=-\frac{\partial U}{\partial u_{\ell\kappa\alpha}}=-\sum_{\ell'\kappa'\beta}\Phi_{\ell\kappa\alpha,\ell'\kappa'\beta}\,u_{\ell'\kappa'\beta} . \tag{A1.4}$$

The factor $\frac12$ is gone because $u_{\ell\kappa\alpha}$ appears in both slots of the double sum in (A1.3), and the two contributions are equal because $\Phi$ is symmetric (property 2 below).

Equation (A1.4) gives each force constant a direct meaning. Displace a single atom $\ell'\kappa'$ by a small amount $u_{\ell'\kappa'\beta}$ along $\beta$ and hold all the others fixed. Then each atom $\ell\kappa$ feels the force $F_{\ell\kappa\alpha}=-\Phi_{\ell\kappa\alpha,\ell'\kappa'\beta}\,u_{\ell'\kappa'\beta}$. So $\Phi_{\ell\kappa\alpha,\ell'\kappa'\beta}$ is minus the force along $\alpha$ on atom $\ell\kappa$ per unit push of atom $\ell'\kappa'$ along $\beta$. One column of the matrix is the pattern of forces that one pushed atom creates across the crystal. This is also a practical way to compute force constants: push one atom in a supercell, compute all forces with density-functional theory, and repeat for each atom of the basis and each direction; translation invariance (property 3 below) supplies the pushes in every other cell. Section A.3 describes the perturbative alternative.

For a fixed pair of atoms, the entries for all directions $\alpha,\beta$ form a $3\times3$ block of $\Phi$ ($2\times2$ in a sheet), written $\Phi_{\ell\kappa,\ell'\kappa'}$ when the Cartesian indices are left out. When the pushed atom is the atom itself, $\ell'\kappa'=\ell\kappa$, the block $\Phi_{\ell\kappa,\ell\kappa}$ is the on-site force constant: the stiffness of the cage that holds the atom in place while all its neighbours stay fixed.

Four properties of the force constants

  1. Real: $\Phi$ is real, because $U$ is a real function of real coordinates.
  2. Symmetric, $\Phi_{\ell\kappa\alpha,\ell'\kappa'\beta}=\Phi_{\ell'\kappa'\beta,\ell\kappa\alpha}$, because the order of two partial derivatives does not matter. In words: the force along $\alpha$ on atom $\ell\kappa$ per unit push of atom $\ell'\kappa'$ along $\beta$ equals the force along $\beta$ on $\ell'\kappa'$ per unit push of $\ell\kappa$ along $\alpha$.
  3. Translation invariant. $\Phi$ depends on the two cells only through the vector between them:
    $$\begin{gathered}\Phi_{\ell\kappa\alpha,\ell'\kappa'\beta}=\Phi_{0\kappa\alpha,\ell''\kappa'\beta}\\ \text{where }\bR_{\ell''}=\bR_{\ell'}-\bR_\ell .\end{gathered} \tag{A1.5}$$
    All cells are equivalent. Pushing an atom of cell $\ell$ and looking at cell $\ell'$ is the same as pushing the same atom of cell 0 and looking at the cell displaced by $\bR_{\ell'}-\bR_\ell$.
  4. Acoustic sum rule. For every atom $\ell\kappa$ and every pair of directions $\alpha,\beta$,
    $$\sum_{\ell'\kappa'}\Phi_{\ell\kappa\alpha,\ell'\kappa'\beta}=0 . \tag{A1.6}$$

The sum rule rests on one observation: moving the whole crystal costs no energy and creates no force. In steps:

  1. Displace every atom by the same small vector $\bd$, that is $u_{\ell'\kappa'\beta}=d_\beta$ for all $\ell'\kappa'$.

    This is a rigid translation. No bond is stretched or bent, so no atom feels a force.

  2. Insert this displacement into (A1.4) and set the force to zero:

    $$0=F_{\ell\kappa\alpha}=-\sum_\beta\Big(\sum_{\ell'\kappa'}\Phi_{\ell\kappa\alpha,\ell'\kappa'\beta}\Big)\,d_\beta .$$
    $d_\beta$ is the same for every atom, so it comes out of the sum over atoms.

  3. Take $\bd$ along $x$, then $y$, then $z$. Each choice keeps a single $\beta$, so each bracket vanishes on its own, which is (A1.6).

    The force must vanish whatever the direction of the translation.

Moving the on-site term to the other side turns the rule into a recipe for the on-site force constant:

$$\Phi_{\ell\kappa\alpha,\ell\kappa\beta}=-\sum_{\ell'\kappa'\neq\ell\kappa}\Phi_{\ell\kappa\alpha,\ell'\kappa'\beta} .$$

For atoms joined by springs this is a familiar statement. The stiffness that holds an atom in place is the sum of the stiffnesses of all the springs attached to it, and its coupling to each neighbour is minus the stiffness of the spring in between. In two or three dimensions a spring acts only along its bond. A spring of stiffness $K$ along the unit bond vector $\bn$ stores $\frac K2\big[\bn\cdot(\bu-\bu')\big]^2$ to second order when its two ends move by $\bu$ and $\bu'$; differentiating twice gives the blocks $+K\bn\bn^{\mathsf T}$ on each end and $-K\bn\bn^{\mathsf T}$ between them, a stiffness $K\cos^2\varphi$ along a direction at angle $\varphi$ to the bond. The “shift every atom” setting of Figure A1.2 shows the rule at work. When the whole crystal shifts, every coupling still produces a force on the selected atom, but these forces add up tip to tail to zero. Computed force constants break (A1.6) slightly through numerical noise, so codes restore it before computing frequencies. The rule returns below, at zero wavevector, where it guarantees the vibrations of zero frequency.5

The simplest example: a chain of springs

Take a line of identical atoms of mass $M$ at spacing $a$, joined to their nearest neighbours by springs of stiffness $K$, and let them move only along the line. There is one atom per cell and one direction, so the labels $\kappa$ and $\alpha$ can be dropped. The energy of the springs is

$$U=U_0+\frac K2\sum_\ell\big(u_{\ell+1}-u_\ell\big)^2 . \tag{A1.7}$$

The displacement $u_\ell$ appears in two terms of the sum, $(u_{\ell+1}-u_\ell)^2$ and $(u_\ell-u_{\ell-1})^2$. Differentiating twice therefore gives $\partial^2U/\partial u_\ell^2=K+K$ on the diagonal, $\partial^2U/\partial u_\ell\,\partial u_{\ell\pm1}=-K$ next to it, and zero for any other pair:

$$\Phi_{\ell,\ell}=2K,\qquad \Phi_{\ell,\ell\pm1}=-K,\qquad \Phi=\begin{pmatrix}2K&-K&0&0&-K\\-K&2K&-K&0&0\\0&-K&2K&-K&0\\0&0&-K&2K&-K\\-K&0&0&-K&2K\end{pmatrix}\ \ (N_\ell=5).$$

Every row holds $-K$, $2K$, $-K$, which add up to zero: the acoustic sum rule. Translation invariance is visible too, since each row is the row above shifted by one place. In the Born–von Kármán supercell the last atom is tied to the first, which is why entries appear in the corners of the matrix.

What the harmonic, Born–Oppenheimer picture leaves out

The force constants are computed with the electrons in their ground state for each frozen arrangement of the nuclei, so they contain the static screening of the nuclei by electrons that follow instantly. In metals and doped semiconductors, screening a vibration of long wavelength means moving electrons across that wavelength, which can take longer than one vibration period; the electrons then cannot follow (doped graphene is the textbook case). Static screening is then wrong, and Section A.6 corrects it with a phonon self-energy. The harmonic truncation fails for very light atoms such as hydrogen, near structural instabilities where a vibration softens, and at high temperature.

Why waves: the chain again

Newton's equation for atom $\ell$ of the chain is mass times acceleration equals force, with the force from (A1.4):

$$M\ddot u_\ell=-\sum_{\ell'}\Phi_{\ell,\ell'}\,u_{\ell'}=K\big(u_{\ell+1}-u_\ell\big)+K\big(u_{\ell-1}-u_\ell\big). \tag{A1.8}$$

These $N_\ell$ equations are coupled, because each atom is pulled by its neighbours. The way out is to look for patterns of motion in which all atoms oscillate at one common frequency, the normal modes, for which the coupled equations collapse into a single one. Translation invariance suggests which patterns to try. Shifting a normal mode along the chain by one cell must give another normal mode with the same frequency. The simplest patterns that reproduce themselves under such a shift, up to a constant factor, are waves in which each atom's amplitude is its left neighbour's times a fixed phase factor $\ee^{\ii qa}$. This is more than a guess. Let $\mathcal S$ shift every displacement by one cell, $(\mathcal Su)_\ell=u_{\ell+1}$. Every row of $\Phi$ is the row above shifted by one place, so computing the forces and then shifting gives the same result as shifting and then computing the forces: $\mathcal S$ commutes with $\Phi$. Two commuting matrices that can each be diagonalized share a complete set of eigenvectors; here $\Phi$ is real and symmetric, and $\mathcal S$ is unitary because it only moves every atom one place around the Born–von Kármán ring. So the normal modes can be chosen with $u_{\ell+1}=\eta\,u_\ell$. The Born–von Kármán condition says that $N_\ell$ shifts change nothing, $\mathcal S^{N_\ell}=1$, so $\eta^{N_\ell}=1$ and $\eta=\ee^{\ii qa}$ is a pure phase. (The same argument applied to the electron Hamiltonian gives Bloch's theorem.)

$$u_\ell(t)=\mathrm{Re}\big[\xi\,\ee^{\ii(q\ell a-\omega t)}\big]. \tag{A1.9}$$
$q$
wavevector (Å⁻¹): the phase advances by $qa$ from one atom to the next, and the wavelength is $2\pi/|q|$
$\omega$
angular frequency of the oscillation (s⁻¹); this primer quotes the energy $\hbar\omega$ in meV
$\xi$
complex amplitude (Å): $|\xi|$ is the size of the oscillation and its argument is the phase of atom 0 at $t=0$

Complex exponentials are used because a shift multiplies them by a phase. The equation of motion is linear with real coefficients, so whenever a complex pattern solves it, so does its real part, and the real part is the physical displacement. In the wave calculations that follow, up to (A1.19), $u$ denotes the complex displacement, here $\xi\,\ee^{\ii(q\ell a-\omega t)}$; its real part is the physical displacement.

  1. Insert (A1.9) into (A1.8). Two time derivatives give $-\omega^2$, and the neighbours carry the same wave evaluated at $\ell\pm1$:

    $$-M\omega^2\,\xi\,\ee^{\ii(q\ell a-\omega t)}=K\,\xi\,\ee^{\ii(q\ell a-\omega t)}\big(\ee^{\ii qa}-1\big)+K\,\xi\,\ee^{\ii(q\ell a-\omega t)}\big(\ee^{-\ii qa}-1\big).$$
    $u_{\ell\pm1}=\ee^{\pm\ii qa}\,u_\ell$: each neighbour carries the complex displacement of atom $\ell$ turned by $\pm qa$.

  2. Cancel the common factor $\xi\,\ee^{\ii(q\ell a-\omega t)}$. The label $\ell$ disappears:

    $$M\omega^2=2K\big(1-\cos qa\big)\equiv K_{\mathrm{eff}}(q).$$
    $\ee^{\ii qa}+\ee^{-\ii qa}=2\cos qa$. Every atom obeys the same equation, so one condition on $\omega$ solves all $N_\ell$ equations at once. That is what it means for (A1.9) to be a normal mode.

  3. Solve for the frequency with $1-\cos qa=2\sin^2(qa/2)$:

    $$\omega(q)=2\sqrt{\frac KM}\,\Big|\sin\frac{qa}2\Big| . \tag{A1.10}$$
    This is the dispersion relation, the frequency of a mode as a function of its wavevector. The positive root is enough: the negative one describes the same motion as the wave at $-q$ with positive frequency, because $\mathrm{Re}\big[\xi\,\ee^{\ii(q\ell a+\omega t)}\big]=\mathrm{Re}\big[\xi^*\ee^{\ii(-q\ell a-\omega t)}\big]$. Each continuous curve $\omega(q)$ is called a branch; the monatomic chain has one.

Figure A1.3 shows the geometry behind step 2. Draw the complex displacements $u_{\ell-1}$, $u_\ell$ and $u_{\ell+1}$ as arrows (phasors) in the complex plane. The two neighbours are the arrow of atom $\ell$ turned by $+qa$ and by $-qa$, so their sum always lies on the line of $u_\ell$: it equals $2\cos qa$ times $u_\ell$, pointing backwards when $\cos qa\lt0$. The net force on atom $\ell$ is therefore antiparallel to its own displacement, $F_\ell=-K_{\mathrm{eff}}(q)\,u_\ell$. In a wave, every atom moves as if it were tied to its equilibrium position by a single spring of stiffness $K_{\mathrm{eff}}(q)=2K(1-\cos qa)$.

Which wavevectors exist

Two facts restrict $q$.

  • $q$ and $q+2\pi/a$ are the same wave. At every atom, $\ee^{\ii(q+2\pi/a)\ell a}=\ee^{\ii q\ell a}\,\ee^{2\pi\ii\ell}=\ee^{\ii q\ell a}$. The two continuous waves differ between the atoms, but there is nothing there to move. So all distinct waves have $q$ in an interval of length $2\pi/a$. The conventional choice is $-\pi/a\lt q\le\pi/a$, the first Brillouin zone.
  • The Born–von Kármán condition makes $q$ discrete. Condition (A1.2) requires $u_{\ell+N_\ell}=u_\ell$, so $\ee^{\ii qN_\ell a}=1$ and
    $$q=\frac{2\pi}{N_\ell\,a}\,s,\qquad s\ \text{an integer}. \tag{A1.11}$$
    The Brillouin zone holds exactly $N_\ell$ of these values: one mode per coordinate of the chain.

Two limits can be read off (A1.10). At $q=0$ every atom moves by the same amount, a rigid translation, and $\omega=0$: the sum rule at work. At long wavelengths, $qa\ll1$, the frequency grows linearly, $\omega\simeq v_{\mathrm s}|q|$ with $v_{\mathrm s}=a\sqrt{K/M}$. This is a sound wave with velocity $v_{\mathrm s}$, and a branch whose frequency vanishes at $q=0$ is called acoustic.

Two atoms per cell: the diatomic chain

Now put two atoms in each cell: $\kappa=1$ with mass $M_1$ at the corner of the cell and $\kappa=2$ with mass $M_2$ halfway along it. A spring $K_1$ joins the two atoms of a cell, and a spring $K_2$ joins atom 2 of cell $\ell$ to atom 1 of cell $\ell+1$. Counting the springs attached to each atom, as for the monatomic chain, gives the nonzero force constants

$$\Phi_{\ell1,\ell1}=\Phi_{\ell2,\ell2}=K_1+K_2,\qquad \Phi_{\ell1,\ell2}=\Phi_{\ell2,\ell1}=-K_1,\qquad \Phi_{\ell2,(\ell+1)1}=\Phi_{(\ell+1)1,\ell2}=-K_2 ,$$

and each row again sums to zero. Newton's equations for the two atoms of cell $\ell$ read

$$\begin{aligned}M_1\ddot u_{\ell1}&=K_1\big(u_{\ell2}-u_{\ell1}\big)+K_2\big(u_{(\ell-1)2}-u_{\ell1}\big),\\ M_2\ddot u_{\ell2}&=K_1\big(u_{\ell1}-u_{\ell2}\big)+K_2\big(u_{(\ell+1)1}-u_{\ell2}\big).\end{aligned}$$

A wave now needs one complex amplitude per atom of the cell, because the two atoms can move by different amounts and out of phase. The phase still advances by $qa$ from cell to cell:

$$u_{\ell\kappa}(t)=\xi_\kappa\,\ee^{\ii(q\ell a-\omega t)},\qquad\kappa=1,2. \tag{A1.12}$$

The phase is taken at the corner of the cell, $\ell a$, not at the atom. Taking it at the atom instead, $u_{\ell2}=\xi'_2\,\ee^{\ii[q(\ell a+a/2)-\omega t]}$, would replace $\xi_2$ by $\xi'_2=\xi_2\,\ee^{-\ii qa/2}$. Nothing physical would change, but the matrices below would pick up phases. This primer always uses the cell corner.6

    $$\omega^2\begin{pmatrix}M_1&0\\0&M_2\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\xi_1\\ \xi_2\end{pmatrix}=\underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}K_1+K_2&-\big(K_1+K_2\,\ee^{-\ii qa}\big)\\-\big(K_1+K_2\,\ee^{\ii qa}\big)&K_1+K_2\end{pmatrix}}_{\displaystyle\Phi(q)}\begin{pmatrix}\xi_1\\ \xi_2\end{pmatrix}. \tag{A1.13}$$
    As for the monatomic chain, the cell index disappears, leaving one $2\times2$ problem for each $q$. The matrix $\Phi(q)$ collects the force constants, each weighted by the phase of the partner atom's cell. Its two off-diagonal entries are complex conjugates of each other: it is Hermitian.

  2. Move the masses out of the way by measuring amplitudes in mass-weighted units, $\xi_\kappa=e_\kappa/\sqrt{M_\kappa}$, and dividing row $\kappa$ by $\sqrt{M_\kappa}$:

    $$\sum_{\kappa'}\tPhi_{\kappa\kappa'}(q)\,e_{\kappa'}=\omega^2e_\kappa,\qquad \tPhi_{\kappa\kappa'}(q)=\frac{\Phi_{\kappa\kappa'}(q)}{\sqrt{M_\kappa M_{\kappa'}}} . \tag{A1.14}$$
    (A1.13) has a mass matrix on the left: a generalized eigenvalue problem. After the rescaling it is an ordinary eigenvalue problem for a Hermitian matrix, which guarantees real $\omega^2$ and orthonormal eigenvectors. Rows and columns are rescaled alike: row $\kappa$ is divided by $\sqrt{M_\kappa}$, and column $\kappa'$ picks up $1/\sqrt{M_{\kappa'}}$ from $\xi_{\kappa'}=e_{\kappa'}/\sqrt{M_{\kappa'}}$. That symmetric rescaling is what keeps $\tPhi$ Hermitian. $\tPhi(q)$ is called the dynamical matrix.

  3. Solve the $2\times2$ problem. With $A=\tPhi_{11}$, $C=\tPhi_{22}$ and $B=\tPhi_{12}$,

    $$\begin{gathered}\omega^2_\pm(q)=\frac{A+C}2\pm\sqrt{\Big(\frac{A-C}2\Big)^2+|B|^2},\\ A=\frac{K_1+K_2}{M_1},\qquad C=\frac{K_1+K_2}{M_2},\qquad |B|^2=\frac{K_1^2+K_2^2+2K_1K_2\cos qa}{M_1M_2}.\end{gathered} \tag{A1.15}$$
    These are the two roots of $\det\big(\tPhi(q)-\omega^2\big)=0$, a quadratic equation in $\omega^2$. Two atoms per cell give two branches.

The pair $(e_1,e_2)$ is the eigenvector of the mode. Its two complex numbers say how far each atom moves, in mass-weighted units, and with which phase. The physical amplitudes are $\xi_\kappa=e_\kappa/\sqrt{M_\kappa}$, so a heavier atom moves less for the same $|e_\kappa|$. The overall phase of an eigenvector is free; Figure A1.4 makes $e_1$ real and positive. Three limits carry most of the physics.

  • $q=0$, lower branch: $\omega_-=0$ and $\xi_1=\xi_2$. The whole cell moves rigidly; this is the acoustic branch again. Near $q=0$ it is a sound wave, $\omega_-\simeq v_{\mathrm s}|q|$ with $v_{\mathrm s}=a\sqrt{K_1K_2/[(K_1+K_2)(M_1+M_2)]}$: the two springs act in series and the whole mass of the cell moves.
  • $q=0$, upper branch: $\omega_+^2=(K_1+K_2)/M_{\mathrm r}$, with the reduced mass $1/M_{\mathrm r}=1/M_1+1/M_2$, and $M_1\xi_1+M_2\xi_2=0$. The two atoms move against each other while the centre of mass of the cell stays at rest. A branch with a nonzero frequency at $q=0$ is called optical: in an ionic crystal this antiphase motion carries an oscillating dipole that infrared light can drive.
  • $q=\pi/a$: $|B|=|K_1-K_2|/\sqrt{M_1M_2}$, and a gap separates the branches unless both $M_1=M_2$ and $K_1=K_2$. When both are equal the two branches are a single monatomic branch, folded back because the chosen cell is twice as long as needed: the chain of spacing $a/2$ has the zone $-2\pi/a\lt q\le2\pi/a$, and with the doubled cell its parts with $\pi/a\lt|q|\le2\pi/a$ are shifted by $\mp2\pi/a$ into the smaller zone, where they form the upper branch.7
Derivation: the eigenvectors at $q=0$ and the sound velocity

At $q=0$, $B=-(K_1+K_2)/\sqrt{M_1M_2}$ is real. The lower root is $\omega_-^2=0$, and solving $\tPhi\,e=0$ gives $e_-\propto(\sqrt{M_1},\sqrt{M_2})$, so $\xi_1=\xi_2$. The upper eigenvector is orthogonal to it, $e_+\propto(\sqrt{M_2},-\sqrt{M_1})$, which gives $\xi_1/\xi_2=-M_2/M_1$, that is $M_1\xi_1+M_2\xi_2=0$.

For the sound velocity, use the product of the two eigenvalues, which equals the determinant: $\omega_+^2\omega_-^2=\det\tPhi=\big[(K_1+K_2)^2-|K_1+K_2\ee^{\ii qa}|^2\big]/(M_1M_2)=2K_1K_2(1-\cos qa)/(M_1M_2)$. For small $q$, $1-\cos qa\simeq q^2a^2/2$ and $\omega_+^2\simeq(K_1+K_2)(M_1+M_2)/(M_1M_2)$, so

$$\omega_-\simeq v_{\mathrm s}|q|,\qquad v_{\mathrm s}=a\sqrt{\frac{K_1K_2}{(K_1+K_2)(M_1+M_2)}} .$$

With $K$ in eV/Å² and $M$ in amu, $\omega^2$ comes out in eV Å⁻² amu⁻¹, and $\hbar\omega=64.654\ \text{meV}\times\sqrt{\omega^2}$.

Any crystal: the dynamical matrix

Nothing in the chain calculation depended on one dimension or on two atoms. For a three-dimensional crystal with $N_\kappa$ atoms per cell the same steps go through, now with Cartesian indices: insert a wave, which turns the force constants into their lattice Fourier transform; scale by the masses; diagonalize. Newton's equation for the $\alpha$ coordinate of atom $\ell\kappa$ is

$$M_\kappa\,\ddot u_{\ell\kappa\alpha}=-\sum_{\ell'\kappa'\beta}\Phi_{\ell\kappa\alpha,\ell'\kappa'\beta}\,u_{\ell'\kappa'\beta}, \tag{A1.16}$$

and the wave has one mass-weighted complex amplitude $e_{\kappa\alpha}$ for each atom and direction, multiplied by the phase $\ee^{\ii\bq\cdot\bR_\ell}$ of the cell:

$$u_{\ell\kappa\alpha}(t)=\frac{e_{\kappa\alpha}}{\sqrt{M_\kappa}}\,\ee^{\ii(\bq\cdot\bR_\ell-\omega t)} . \tag{A1.17}$$
$\bq$
wavevector (Å⁻¹): the phase changes by $\bq\cdot\ba_j$ from a cell to its neighbour along $\ba_j$
$e_{\kappa\alpha}$
mass-weighted complex amplitude of atom $\kappa$ along $\alpha$ (units of $\sqrt{\text{amu}}\cdot$Å); the physical amplitude is $\xi_{\kappa\alpha}=e_{\kappa\alpha}/\sqrt{M_\kappa}$ (Å)

  1. Insert (A1.17) into (A1.16):

    $$-\omega^2\sqrt{M_\kappa}\,e_{\kappa\alpha}\,\ee^{\ii\bq\cdot\bR_\ell}=-\sum_{\ell'\kappa'\beta}\Phi_{\ell\kappa\alpha,\ell'\kappa'\beta}\,\frac{e_{\kappa'\beta}}{\sqrt{M_{\kappa'}}}\,\ee^{\ii\bq\cdot\bR_{\ell'}} .$$
    Two time derivatives of $\ee^{-\ii\omega t}$ give $-\omega^2$. The time factor is the same on both sides and has been cancelled.

  2. Use translation invariance (A1.5). Label the partner's cell relative to cell $\ell$, $\bR_{\ell'}=\bR_\ell+\bR_{\ell''}$, so that $\Phi_{\ell\kappa\alpha,\ell'\kappa'\beta}=\Phi_{0\kappa\alpha,\ell''\kappa'\beta}$ and $\ee^{\ii\bq\cdot\bR_{\ell'}}=\ee^{\ii\bq\cdot\bR_\ell}\,\ee^{\ii\bq\cdot\bR_{\ell''}}$.

    Summing over all cells $\ell'$ is the same as summing over all relative cells $\ell''$. The relabelling isolates the factor $\ee^{\ii\bq\cdot\bR_\ell}$, which also stands on the left.

  3. Cancel $\ee^{\ii\bq\cdot\bR_\ell}$ and divide by $\sqrt{M_\kappa}$. The cell index has disappeared:

    $$\begin{gathered}\sum_{\kappa'\beta}\tPhi_{\kappa\alpha,\kappa'\beta}(\bq)\,e_{\kappa'\beta}=\omega^2\,e_{\kappa\alpha},\\ \tPhi_{\kappa\alpha,\kappa'\beta}(\bq)=\frac{\Phi_{\kappa\alpha,\kappa'\beta}(\bq)}{\sqrt{M_\kappa M_{\kappa'}}},\qquad \Phi_{\kappa\alpha,\kappa'\beta}(\bq)=\sum_{\ell}\Phi_{0\kappa\alpha,\ell\kappa'\beta}\,\ee^{\ii\bq\cdot\bR_\ell} .\end{gathered} \tag{A1.18}$$
    Every cell gives the same equation. So the wave (A1.17) solves all $3N_\kappa N_\ell$ equations of motion exactly when the amplitudes $e_{\kappa\alpha}$ form an eigenvector of $\tPhi(\bq)$ with eigenvalue $\omega^2$.
$\Phi_{\kappa\alpha,\kappa'\beta}(\bq)$
lattice Fourier transform of the force constants: atom $\kappa$ of cell 0 coupled to atom $\kappa'$ of every cell $\ell$, each coupling weighted by the phase $\ee^{\ii\bq\cdot\bR_\ell}$ of the partner's cell (eV/Å²)
$\tPhi_{\kappa\alpha,\kappa'\beta}(\bq)$
the dynamical matrix, the same object mass-scaled: a $3N_\kappa\times3N_\kappa$ Hermitian matrix whose eigenvalues are squared frequencies (eV Å⁻² amu⁻¹)

The problem of $3N_\kappa N_\ell$ coupled coordinates has become $N_\ell$ independent problems, one for each allowed $\bq$, each of size $3N_\kappa\times3N_\kappa$.8 For each $\bq$ there are $3N_\kappa$ eigenvalues $\omega^2_{\bq\nu}$, labelled by the branch index $\nu=1,\dots,3N_\kappa$, and as many eigenvectors $e_{\kappa\alpha,\nu}(\bq)$:

$$\sum_{\kappa'\beta}\tPhi_{\kappa\alpha,\kappa'\beta}(\bq)\,e_{\kappa'\beta,\nu}(\bq)=\omega^2_{\bq\nu}\,e_{\kappa\alpha,\nu}(\bq) . \tag{A1.19}$$

Here $\nu$ counts the modes at each $\bq$ in order of increasing frequency. Where two continuous curves cross, the numbered branches meet and exchange characters (Figure A1.6).

An eigenvector is fixed only up to a factor. From here on the eigenvectors are normalized, as in (A1.20) below, and dimensionless; the size of a wave is then a separate amplitude, made precise by the normal coordinate of (A1.23).

A plot of $\omega_{\bq\nu}$ along lines in the Brillouin zone is the phonon dispersion, which inelastic neutron and x-ray scattering measure. In two and three dimensions the role of $2\pi/a$ is played by the reciprocal lattice. Define the vectors $\bb_j$ by $\ba_j\cdot\bb_{j'}=2\pi\delta_{jj'}$; their integer combinations $\bG=m_1\bb_1+m_2\bb_2+m_3\bb_3$ form the reciprocal lattice. Since $\bG\cdot\bR_\ell=2\pi(m_1\ell_1+m_2\ell_2+m_3\ell_3)$, the phase $\ee^{\ii\bG\cdot\bR_\ell}$ equals 1 in every cell, so $\bq$ and $\bq+\bG$ move every atom identically, exactly like $q$ and $q+2\pi/a$ in the chain. The Born–von Kármán condition (A1.2) requires $\ee^{\ii N_j\bq\cdot\ba_j}=1$. Writing $\bq=\sum_jc_j\bb_j$ gives $\bq\cdot\ba_j=2\pi c_j$, so $c_j=s_j/N_j$ with integers $s_j$. Since $s_j$ and $s_j+N_j$ differ by $\bb_j$, a reciprocal lattice vector, they give the same wave, so $s_j=0,\dots,N_j-1$ lists each distinct wave once: there are $N_\bq=N_1N_2N_3=N_\ell$ of them.

The conventional choice of one representative for each is the first Brillouin zone: the wavevectors closer to $\bq=0$ than to any other $\bG$, which is $-\pi/a\lt q\le\pi/a$ in the chain and a hexagon for the hexagonal sheet. Special points of the zone carry upright capital names: the centre $\bq=0$ is $\Gamma$, and in the hexagon the midpoint of an edge is $\mathrm M$ and a corner is $\mathrm K$ (not to be confused with a mass $M$ or a spring $K$). Figure A1.5 shows the grid of allowed wavevectors, the zone, and the phase $\ee^{\ii\bq\cdot\bR_\ell}$ that each wavevector gives to the cells of the crystal.

Reading $e_{\kappa\alpha,\nu}(\bq)$

The eigenvector carries four labels, and each has a geometric meaning, shown in Figure A1.6:

  • $\bq$ says how the pattern repeats from cell to cell: the motion in cell $\ell$ is the motion in cell 0 times the phase $\ee^{\ii\bq\cdot\bR_\ell}$.
  • $\nu$ says which of the $3N_\kappa$ patterns at this $\bq$ is meant. For fixed $\bq$ the eigenvectors are the columns of a $3N_\kappa\times3N_\kappa$ matrix, one column per branch.
  • $\kappa\alpha$ labels the rows of that matrix: which atom, and which component of its arrow. The three components for one atom form a complex vector, $\be_{\kappa\nu}(\bq)=\big(e_{\kappa x,\nu},e_{\kappa y,\nu},e_{\kappa z,\nu}\big)$, the arrow attached to atom $\kappa$ in mode $\nu$.

The physical displacement of atom $\ell\kappa$ in mode $\bq\nu$ is the real part of $\be_{\kappa\nu}(\bq)\,\ee^{\ii(\bq\cdot\bR_\ell-\omega_{\bq\nu}t)}/\sqrt{M_\kappa}$ times one complex number common to all atoms, taken inside the real part: the amplitude of the mode, made precise below in (A1.23). A complex component means that the atom moves with a phase lag relative to others, or along an ellipse.

Along a symmetry direction of the crystal, such as $\Gamma$–M in the hexagonal sheet, the modes separate by their direction of motion. In a longitudinal mode the atoms move along $\hat\bq=\bq/q$, the direction of propagation, like sound in air; in a transverse mode they move perpendicular to it. Acoustic and optical branches of each kind are labelled LA, TA, LO and TO, and a longitudinal and a transverse sound wave in general travel at different velocities.

Four properties of the modes

Each follows from a property of the force constants, and each is used later.9

  1. The dynamical matrix is Hermitian, $\tPhi_{\kappa'\beta,\kappa\alpha}(\bq)^*=\tPhi_{\kappa\alpha,\kappa'\beta}(\bq)$, so the squared frequencies $\omega^2_{\bq\nu}$ are real.

    Conjugate the definition (A1.18). Since $\Phi$ is real, $\Phi_{\kappa'\beta,\kappa\alpha}(\bq)^*=\sum_\ell\Phi_{0\kappa'\beta,\ell\kappa\alpha}\ee^{-\ii\bq\cdot\bR_\ell}$. Translation invariance moves both atoms by $-\bR_\ell$, giving $\Phi_{(-\ell)\kappa'\beta,0\kappa\alpha}$, and symmetry swaps them, giving $\Phi_{0\kappa\alpha,(-\ell)\kappa'\beta}$. Renaming $-\ell$ as $\ell$ returns $\Phi_{\kappa\alpha,\kappa'\beta}(\bq)$.

  2. A stable crystal has $\omega^2_{\bq\nu}\ge0$.

    Equilibrium is a minimum of $U$, so the harmonic energy (A1.3) is never negative. Inserting the real displacement of a single mode, $u_{\ell\kappa\alpha}=\mathrm{Re}\big[e_{\kappa\alpha,\nu}(\bq)\,\ee^{\ii\bq\cdot\bR_\ell}\big]/\sqrt{M_\kappa}$, into (A1.3) gives an energy that is a positive multiple of $\omega^2_{\bq\nu}$, so a negative $\omega^2$ would lower $U$ below $U_0$. A negative eigenvalue, an imaginary frequency, means the assumed structure is unstable and distorts along that mode.

  3. The eigenvectors are orthonormal and complete:

    $$\begin{gathered}\sum_{\kappa\alpha}e^*_{\kappa\alpha,\nu}(\bq)\,e_{\kappa\alpha,\nu'}(\bq)=\delta_{\nu\nu'},\\ \sum_{\nu}e_{\kappa\alpha,\nu}(\bq)\,e^*_{\kappa'\beta,\nu}(\bq)=\delta_{\kappa\kappa'}\delta_{\alpha\beta} .\end{gathered} \tag{A1.20}$$
    A Hermitian matrix has a full set of orthonormal eigenvectors. Arranged as columns they form a unitary matrix. The first relation says its columns are orthonormal, the second that its rows are, so any pattern of mass-weighted amplitudes in a cell is a combination of the modes.

  4. Waves running in opposite directions have the same frequency, and the eigenvectors can be chosen so that

    $$\omega_{-\bq\nu}=\omega_{\bq\nu},\qquad e_{\kappa\alpha,\nu}(-\bq)=e^*_{\kappa\alpha,\nu}(\bq) . \tag{A1.21}$$
    Because $\Phi$ is real, replacing $\bq$ by $-\bq$ in (A1.18) conjugates the matrix: $\tPhi(-\bq)=\tPhi(\bq)^*$. Conjugating (A1.19) then shows that $e^*(\bq)$ is an eigenvector of $\tPhi(-\bq)$ with the same real eigenvalue. Physically this is time-reversal symmetry: a motion played backwards is also a solution. Two details make the choice consistent. Since the phase is taken at the cell corner, $\Phi(\bq+\bG)=\Phi(\bq)$, so the eigenvectors are taken periodic, $e(\bq+\bG)=e(\bq)$. At a wavevector equivalent to its own negative ($-\bq=\bq+\bG$, such as $\bq=0$ or $q=\pi/a$), (A1.21) then reads $e(\bq)=e^*(\bq)$: the eigenvectors must be real, and real ones exist because there $\tPhi(\bq)=\tPhi(-\bq)=\tPhi(\bq)^*$ is a real symmetric matrix.

These conditions fix each eigenvector only up to a phase factor and, where several branches share a frequency (the acoustic pair and the optical pair of Figure A1.6 at $\Gamma$), only up to a unitary mixing of the degenerate modes; (A1.21) is a convention for the phases. Anything built from a single $e_{\kappa\alpha,\nu}(\bq)$, such as the electron–phonon matrix elements of Section A.2, inherits this freedom, while measurable quantities sum over degenerate modes and do not.

Acoustic and optical branches

At $\bq=0$ the phase factor is 1 in every cell, and (A1.18) reads $\Phi_{\kappa\alpha,\kappa'\beta}(0)$$\,=\sum_\ell\Phi_{0\kappa\alpha,\ell\kappa'\beta}$. Summed over $\kappa'$, this is the left side of the acoustic sum rule (A1.6), so

$$\sum_{\kappa'\beta}\Phi_{\kappa\alpha,\kappa'\beta}(0)\,d_\beta=0\quad\text{for any vector }\bd . \tag{A1.22}$$

In words: moving all atoms by the same vector $\bd$, so that the physical amplitude of every atom is $\boldsymbol\xi_\kappa=\bd$, is a mode with $\omega=0$ at $\bq=0$, and its eigenvector is $\be_{\kappa\nu}(\mathbf 0)\propto\sqrt{M_\kappa}\,\bd$. There is one such mode for each of the three independent directions of $\bd$, so every three-dimensional crystal has three acoustic branches, whose frequencies vanish at $\bq=0$. A sheet whose atoms move only within its plane has two. (A real sheet also moves out of its plane. Central springs give no harmonic restoring force for motion perpendicular to the bonds, which is why the hBN models of this section keep the atoms in the plane. Real sheets resist that motion through bond-angle forces and have a third acoustic branch, the flexural branch ZA, whose frequency grows as $q^2$ rather than linearly.) The remaining $3N_\kappa-3$ branches are optical. By (A1.20) their eigenvectors are orthogonal to the acoustic ones, which at $\bq=0$ reads

$$\sum_\kappa\sqrt{M_\kappa}\,\be_{\kappa\nu}(0)=0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\sum_\kappa M_\kappa\,\boldsymbol\xi_{\kappa}=0 ,$$

with $\boldsymbol\xi_\kappa\propto\be_{\kappa\nu}/\sqrt{M_\kappa}$ the physical amplitude. In an optical mode at $\bq=0$ the centre of mass of each cell is at rest, and the sublattices move against each other. When the atoms carry opposite charges, the cell can then hold an oscillating dipole. With two atoms per cell it always does; with more atoms the dipoles of the sublattices can cancel by symmetry, as in optical modes of bulk hBN or of CaF$_2$ that infrared light cannot drive. The oscillating dipole is the starting point for the coupling of lattice vibrations to electric fields in Section A.4 and to photons in Part B.

These dipoles also act on each other across the crystal. In polar crystals the force constants therefore have a long-range tail that falls off as the inverse cube of the distance, which the short-range spring models of this section lack. In a bulk crystal it lifts the longitudinal optical frequency above the transverse ones even as $\bq\to0$, so the modes at $\Gamma$ depend on the direction from which $\bq$ approaches zero: the LO–TO splitting of Section A.4. In a single sheet the splitting vanishes at $\bq=0$ and grows linearly with $|\bq|$.

From normal modes to phonons

The normal modes are independent oscillators, so quantizing the crystal means quantizing each mode as a harmonic oscillator. One complication remains: because displacements are real, the complex amplitude of a mode at $\bq$ is tied to that of the mode at $-\bq$. The construction below takes care of it. In this subsection hats mark operators, because classical and quantum displacements appear side by side.

The quantum harmonic oscillator skip if familiar

A particle of mass $M$ in the potential $\frac12M\omega^2x^2$ has the Hamiltonian $\hat H=\hat p^2/2M+\frac12M\omega^2\hat x^2$, with $[\hat x,\hat p]=\ii\hbar$. The ladder operators

$$\hat a=\sqrt{\frac{M\omega}{2\hbar}}\Big(\hat x+\frac{\ii}{M\omega}\hat p\Big),\qquad \hat a^\dagger=\sqrt{\frac{M\omega}{2\hbar}}\Big(\hat x-\frac{\ii}{M\omega}\hat p\Big)$$

obey $[\hat a,\hat a^\dagger]=1$. Inverting, $\hat x=\sqrt{\hbar/2M\omega}\,(\hat a+\hat a^\dagger)$, and the Hamiltonian becomes $\hat H=\hbar\omega\big(\hat a^\dagger\hat a+\frac12\big)$. Its eigenstates $|n\rangle$, $n=0,1,2,\dots$, have energies $\hbar\omega\big(n+\frac12\big)$; $\hat a^\dagger$ raises $n$ by one and $\hat a$ lowers it. In every eigenstate $\langle\hat x\rangle=0$, while

$$\langle n|\hat x^2|n\rangle=\frac{\hbar}{2M\omega}\,(2n+1) .$$

In the ground state the particle is spread over a Gaussian of root-mean-square width $\sqrt{\hbar/2M\omega}$, the zero-point amplitude. At temperature $T$ the states are occupied with Boltzmann weights. The mean of $n$ is then the Bose–Einstein occupation $n_{\mathrm B}=1/\big(\ee^{\hbar\omega/\kB T}-1\big)$, and $\langle\hat x^2\rangle=(\hbar/2M\omega)(2n_{\mathrm B}+1)=(\hbar/2M\omega)\coth(\hbar\omega/2\kB T)$.

Normal coordinates

Any real displacement pattern $u_{\ell\kappa\alpha}$ (the physical displacements again, not a complex trial wave), not only a single wave, can be written as a superposition of all modes, with one complex amplitude per mode:

$$\begin{gathered}u_{\ell\kappa\alpha}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_\ell M_\kappa}}\sum_{\bq\nu}\ee^{\ii\bq\cdot\bR_\ell}\,e_{\kappa\alpha,\nu}(\bq)\,Q_{\bq\nu},\\ Q_{\bq\nu}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_\ell}}\sum_{\ell\kappa\alpha}\ee^{-\ii\bq\cdot\bR_\ell}\sqrt{M_\kappa}\,e^*_{\kappa\alpha,\nu}(\bq)\,u_{\ell\kappa\alpha} .\end{gathered} \tag{A1.23}$$
$Q_{\bq\nu}$
normal coordinate: the complex amplitude of mode $\bq\nu$ in the pattern (units of $\sqrt{\text{mass}}\times$length)
$\sum_{\bq}$
sum over the $N_\ell$ wavevectors allowed by the Born–von Kármán condition

The factor $1/\sqrt{N_\ell}$ in both relations shares the $N_\ell$ of the lattice sum $\sum_\ell\ee^{\ii(\bq'-\bq)\cdot\bR_\ell}=N_\ell\delta_{\bq\bq'}$ evenly between them, so that they invert each other and the energy (A1.24) carries no factor $N_\ell$; the $\sqrt{M_\kappa}$ makes every $Q_{\bq\nu}$ a coordinate of unit mass.

Unlike in (A1.9), no "Re" is taken here: the terms at $\bq$ and $-\bq$ are complex conjugates of each other (shown below), so the sum is real by itself.

Derivation: why the two relations in (A1.23) invert each other

  1. Insert the first relation into the second:

    $$Q_{\bq\nu}=\frac1{N_\ell}\sum_{\bq'\nu'}\Big(\sum_\ell\ee^{\ii(\bq'-\bq)\cdot\bR_\ell}\Big)\Big(\sum_{\kappa\alpha}e^*_{\kappa\alpha,\nu}(\bq)\,e_{\kappa\alpha,\nu'}(\bq')\Big)Q_{\bq'\nu'} .$$
    The masses cancel: $\sqrt{M_\kappa}$ from the second relation against $1/\sqrt{M_\kappa}$ from the first.

  2. The lattice sum is $\sum_\ell\ee^{\ii(\bq'-\bq)\cdot\bR_\ell}=N_\ell\,\delta_{\bq\bq'}$ for wavevectors on the Born–von Kármán grid.

    For $\bq'=\bq$ every term is 1. Otherwise $\bq$ and $\bq'$ differ along at least one direction $\ba_j$. The sum over cells is a product of one sum per direction, and along that direction the phases are $N_j$ unit arrows spread evenly around the circle (with $\bq=\sum_j(s_j/N_j)\bb_j$ and $\bq'=\sum_j(s'_j/N_j)\bb_j$, the phase step is $2\pi(s'_j-s_j)/N_j$), which add up to zero. So the product vanishes. The two row-sum dials of Figure A1.5 show this pattern.

  3. With $\bq'=\bq$, orthonormality (A1.20) gives $\sum_{\kappa\alpha}e^*_{\kappa\alpha,\nu}e_{\kappa\alpha,\nu'}=\delta_{\nu\nu'}$, and the right side reduces to $Q_{\bq\nu}$.

    The converse, inserting the second relation into the first, uses the companion sum over the grid, $\sum_{\bq}\ee^{\ii\bq\cdot(\bR_\ell-\bR_{\ell'})}=N_\ell\,\delta_{\ell\ell'}$ (the same evenly-spread-arrows argument), together with completeness (A1.20).

Because displacements are real, the normal coordinates at $\bq$ and $-\bq$ are not independent. Conjugating the second relation of (A1.23) and using (A1.21) gives $Q_{-\bq\nu}=Q^*_{\bq\nu}$. Inserting (A1.23) into the kinetic energy $\sum_{\ell\kappa\alpha}\frac12M_\kappa\dot u^2_{\ell\kappa\alpha}$ and into the harmonic energy (A1.3) gives the vibrational energy $H$, kinetic plus harmonic, measured from $U_0$:10

$$H=\sum_{\bq\nu}\Big(\frac12\,\dot Q^*_{\bq\nu}\dot Q_{\bq\nu}+\frac12\,\omega^2_{\bq\nu}\,Q^*_{\bq\nu}Q_{\bq\nu}\Big) . \tag{A1.24}$$
Derivation: the energy in normal coordinates

  1. Kinetic energy. Write $\dot u^2=\dot u^*\dot u$, which is allowed because $u$ is real, and insert (A1.23) for each factor:

    $$\sum_{\ell\kappa\alpha}\frac{M_\kappa}2\dot u^*_{\ell\kappa\alpha}\dot u_{\ell\kappa\alpha}=\frac12\sum_{\bq\nu,\bq'\nu'}\frac1{N_\ell}\Big(\sum_\ell\ee^{\ii(\bq'-\bq)\cdot\bR_\ell}\Big)\Big(\sum_{\kappa\alpha}e^*_{\kappa\alpha,\nu}(\bq)e_{\kappa\alpha,\nu'}(\bq')\Big)\dot Q^*_{\bq\nu}\dot Q_{\bq'\nu'} .$$
    $M_\kappa$ cancels against the two factors $1/\sqrt{M_\kappa}$. The lattice sum gives $N_\ell\delta_{\bq\bq'}$ and orthonormality gives $\delta_{\nu\nu'}$, leaving $\frac12\sum_{\bq\nu}\dot Q^*_{\bq\nu}\dot Q_{\bq\nu}$.

  2. Harmonic energy. Again write one factor of (A1.3) as $u^*$. The sum over the partner atom, $\sum_{\ell'}\Phi_{\ell\kappa\alpha,\ell'\kappa'\beta}\,\ee^{\ii\bq'\cdot\bR_{\ell'}}$, equals $\ee^{\ii\bq'\cdot\bR_\ell}\,\Phi_{\kappa\alpha,\kappa'\beta}(\bq')$ by translation invariance and the definition (A1.18).

    This is step 2 of the derivation of (A1.18) once more. What remains is the lattice sum over $\ell$, which sets $\bq'=\bq$, and the matrix element $\sum e^*_\nu\,\tPhi\,e_{\nu'}=\omega^2_{\bq\nu}\delta_{\nu\nu'}$ by (A1.19) and (A1.20).

Each mode is now a separate oscillator of unit mass and frequency $\omega_{\bq\nu}$; the masses have been absorbed into the definition of $Q_{\bq\nu}$.

Phonons

The momentum conjugate to $Q_{\bq\nu}$ is $P_{\bq\nu}=\dot Q^*_{\bq\nu}$, not $\dot Q_{\bq\nu}$. A conjugate momentum is the derivative of the Lagrangian (kinetic minus harmonic energy) with respect to a velocity, and only the kinetic part of (A1.24) depends on velocities. Take $Q_{\bq\nu}$ and $Q_{-\bq\nu}$ as independent coordinates (they are two independent combinations of the real coordinates $\mathrm{Re}\,Q_{\bq\nu}$ and $\mathrm{Im}\,Q_{\bq\nu}$). $\dot Q_{\bq\nu}$ appears in two equal terms of (A1.24), at $\bq$ and at $-\bq$, whose sum is $\dot Q_{-\bq\nu}\dot Q_{\bq\nu}$, so $P_{\bq\nu}=\dot Q_{-\bq\nu}=\dot Q^*_{\bq\nu}$. Quantization imposes $[\hat Q_{\bq\nu},\hat P_{\bq'\nu'}]=\ii\hbar\,\delta_{\bq\bq'}\delta_{\nu\nu'}$, and the ladder operators of a single oscillator of unit mass, $\hat x=\sqrt{\hbar/2\omega}\,(\hat a+\hat a^\dagger)$ with $[\hat a,\hat a^\dagger]=1$, carry over with one change forced by $Q_{-\bq\nu}=Q^*_{\bq\nu}$:

$$\begin{gathered}\hat Q_{\bq\nu}=\sqrt{\frac{\hbar}{2\omega_{\bq\nu}}}\,\big(\hat a_{\bq\nu}+\hat a^\dagger_{-\bq\nu}\big),\\ \big[\hat a_{\bq\nu},\hat a^\dagger_{\bq'\nu'}\big]=\delta_{\bq\bq'}\delta_{\nu\nu'} .\end{gathered} \tag{A1.25}$$

The single oscillator has $\hat x\propto\hat a+\hat a^\dagger$. Here the Hermitian conjugate of $\hat Q_{\bq\nu}$ must be $\hat Q_{-\bq\nu}$, the operator form of $Q^*_{\bq\nu}=Q_{-\bq\nu}$, and $(\hat a_{\bq\nu}+\hat a^\dagger_{-\bq\nu})^\dagger=\hat a_{-\bq\nu}+\hat a^\dagger_{\bq\nu}$ is exactly the same combination at $-\bq$. With $\hat P_{\bq\nu}=\ii\sqrt{\hbar\omega_{\bq\nu}/2}\,\big(\hat a^\dagger_{\bq\nu}-\hat a_{-\bq\nu}\big)$ the commutator above is reproduced.

With $P=\dot Q^*$ the energy (A1.24) reads $H=\sum_{\bq\nu}\big(\frac12P^*_{\bq\nu}P_{\bq\nu}+\frac12\omega^2_{\bq\nu}Q^*_{\bq\nu}Q_{\bq\nu}\big)$. Inserting (A1.25) and $\hat P_{\bq\nu}$, the products $\hat a_{\bq\nu}\hat a_{-\bq\nu}$ and $\hat a^\dagger_{\bq\nu}\hat a^\dagger_{-\bq\nu}$ enter the kinetic and the harmonic term with opposite signs and cancel. With $[\hat a,\hat a^\dagger]=1$, what remains for each $\bq$ is $\frac{\hbar\omega_{\bq\nu}}2\big(\hat a^\dagger_{\bq\nu}\hat a_{\bq\nu}+\hat a^\dagger_{-\bq\nu}\hat a_{-\bq\nu}+1\big)$. Relabelling $-\bq$ as $\bq$ in the middle term, with $\omega_{-\bq\nu}=\omega_{\bq\nu}$, gives a sum of independent quantum oscillators:

$$\hat H=\sum_{\bq\nu}\hbar\omega_{\bq\nu}\Big(\hat a^\dagger_{\bq\nu}\hat a_{\bq\nu}+\frac12\Big) . \tag{A1.26}$$

$\hat a^\dagger_{\bq\nu}$ creates a quantum of vibration with energy $\hbar\omega_{\bq\nu}$ and crystal momentum $\hbar\bq$, a phonon, and $\hat a_{\bq\nu}$ removes one. Phonons are bosons: each mode is an oscillator that can hold any number of quanta. The quantity $\hbar\bq$ is called crystal momentum. It is not the momentum of the atoms (a wave with $\bq\neq0$ moves no net mass), but it plays the role of momentum because the crystal is symmetric under lattice translations; since $\bq$ and $\bq+\bG$ are the same wave, it is defined, and conserved in processes, only up to $\hbar\bG$. The three acoustic modes at $\bq=0$ have $\omega=0$. They are translations of the whole crystal rather than oscillations, and they are left out of every sum over modes from here on.12

Inserting (A1.25) into (A1.23) gives the displacement of any atom as an operator:

$$\begin{gathered}\hat u_{\ell\kappa\alpha}=\sum_{\bq\nu}\left(\frac{\hbar}{2N_\ell M_\kappa\,\omega_{\bq\nu}}\right)^{1/2}e_{\kappa\alpha,\nu}(\bq)\,\ee^{\ii\bq\cdot\bR_\ell}\,\hat\phi_{\bq\nu},\\ \hat\phi_{\bq\nu}=\hat a_{\bq\nu}+\hat a^\dagger_{-\bq\nu} .\end{gathered} \tag{A1.27}$$
$\hat\phi_{\bq\nu}$
phonon field of mode $\bq\nu$ (dimensionless): it removes a phonon $\bq\nu$ or creates a phonon $-\bq\nu$
$\big(\hbar/2M_\kappa\omega_{\bq\nu}\big)^{1/2}$
zero-point amplitude of atom $\kappa$ in mode $\bq\nu$ (Å)

Because $e_{\kappa\alpha,\nu}(-\bq)=e^*_{\kappa\alpha,\nu}(\bq)$ and $\hat\phi_{-\bq\nu}=\hat\phi^\dagger_{\bq\nu}$, the terms at $\bq$ and $-\bq$ are Hermitian conjugates of each other, so $\hat u_{\ell\kappa\alpha}$ is Hermitian, as a displacement must be. This is the formula of the section that the rest of the primer uses most. Each mode contributes a displacement wave whose size is the zero-point amplitude divided by $\sqrt{N_\ell}$ ($N_\ell$ is the number of cells), because one phonon is spread over the whole crystal. Both terms of $\hat\phi_{\bq\nu}$ hand crystal momentum $\hbar\bq$ to whatever the displacement acts on: absorbing a phonon of wavevector $\bq$ or emitting one of wavevector $-\bq$. When an electron feels the displacement, in Section A.2, $\hat\phi_{\bq\nu}$ is the operator that multiplies the electron–phonon coupling.

How far atoms move

Thermal averages of two displacements need one fact about the phonon field. In thermal equilibrium $\langle\hat\phi_{\bq\nu}\hat\phi_{\bq'\nu'}\rangle$ vanishes unless $\bq'=-\bq$ and $\nu'=\nu$, and then $\langle\hat\phi_{\bq\nu}\hat\phi_{-\bq\nu}\rangle=2n_{\bq\nu}+1$, where $n_{\bq\nu}=1/\big(\ee^{\hbar\omega_{\bq\nu}/\kB T}-1\big)$ is the Bose–Einstein occupation of the mode at temperature $T$. The reason: a thermal state is diagonal in the phonon numbers, so only products that remove and restore the same phonon survive. In $\hat\phi_{\bq\nu}\hat\phi_{-\bq\nu}=(\hat a_{\bq\nu}+\hat a^\dagger_{-\bq\nu})(\hat a_{-\bq\nu}+\hat a^\dagger_{\bq\nu})$ these are $\langle\hat a_{\bq\nu}\hat a^\dagger_{\bq\nu}\rangle=n_{\bq\nu}+1$ and $\langle\hat a^\dagger_{-\bq\nu}\hat a_{-\bq\nu}\rangle=n_{\bq\nu}$. Squaring (A1.27), averaging, and using (A1.21) gives the mean-square displacement of one atom along one direction:

$$\big\langle\hat u^2_{\ell\kappa\alpha}\big\rangle=\frac1{N_\ell}\sum_{\bq\nu}\frac{\hbar}{2M_\kappa\omega_{\bq\nu}}\,\big|e_{\kappa\alpha,\nu}(\bq)\big|^2\,\coth\frac{\hbar\omega_{\bq\nu}}{2\kB T} . \tag{A1.28}$$

Each mode adds its squared zero-point amplitude, weighted by how strongly atom $\kappa$ takes part in it, $|e_{\kappa\alpha,\nu}|^2$, and by $\coth(\hbar\omega_{\bq\nu}/2\kB T)=2n_{\bq\nu}+1$. That factor stays at 1 while $\kB T\ll\hbar\omega$ and grows as $2\kB T/\hbar\omega$ in the classical regime. In numbers, $\hbar/(2M\omega)=2.0901\ \text{Å}^2/\big(M[\text{amu}]\cdot\hbar\omega[\text{meV}]\big)$. An optical phonon of silicon (28.09 amu, about 64 meV) has a zero-point amplitude of 0.034 Å, and so does the boron atom in an optical phonon of hBN (10.81 amu, about 170 meV); the bond lengths are 2.35 and 1.45 Å (literature values, illustrative). Figure A1.7 explores single modes. In a large crystal the grid sum becomes an average over the zone, $\frac1{N_\ell}\sum_\bq\to\frac{\OV}{(2\pi)^3}\int_{\mathrm{BZ}}\dd^3q$, with $\OV$ the volume of the unit cell ($\OS/(2\pi)^2$ and $\dd^2q$ for a sheet of cell area $\OS$).

Physical picture: a phonon is not an oscillating atom

In a state with a definite number of phonons the mean displacement of every atom is zero, $\langle\hat u\rangle=0$; only the spread $\langle\hat u^2\rangle\propto2n+1$ is nonzero. The running waves animated in the figures are classical oscillations, the mean displacement in a coherent state, an eigenstate of $\hat a_{\bq\nu}$ with a complex eigenvalue, which superposes many phonon numbers. In such a state $\langle\hat u\rangle$ is exactly a classical wave, the real part of a wave of the form (A1.17) whose amplitude is set by the eigenvalue, while the spread around it stays at its zero-point value. The zero-point spread is physical: it makes band gaps depend on the isotope mass, and it shifts them even at zero temperature (Section A.8).

Where these objects go next

The displacement operator (A1.27) feeds everything that follows. An electron feels the potential of the displaced nuclei. Expanding that potential to first order in $\hat u$ gives the electron–phonon coupling of Section A.2, with $\hat\phi_{\bq\nu}$ and the zero-point amplitude as factors.

The EPP formalism of Part B builds the same normal modes in the same way, but admits only the short-range part of the force constants. It regenerates the long-range dipole part, and the LO–TO splitting this part causes in polar crystals, from the exchange of photons. The same three steps (Fourier transform, scaling, diagonalization) later quantize the electromagnetic field itself.

Check yourself: three short questions

  1. A crystal has three atoms per cell. How many phonon branches does it have, how many acoustic and how many optical?

    Answer. $3N_\kappa=9$ branches: 3 acoustic and 6 optical.

  2. Why does the acoustic frequency vanish at $\bq=0$, but not the optical one?

    Answer. At $\bq=0$ the acoustic modes are rigid translations, which stretch no bond; the acoustic sum rule (A1.6), in its zero-wavevector form (A1.22), gives them $\omega=0$. Optical modes move the sublattices against each other, which stretches bonds.

  3. Replace every atom by a heavier isotope, $M_\kappa\to2M_\kappa$. How do the frequencies and the zero-point amplitudes change?

    Answer. The force constants do not change, because they come from the electrons. So $\tPhi\to\tPhi/2$, every $\omega$ drops by a factor $\sqrt2$, and the zero-point amplitude $\sqrt{\hbar/2M\omega}$, which scales as $M^{-1/4}$, drops by $2^{1/4}\approx1.19$.
