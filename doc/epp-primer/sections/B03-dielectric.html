B.3EPP §4.2 · Eqs. 144–165

The polar crystal as a dielectric

Drive the ions of a diatomic crystal with a uniform field and the Lagrangian of B.2 returns a Lorentz oscillator. Its dielectric function has poles at the TO and zeros at the LO frequency. The Reststrahlen band, the Lyddane–Sachs–Teller relation and the lattice part of the screened interaction $W$ all follow from that one function.

In §B.2 the electron–phonon–photon Lagrangian (E.134) gave every ion a harmonic equation of motion, driven by the electric field through its Born charge: $m_a\ddot Q_{ia\mu}+\sum_{jb\nu}k_{ia\mu,jb\nu}Q_{jb\nu}=\sum_\nu Z^*_{a\nu\mu}E_\nu(\bR_i^0)$ (E.143). Before quantizing anything, EPP §4.2.1 tests this equation on the textbook case: a diatomic polar crystal at long wavelength. EPP §4.2.1 is classical (no $\hbar$) and written in Gaussian units. $Z^*$ carries the dimension of charge, $Z^*_{\rm EPP}=|e|\,Z^*_{\rm G}$. Here $\mathbf D$ is the electric displacement, not the photon propagator $D$ of §B.5–§B.7.

Two masses, one spring, one field

At $|\bq|\ll 1/a$ neighbouring cells move in phase, $Q_{ja\mu}\approx Q_{ia\mu}$. Every short-range spring between the two sublattices then adds into one effective spring $k_\mu$, and the crystal behaves like one diatomic molecule per cell, repeated in lockstep. With diagonal Born charges and force constants (exact for cubic sites) the three Cartesian directions decouple, and the nearest-neighbour equations (E.144)–(E.145) reduce to

$$ m_a\ddot Q_{a\mu}=-k_\mu\left(Q_{a\mu}-Q_{b\mu}\right)+Z^*_{a\mu}E_\mu,\qquad m_b\ddot Q_{b\mu}=-k_\mu\left(Q_{b\mu}-Q_{a\mu}\right)+Z^*_{b\mu}E_\mu . $$

The equal and opposite spring constants come from the acoustic sum rule and the symmetry of the force-constant matrix, applied to the lattice-summed constants. A harmonic drive $[Q_{a\mu},Q_{b\mu}]=[A,B]\,e^{-i\omega t}$ (E.146) turns this into a $2\times2$ linear system with determinant

$$ \det = m_am_b\,\omega^2\left[\omega^2-\omega^2_{\mu{\rm O}}\right]. \tag{E.148}$$

Its zeros are the normal modes. Three acoustic modes have $\omega=0$ and both atoms moving together. Three bare optical modes have $\omega^2_{\mu\rm O}=k_\mu/M$, with $M=m_am_b/(m_a+m_b)$, and the atoms moving against each other with the centre of mass at rest ($A:B=1:-m_a/m_b$). "Bare" is the paper's word: $\omega_{\rm O}$ is set by short-range springs only and has not yet felt the macroscopic field that its own dipoles create.

A Lorentz oscillator with an ionic plasma frequency

The polarization is the dipole per cell over the cell volume, $P_\mu=(Z^*_{a\mu}Q_{a\mu}+Z^*_{b\mu}Q_{b\mu})/\Omega_{\rm uc}$. In a diatomic crystal the two Born charges are equal and opposite, $Z^*_\mu\equiv Z^*_{a\mu}=-Z^*_{b\mu}$, and the driven response becomes

$$ P_\mu=-\frac{\left[Z^*_\mu\right]^2}{\Omega_{\rm uc}M}\,\frac{1}{\omega^2-\omega^2_{\mu\rm O}}\,E_\mu . \tag{E.150}$$

The acoustic pole has dropped out. A rigid translation of a neutral cell carries no dipole, so the field drives only the relative coordinate, and what is left is a Lorentz oscillator resonant at the bare frequency.

The macroscopic $E$ is an average over the cell. A given dipole feels that average plus the field of its near neighbours, which the average smears out. For sites of cubic symmetry this local field is $E_\mu\to E^*_\mu=E_\mu+\frac{4\pi}{3}P_\mu$ (Lorentz, E.151). Inserting it into (E.150) gives

$$ P_\mu=-\frac{\omega^2_{p\mu}}{4\pi}\,\frac{1}{\omega^2-\omega^2_{\mu\rm O}}\left(E_\mu+\frac{4\pi}{3}P_\mu\right), \tag{E.152}$$

where the paper introduces the ionic plasmon frequency

$$ \omega^2_{p\mu}=\frac{4\pi\left[Z^*_\mu\right]^2}{\Omega_{\rm uc}M}. $$

This is exactly a plasma frequency $4\pi ne^2/m$ for a gas of charges $Z^*$ with mass $M$ and density $1/\Omega_{\rm uc}$. In SI units with dimensionless $Z^*$ it reads $\omega_p^2=Z^{*2}e^2/(\varepsilon_0\Omega_{\rm uc}M)$, or $\omega_p^2\,[\mathrm{cm^{-2}}]=4.92\times10^{7}\,Z^{*2}/(\Omega_{\rm uc}[\text{Å}^3]\,M[\mathrm u])$. For NaCl ($Z^*\approx1.12$, $\Omega_{\rm uc}=44.9$ Å$^3$, $M=13.9$ u; literature values, illustrative) this gives $\omega_p\approx314\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}\approx39$ meV, the same scale as the phonons themselves. Solving (E.152) for $P$ moves the resonance down to $\omega^2_{\mu\rm O}-\frac13\omega^2_{p\mu}$ (E.153): the local field reinforces the displacement and softens the restoring force.

Derivation: from the driven two-mass problem to (E.150)

With $e^{-i\omega t}$ the reduced equations become

$$\begin{bmatrix}k_\mu-\omega^2m_a&-k_\mu\\-k_\mu&k_\mu-\omega^2m_b\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}A\\B\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}Z^*_{a\mu}\\Z^*_{b\mu}\end{bmatrix}E_\mu ,$$

with $\det=(k-\omega^2m_a)(k-\omega^2m_b)-k^2=m_am_b\omega^4-k\omega^2(m_a+m_b)$, which is (E.148). Inverting,

$$\begin{bmatrix}A\\B\end{bmatrix}=\frac{E_\mu}{\det}\begin{bmatrix}(Z^*_{a\mu}+Z^*_{b\mu})k_\mu-m_b\omega^2Z^*_{a\mu}\\(Z^*_{a\mu}+Z^*_{b\mu})k_\mu-m_a\omega^2Z^*_{b\mu}\end{bmatrix}, \tag{E.147}$$ $$P_\mu=\frac{E_\mu}{\Omega_{\rm uc}\det}\left\{\left(Z^*_{a\mu}+Z^*_{b\mu}\right)^2k_\mu-\omega^2\left[m_a\left(Z^*_{b\mu}\right)^2+m_b\left(Z^*_{a\mu}\right)^2\right]\right\}e^{-i\omega t}. \tag{E.149}$$

For $Z^*_{b\mu}=-Z^*_{a\mu}$ the curly bracket is $-\omega^2(m_a+m_b)Z^{*2}_\mu$. Its $\omega^2$ cancels the acoustic factor $\omega^2$ in $\det$, and $(m_a+m_b)/(m_am_b)=1/M$ gives (E.150). If the charges did not sum to zero, a term $(Z^*_a+Z^*_b)^2k/(\Omega_{\rm uc}\det)\propto1/\omega^2$ would survive at low frequency: a free-charge (Drude-like) response that an insulator cannot have. This is why the charge-neutrality sum rule $\sum_\kappa Z^*_\kappa=0$ must hold.

Adding the electrons: $\epsilon(\omega)$

Macroscopic electrodynamics closes the loop. With $P_\mu=\chi_\mu E_\mu$ (E.154) and $D_\mu=\epsilon_\mu(\omega)E_\mu=E_\mu+4\pi P_\mu$ (E.155), the lattice alone gives $\epsilon_\mu(\omega)=1-\omega^2_{p\mu}/(\omega^2-\omega^2_{\mu\rm O}+\frac13\omega^2_{p\mu})$ (E.157). The valence electrons are then added by hand:

$$ \epsilon_\mu(\omega)=\epsilon_\infty-\frac{\omega^2_{p\mu}}{\omega^2-\omega^2_{\mu\rm O}+\frac13\omega^2_{p\mu}} . \tag{E.158}$$

The justification is that independent polarizable subsystems add their susceptibilities, $\epsilon_{12}=\epsilon_1+\epsilon_2-1$. In RPA language, $W=v+v(\chi^0_1+\chi^0_2)W$, so $\epsilon_{12}=1-v(\chi^0_1+\chi^0_2)$. A constant $\epsilon_\infty$ means the electrons follow the ions instantaneously, i.e. no retardation in Giustino's sense (no frequency dependence of the electronic screening on phonon time scales). The paper calls this step ad hoc and promises that the diagrammatic theory will generate $\epsilon_\infty$ from the electronic photon self-energy (§B.5, §B.9). Written with its zero and its pole, (E.158) becomes

$$ \epsilon_\mu(\omega)=\epsilon_\infty\,\frac{\omega^2-\tilde\omega^2_{{\rm LO}\mu}}{\omega^2-\tilde\omega^2_{{\rm TO}\mu}}, \tag{E.159}$$
$$ \begin{aligned} \tilde\omega^2_{{\rm LO}\mu}&=\omega^2_{\mu\rm O}+\left[\frac{1}{\epsilon_\infty}-\frac13\right]\omega^2_{p\mu},\\ \tilde\omega^2_{{\rm TO}\mu}&=\omega^2_{\mu\rm O}-\frac13\,\omega^2_{p\mu}. \end{aligned} \tag{E.160}$$
Derivation: from the local field to (E.157) and the factorized form (E.159)–(E.160)

Multiply (E.152) by $\omega^2-\omega_{\rm O}^2$ and collect $P$: $P\,(\omega^2-\omega_{\rm O}^2+\tfrac13\omega_p^2)=-\tfrac{\omega_p^2}{4\pi}E$, which is (E.153). With $P=(\epsilon-1)E/4\pi$ (E.156) this is $\epsilon-1=-\omega_p^2/(\omega^2-\omega_{\rm O}^2+\tfrac13\omega_p^2)$, i.e. (E.157). Replacing $1\to\epsilon_\infty$ and writing $\tilde\omega_{\rm TO}^2=\omega_{\rm O}^2-\tfrac13\omega_p^2$,

$$\epsilon_\infty-\frac{\omega_p^2}{\omega^2-\tilde\omega_{\rm TO}^2}=\epsilon_\infty\,\frac{\omega^2-\left(\tilde\omega_{\rm TO}^2+\omega_p^2/\epsilon_\infty\right)}{\omega^2-\tilde\omega_{\rm TO}^2},$$

so $\tilde\omega_{\rm LO}^2=\tilde\omega_{\rm TO}^2+\omega_p^2/\epsilon_\infty=\omega_{\rm O}^2+(1/\epsilon_\infty-1/3)\,\omega_p^2$, which is (E.160). The pole of $\epsilon$ is the pole of the oscillator. The zero is where the electronic and ionic polarizations cancel the vacuum term in $\mathbf D$.

Zeros are LO, poles are TO

Picture a longitudinal optical wave first. The ions oscillate along $\bq$, so $\bP\parallel\bq$ and $\nabla\cdot\bP\neq0$. Sheets of bound charge $\rho_{\rm b}=-\nabla\cdot\bP$ form at the nodes of $\bP$ and produce a macroscopic field that opposes the displacement. That field is an extra restoring force, so the longitudinal mode is stiffer. In a transverse wave $\bP\perp\bq$, $\nabla\cdot\bP=0$, no bound charge forms, and in the electrostatic limit there is no macroscopic field at all.

Formally, with no free charge $\nabla\cdot\mathbf D=0$, and in electrostatics $\nabla\times\bE=0$. For a plane wave $e^{i\bq\cdot\br}$ these read $\epsilon\,\bq\cdot\bE=0$ and $\bq\times\bE=0$. A longitudinal field ($\bE\parallel\bq$) survives the first condition only if $\epsilon(\omega)=0$ (E.161), and then

$$ D_\mu=\epsilon_\mu(\omega)E_\mu=E_\mu+4\pi P_\mu=0, \tag{E.163}$$
$$ E_\mu=-4\pi P_\mu . \tag{E.164}$$

The zeros of $\epsilon$ are therefore the dressed LO phonons: longitudinal electromagnetic modes in which the polarization sustains its own field. Once $\epsilon_\infty$ is in, $P$ in (E.163)–(E.164) is the total polarization. The electrons screen the field of the ions, so the ionic part alone gives $E=-4\pi P_{\rm ion}/\epsilon_\infty$. A transverse field must vanish, $\bE=0$, and a finite $\bP$ with $\bE=0$ requires $\epsilon\to\infty$. The poles of $\epsilon$ are the TO phonons, with $\mathbf D=4\pi\bP\neq0$ and $\nabla\cdot\mathbf D=0$ guaranteed by transversality. A mode with $Z^*=0$ has $\omega_p=0$ and is not shifted at all.

Lyddane–Sachs–Teller, and what is model independent

At $\omega\to0$, (E.159) gives the Lyddane–Sachs–Teller (LST) relation

$$ \frac{\epsilon_\mu(0)}{\epsilon_\infty}=\frac{\tilde\omega^2_{{\rm LO}\mu}}{\tilde\omega^2_{{\rm TO}\mu}} . \tag{E.165}$$

The static dielectric constant exceeds the electronic one by the squared ratio of LO to TO frequencies. In NaCl, $\epsilon(0)=2.34\times(264/164)^2=6.06$, close to the measured 5.9 (literature values, illustrative). Subtracting the two lines of (E.160) gives a second exact statement, not written in the paper but immediate:

$$ \tilde\omega^2_{\rm LO}-\tilde\omega^2_{\rm TO}=\frac{\omega_p^2}{\epsilon_\infty}=\frac{4\pi Z^{*2}}{\epsilon_\infty\,\Omega_{\rm uc}M}. $$

The local-field term cancels. This difference of squares is the observable splitting, and it is exactly what the Gonze–Lee non-analytic term adds to the longitudinal mode of a cubic diatomic crystal. The positions of $\tilde\omega_{\rm TO}$ and $\tilde\omega_{\rm LO}$ relative to the bare $\omega_{\rm O}$ depend on the local-field model, and $\omega_{\rm O}$ itself cannot be measured.

The author argues that "LO–TO splitting" is a misnomer and prefers "LO shift": the LO mode is the dipole-active phonon dressed by its own macroscopic field. In (E.160) both frequencies move away from $\omega_{\rm O}$, so the clean form of the argument is this. The TO frequency is the analytic one, which already contains the short-range and local-field physics. The LO frequency is the TO frequency plus the dressing by the macroscopic longitudinal field, $\omega_p^2/\epsilon_\infty$ in $\omega^2$.

Bridge to Giustino: LO–TO splitting

Giustino does not derive the LO–TO splitting. In §VI.A.3 he only notes that his treatment of the long-range vertex is analogous to the way DFPT codes compute the splitting (Gonze and Lee, 1997): a non-analytic term of the dynamical matrix, $\propto(\bq\cdot\bZ^*_\kappa)(\bq\cdot\bZ^*_{\kappa'})/(\bq\cdot\boldsymbol\epsilon^\infty\cdot\bq)$, with the same kernel as the Verdi–Giustino vertex (see §A.4). For an isotropic diatomic crystal that term adds exactly $\omega_p^2/\epsilon_\infty$ to the longitudinal $\omega^2$, the model-independent difference above. In DFPT the analytic part already gives $\omega_{\rm TO}$, so DFPT's "bare" frequency is the TO, not the paper's $\omega_{\rm O}$. EPP re-derives the shift as a photon-mediated phonon self-energy in §B.7 and recovers the Gonze–Lee form for $c\to\infty$ in §B.9.

Subtlety: the local field and the bare frequency

Three warnings about (E.151)–(E.160). All three are built into the lab below.

  • Born charges already contain the local field. EPP takes $Z^*=\Omega_{\rm uc}\,\delta P/\delta R$ (E.110) from DFT, where it is computed at zero macroscopic field and already includes the electronic and local-field response. In a Clausius–Mossotti model the charge that feels the Lorentz field is the Szigeti charge $Z_s=3Z^*/(\epsilon_\infty+2)$, so applying (E.151) with $Z^*$ double counts. The factor $4\pi/3$ also holds only for cubic sites.
  • $\tilde\omega_{\rm LO}<\omega_{\rm O}$ when $\epsilon_\infty>3$. Keeping the vacuum factor $\frac13\omega_p^2$ after inserting $\epsilon_\infty$ makes the bracket in (E.160) negative for GaAs, SiC or hBN, so the "shifted" LO sits below the bare mode. A consistent Clausius–Mossotti treatment (not in the paper), with $\omega_p$ still built from the Born charge, gives $\tilde\omega_{\rm TO}^2=\omega_{\rm O}^2-\omega_p^2/(\epsilon_\infty+2)$ and still $\tilde\omega_{\rm LO}^2=\tilde\omega_{\rm TO}^2+\omega_p^2/\epsilon_\infty$. It keeps $\tilde\omega_{\rm LO}>\omega_{\rm O}$ and reduces to (E.160) at $\epsilon_\infty=1$.
  • Cochran instability. If $\omega_p^2>3\omega_{\rm O}^2$ then $\tilde\omega_{\rm TO}^2<0$. The dipolar local field beats the short-range spring and the lattice polarizes spontaneously, a ferroelectric soft mode (Cochran's picture, not in the paper). Approaching it, $\tilde\omega_{\rm TO}\to0$ and LST sends $\epsilon(0)\to\infty$.

The Dyson-equation route of §B.5–§B.7 has no local field. There, away from the light cone, the TO frequency equals the bare $\omega_\sigma$ of the short-range force constants, and the photon propagator supplies only the macroscopic field. That is the "none" setting of the lab.

The Reststrahlen lab

The lab evaluates (E.158)–(E.160) with a retarded damping, $\omega^2\to\omega^2+i\gamma\omega$. The time dependence is $e^{-i\omega t}$, so ${\rm Im}\,\epsilon>0$ means absorption. Four panels show the same $\epsilon(\omega)$: ${\rm Re}$ and ${\rm Im}\,\epsilon$, the normal-incidence reflectance $R=|(\sqrt\epsilon-1)/(\sqrt\epsilon+1)|^2$, the loss function $-{\rm Im}\,1/\epsilon$, and $W/v=1/\epsilon$ split into electronic and lattice parts (derived below). In spectroscopic mode you set $\omega_{\rm TO}$, $\omega_{\rm LO}$ and $\epsilon_\infty$ directly. In microscopic mode you build them from $Z^*$, $\Omega_{\rm uc}$, $M$, $\omega_{\rm O}$ and a local-field model.

The lattice inside $W$

For a homogeneous polar insulator at long wavelength the screened Coulomb interaction is $W(\omega)=v/\epsilon(\omega)$, with $v=4\pi e^2/q^2$. Splitting off the electronic part with (E.159), and using LST in the last step,

$$ \begin{aligned} \frac{W(\omega)}{v}=\frac{1}{\epsilon(\omega)}&=\underbrace{\frac{1}{\epsilon_\infty}}_{W_{\rm e}/v}+\frac{W_{\rm ph}(\omega)}{v},\\ \frac{W_{\rm ph}(\omega)}{v}&=\frac{1}{\epsilon_\infty}\,\frac{\tilde\omega_{\rm LO}^2-\tilde\omega_{\rm TO}^2}{\omega^2-\tilde\omega_{\rm LO}^2}\\ &=\left(\frac{1}{\epsilon_\infty}-\frac{1}{\epsilon(0)}\right)\frac{\tilde\omega_{\rm LO}^2}{\omega^2-\tilde\omega_{\rm LO}^2}. \end{aligned}$$

The lattice part has its pole at $\tilde\omega_{\rm LO}$, not at $\tilde\omega_{\rm TO}$. $W$ is the longitudinal (Coulomb) propagator, so its poles are the zeros of $\epsilon$, and the TO frequency appears in $W$ only as a zero. At $\omega\to0$ the ions screen too and $W\to v/\epsilon(0)$. Far above $\tilde\omega_{\rm LO}$ the ions cannot follow and $W\to v/\epsilon_\infty$. Panel (d) of the lab draws exactly this decomposition.

Bridge to Giustino: this is his lattice contribution to $W$

The lattice term above is the polar, long-wavelength case of Giustino's $W=W_{\rm e}+W_{\rm ph}$ (G Eqs. 104–105) with $W_{\rm e}=v/\epsilon_\infty$: Fröhlich's $|g|^2$ times the LO propagator, worked out in §A.5 and, in Born-charge form, in §A.4. Giustino also describes the alternative of Botti and Marques (§IX.A.1): skip the split and build $W$ directly from a Lyddane–Sachs–Teller total dielectric function, which is the $1/\epsilon(\omega)$ of panel (d). In EPP the same object returns as the longitudinal sector of the photon propagator, with $\epsilon_\infty$ no longer put in by hand (§B.6, §B.9).

Physical picture: optics sees TO, Coulomb sees LO

A transverse light wave probes the poles of $\epsilon$: infrared absorption peaks at $\omega_{\rm TO}$, and the crystal reflects between TO and LO. Longitudinal probes see the zeros: an electron passing through (EELS), Fröhlich scattering of a carrier and the screening of an exciton all go through $1/\epsilon$, which has its pole at $\omega_{\rm LO}$. For BSE work this is the practical message. When exciton binding energies are comparable to $\hbar\omega_{\rm LO}$, the relevant screening lies between $\epsilon_\infty$ and $\epsilon(0)$, and $W_{\rm ph}$ tells you how much.

Subtlety: this is electrostatics

The L/T classification used $\nabla\times\bE=0$, which holds for $cq\gg\omega$: no retardation in EPP's sense (finite speed of light), i.e. $c\to\infty$. With Faraday's law the transverse modes hybridize with photons through $c^2q^2=\omega^2\epsilon(\omega)$ and become phonon polaritons, confined to $q\lesssim\sqrt{\epsilon(0)}\,\omega_{\rm TO}/c$, the same scale as the light-cone wave vector $q_c=\sqrt{\epsilon_\infty}\,\omega/c$ of §X.1 and $10^{-5}$–$10^{-4}$ of the Brillouin zone. The Lagrangian (E.134) already contains the transverse field. §B.4 quantizes it, §B.6 finds the polaritons, and §B.7 shows that the order of the limits $q\to0$ and $c\to\infty$ matters.
