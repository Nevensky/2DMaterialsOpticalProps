Band gaps vs temperature, lifetimes and the Fröhlich catastrophe

Atoms never stand still, not even at zero temperature, so every band energy is shifted and broadened by the lattice. Here the self-energies of §A.7 become measurable numbers: the temperature-dependent gap with its zero-point renormalization, and carrier lifetimes. You will also see why the adiabatic Allen–Heine formula diverges in polar crystals, and why the cure is the phonon energy $\hbar\omega$, not the speed of light.

Quasiparticle energies from Fan–Migdal plus Debye–Waller

An electron in a vibrating crystal feels the lattice in two ways. It sees a crystal potential blurred by the zero-point and thermal motion of the ions (Debye–Waller), and it polarizes the lattice around itself by emitting and reabsorbing virtual phonons (Fan–Migdal). Both effects are second order in the displacement, so both scale with $\langle u^2\rangle\propto(2n_{\bq\nu}+1)\hbar/2M\omega_{\bq\nu}$. At $T=0$ they leave a zero-point renormalization (ZPR); as the phonon populations $n_{\bq\nu}(T)$ grow, the shift grows with them.

Formally, take the clamped-nuclei Kohn–Sham or GW energies $\varepsilon_{n\bk}$ as the reference (§A.7). In the Kohn–Sham basis the Dyson equation (G Eq. 151) becomes

$$ \begin{align} G^{-1}_{nn'\bk}(\omega)&=G^{{\rm cn},-1}_{nn'\bk}(\omega)-\Sigma^{\rm ep}_{nn'\bk}(\omega), \tag{G.162}\\ G^{-1}_{nn'\bk}(\omega)&=(\hbar\omega-\tilde\varepsilon_{n\bk})\,\delta_{nn'}-\Sigma^{\rm ep}_{nn'\bk}(\omega),\qquad \tilde\varepsilon_{n\bk}=\varepsilon_{n\bk}\pm i\hbar\eta, \tag{G.163}\end{align}$$

with the upper sign for occupied states. For a nondegenerate state, neglecting the off-diagonal $\Sigma^{\rm ep}_{nn'\bk}$, and if $\Sigma^{\rm ep}$ only moves the pole slightly, $\varepsilon_{n\bk}\to\tilde E_{n\bk}=E_{n\bk}+i\Gamma_{n\bk}$, the real part of the new pole follows from the Brillouin–Wigner condition (G.164) of §A.7, and the imaginary part is

$$ \Gamma_{n\bk}={\rm Im}\,\Sigma^{\rm ep}_{n\bk}(\tilde E_{n\bk}/\hbar). \tag{G.165}$$

Here $\Gamma_{n\bk}$ is a linewidth, not the vertex function. Inserting the finite-temperature Fan–Migdal (G.157)+(G.158) and Debye–Waller (G.160)+(G.161) self-energies into (G.164) gives Giustino's temperature-dependent "band structure renormalization":

$$ \begin{aligned} E_{n\bk}=\varepsilon_{n\bk}&+\sum_\nu\int\frac{d\bq}{\Omega_{\rm BZ}}\sum_m|g_{mn\nu}(\bk,\bq)|^2\\ &\times{\rm Re}\bigg[\frac{1-f_{m\bk+\bq}+n_{\bq\nu}}{E_{n\bk}-\varepsilon_{m\bk+\bq}-\hbar\omega_{\bq\nu}+i\Gamma_{n\bk}}+\frac{f_{m\bk+\bq}+n_{\bq\nu}}{E_{n\bk}-\varepsilon_{m\bk+\bq}+\hbar\omega_{\bq\nu}+i\Gamma_{n\bk}}\bigg]\\ &+\sum_\nu\int\frac{d\bq}{\Omega_{\rm BZ}}\,g^{\rm DW}_{nn\nu\nu}(\bk,\bq,-\bq)\,(2n_{\bq\nu}+1). \end{aligned} \tag{G.166}$$

The coupling $g_{mn\nu}(\bk,\bq)$ is the screened DFPT matrix element of G Eq. (38), an energy without $N_p$; $f_{m\bk+\bq}$ and $n_{\bq\nu}$ are Fermi–Dirac and Bose–Einstein occupations, and the $\bq$ integrals are Brillouin-zone averages (SI units throughout). Equation (G.166) rests on the harmonic approximation and on approximations (i)–(vii) of §A.7. Allen and Heine (1976) made two more: Rayleigh–Schrödinger instead of Brillouin–Wigner ($E_{n\bk}\to\varepsilon_{n\bk}$, $\Gamma_{n\bk}\to0$ inside the bracket), and phonon energies dropped from the denominators. The occupations then cancel, $(1-f+n)+(f+n)=1+2n$, and one obtains the adiabatic Allen–Heine formula

$$ \begin{aligned} E_{n\bk}=\varepsilon_{n\bk}+\sum_\nu\int\frac{d\bq}{\Omega_{\rm BZ}}\bigg[&\sum_m\frac{|g_{mn\nu}(\bk,\bq)|^2}{\varepsilon_{n\bk}-\varepsilon_{m\bk+\bq}}\\ &+g^{\rm DW}_{nn\nu\nu}(\bk,\bq,-\bq)\bigg](2n_{\bq\nu}+1). \end{aligned} \tag{G.167}$$

Setting $n_{\bq\nu}=0$ defines the zero-point renormalization $\Delta E^{\rm ZP}_{n\bk}=E_{n\bk}(T=0)-\varepsilon_{n\bk}$. Allen and Heine originally reached (G.167) by expanding adiabatic energies in the displacements and averaging with Bose–Einstein statistics; the field theory lands on the same formula.

Two different "adiabatic" approximations

Both rest on the same Born–Oppenheimer assumption, that $\hbar\omega_{\bq\nu}$ is small next to the electronic energy differences involved, but they apply it to different processes. The adiabatic phonons of §A.6 use static electronic screening, $\epsilon_{\rm e}(\omega=0)$: the electrons follow the ions instantly. In an insulator the electronic excitations that screen a phonon cross the gap, so this is excellent. The adiabatic Allen–Heine formula drops $\hbar\omega_{\bq\nu}$ next to $\varepsilon_{n\bk}-\varepsilon_{m\bk+\bq}$: the electron sees a frozen lattice distortion. Near a band extremum the intraband differences $\varepsilon_{n\bk}-\varepsilon_{n\bk+\bq}$ go to zero as $\bq\to0$, so the assumption always fails for small $\bq$, even in a wide-gap insulator. With nonpolar coupling that region carries little weight; with the polar $1/q^2$ coupling it dominates, which is the root of the Fröhlich catastrophe below.

Allen–Heine in practice: Debye–Waller from first-order matrix elements

The Debye–Waller term needs second derivatives of $V^{\rm KS}$, which are expensive. Allen and Heine avoided them with translational invariance: rigidly translating the crystal cannot change an eigenvalue, even when the crystal has already been displaced. Second-order perturbation theory turns this into two sum rules (derivation below). In the rigid-ion approximation, where $V^{\rm KS}$ is a sum of atom-centred potentials, only on-site second derivatives survive, and they become products of first-order matrix elements at $\bq=0$:

$$ \Sigma^{\rm DW}_{nn\bk}=-{\sum_{\nu m}}'\int\frac{d\bq}{\Omega_{\rm BZ}}\,\frac{g^{2,{\rm DW}}_{mn\nu}(\bk,\bq)}{\varepsilon_{n\bk}-\varepsilon_{m\bk}}\,(2n_{\bq\nu}+1). \tag{G.195}$$

The denominator connects states at the same $\bk$, and the overall sign is opposite to Fan–Migdal: the Debye–Waller term looks like a Fan term with the opposite sign, which is what guarantees zero shift for a uniform translation. The effective matrix element $g^{2,{\rm DW}}$ costs only first-order DFPT plus matrix multiplications. The rigid-ion error is about 10 meV in diamond and below 4 meV in all cases tested by Poncé et al. (2014). In diamond, Fan–Migdal and Debye–Waller are of comparable magnitude, so neither can be dropped.

The two sum rules and the effective Debye–Waller element

Translational invariance to first and second order in the displacements gives

$$ \sum_{\kappa p}\langle\psi_{n\bk}|\partial_{\kappa\alpha p}V^{\rm KS}|\psi_{n\bk}\rangle_{\rm sc}=0, \tag{G.193}$$ $$ \begin{aligned} \sum_{\kappa'p'}\langle\psi_{n\bk}|\partial^2_{\kappa\alpha p,\kappa'\alpha'p'}V^{\rm KS}|\psi_{n\bk}\rangle_{\rm sc}=&-2\,{\rm Re}\sum_{\kappa'p'}{\sum_{m\bq}}'\\ &\times\frac{\langle\psi_{n\bk}|\partial_{\kappa\alpha p}V^{\rm KS}|\psi_{m\bk+\bq}\rangle_{\rm sc}\langle\psi_{m\bk+\bq}|\partial_{\kappa'\alpha'p'}V^{\rm KS}|\psi_{n\bk}\rangle_{\rm sc}}{\varepsilon_{n\bk}-\varepsilon_{m\bk+\bq}}, \end{aligned} \tag{G.194}$$

where $\partial_{\kappa\alpha p}\equiv\partial/\partial\tau_{\kappa\alpha p}$, "sc" is an integral over the Born–von Kármán supercell, and the prime excludes degenerate states. The first rule says that the matrix elements of the three translational modes at $\bq=0$ vanish, another form of the acoustic sum rule. In the rigid-ion approximation only $\kappa'p'=\kappa p$ survives on the left of (G.194), which expresses the on-site second derivative through first derivatives. With Eqs. (20), (33)–(35), (38) and a canonical average over adiabatic nuclear states this gives (G.195) with

$$ g^{2,{\rm DW}}_{mn\nu}(\bk,\bq)=\sum_{\kappa\alpha,\kappa'\alpha'}\frac{t^\nu_{\kappa\alpha,\kappa'\alpha'}(\bq)}{2\omega_{\bq\nu}}\,h^*_{mn,\kappa\alpha}(\bk)\,h_{mn,\kappa'\alpha'}(\bk), \tag{G.196}$$ $$ t^\nu_{\kappa\alpha,\kappa'\alpha'}(\bq)=\frac{e_{\kappa\alpha\nu}(\bq)\,e^*_{\kappa\alpha'\nu}(\bq)}{M_\kappa}+\frac{e_{\kappa'\alpha\nu}(\bq)\,e^*_{\kappa'\alpha'\nu}(\bq)}{M_{\kappa'}}, \tag{G.197}$$ $$ h_{mn,\kappa\alpha}(\bk)=\sum_\nu(M_\kappa\omega_{0\nu})^{1/2}\,e_{\kappa\alpha\nu}(0)\,g_{mn\nu}(\bk,0). \tag{G.198}$$

$h$ is, up to normalization, the Cartesian matrix element $\langle\psi_{m\bk}|\partial_{\kappa\alpha,\bq=0}V^{\rm KS}|\psi_{n\bk}\rangle$, recovered from the mode-resolved $g$ at $\Gamma$ (real eigenvectors assumed). For the three translational modes at $\bq=0$ one sets the effective element to zero. Like (G.167), (G.195) needs sums over many empty states; Gonze, Boulanger and Côté (2011) replace most of them by a Sternheimer-type DFPT solution.

The Varshni curve and the zero-point renormalization

To see the generic shape, Giustino takes a semiconductor with parabolic, nondegenerate bands whose extrema couple to all states through one dispersionless phonon $\omega_0$, with Debye–Waller elements much smaller than Fan–Migdal. The dominant denominators in (G.167) are then intraband, $\varepsilon_{n\bk}-\varepsilon_{n\bk+\bq}\simeq\pm\hbar^2|\bq|^2/2m^*_n$ (upper sign for the valence band). The states just above the conduction-band minimum push it down, and the states just below the valence-band maximum push it up: level repulsion closes the gap,

$$ E_g(T)=E_g^{\rm cn}-|\Delta E_g^{\rm ZP}|\,\big[1+2n(\hbar\omega_0/k_BT)\big]. \tag{G.168}$$

Here $n(x)=1/(e^x-1)$ is the Bose–Einstein occupation and $1+2n(x)=\coth(x/2)$. There are two regimes. For $k_BT\ll\hbar\omega_0$ the Bose factor is exponentially small and the gap sits on a plateau at $E_g^{\rm cn}-|\Delta E_g^{\rm ZP}|$; it stays within 10% of the zero-point shift up to $k_BT\approx\hbar\omega_0/3$. For $k_BT\gg\hbar\omega_0$, $1+2n\simeq2k_BT/\hbar\omega_0+\hbar\omega_0/6k_BT$, so the gap falls linearly with slope $-2k_B|\Delta E_g^{\rm ZP}|/\hbar\omega_0$. The linear asymptote extrapolated to $T=0$ lands on $E_g^{\rm cn}$, not on the measured $E_g(0)$, and this is how the ZPR is extracted from experiment.

The red shift is the Varshni effect, first measured by Becker and Fan (1949), which prompted Fan's 1951 theory. It is not universal: copper halides and lead-halide perovskites show an "inverse Varshni" blue shift, and some chalcopyrites are nonmonotonic. Magnitudes can be large. For the direct gap of diamond, Giustino, Louie and Cohen (2010) confirmed a zero-point renormalization above 0.5 eV with DFT-LDA. Antonius et al. (2014) found that GW-corrected matrix elements increase the renormalization by up to about 200 meV; with them it is 628 meV (Giustino Fig. 13), because DFT over-screens the coupling (§A.9). In Fig. 13 their DFPT-LDA curve lies 0.23 eV above the GW one at $T=0$, which implies an LDA value near 0.4 eV if both share one clamped-nuclei gap; the review does not comment on the difference from the earlier value above 0.5 eV.

Lifetimes from the imaginary part

The imaginary part of the pole says how long a carrier stays in $|n\bk\rangle$. The persistence amplitude decays as $\exp(-|\Gamma_{n\bk}|\,|t'-t|/\hbar)$, so the probability decays twice as fast and the lifetime is $\tau_{n\bk}=\hbar/(2|\Gamma_{n\bk}|)$. Replacing $\tilde E_{n\bk}\to\varepsilon_{n\bk}$ in the Fan–Migdal self-energy and taking the imaginary part gives the zero-temperature golden rule (G Eq. 169). The retarded finite-temperature form (G.158) gives

$$ \begin{aligned} \frac{1}{\tau_{n\bk}}=\frac{2\pi}{\hbar}\sum_{m\nu}\int\frac{d\bq}{\Omega_{\rm BZ}}\,|g_{mn\nu}(\bk,\bq)|^2\Big[&(1-f_{m\bk+\bq}+n_{\bq\nu})\,\delta(\varepsilon_{n\bk}-\hbar\omega_{\bq\nu}-\varepsilon_{m\bk+\bq})\\ +&(f_{m\bk+\bq}+n_{\bq\nu})\,\delta(\varepsilon_{n\bk}+\hbar\omega_{\bq\nu}-\varepsilon_{m\bk+\bq})\Big]. \end{aligned} \tag{G.170}$$

This is Fermi's golden rule. The first line is phonon emission, stimulated ($n$) plus spontaneous ($1$), reduced by Pauli blocking of the final state ($-f$); the second is absorption. Debye–Waller does not contribute, because its diagonal elements are real. Neglecting $\hbar\omega$ in the delta functions, Giustino's toy model gives $\Gamma_{n\bk}(T)=\Gamma^{\rm ZP}_{n\bk}[1+2n(\hbar\omega_0/k_BT)]$: width and shift share one Bose factor, as in the figure above. A rigorous treatment multiplies the broadening by the quasiparticle weight $Z_{n\bk}$ of §A.7 and evaluates it at $E_{n\bk}$. For photoluminescence linewidths of lead-iodide perovskites the bare golden rule overestimates the data, and this $Z$ factor restores agreement (Giustino §X.A).

vs

In a polar semiconductor, carriers near the band edge scatter mainly with the LO phonon, through the long-range Fröhlich coupling of §A.4 (G Eqs. 2 and 7, $N_p$ dropped): $|g(\bq)|^2=\frac{e^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{4\pi}{\Omega}\frac{\hbar\omega_{\rm LO}}{2}\big(\frac{1}{\epsilon^\infty}-\frac{1}{\epsilon^0}\big)\frac{1}{q^2}$, with $\Omega$ the cell volume and $\epsilon^\infty$, $\epsilon^0$ the high-frequency and static dielectric constants. For one parabolic band of mass $m^*$, otherwise empty ($f=0$), (G.170) integrates in closed form (derivation below):

$$ \begin{aligned} \frac{1}{\tau(\varepsilon)}&=2\alpha\omega_{\rm LO}\sqrt{\frac{\hbar\omega_{\rm LO}}{\varepsilon}}\Big[n\,{\rm arsinh}\sqrt{\frac{\varepsilon}{\hbar\omega_{\rm LO}}}+(n+1)\,{\rm arsinh}\sqrt{\frac{\varepsilon}{\hbar\omega_{\rm LO}}-1}\;\Theta(\varepsilon-\hbar\omega_{\rm LO})\Big],\\ \alpha&=\frac{e^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0\hbar}\sqrt{\frac{m^*}{2\hbar\omega_{\rm LO}}}\,\Big(\frac{1}{\epsilon^\infty}-\frac{1}{\epsilon^0}\Big). \end{aligned} $$

Here $\varepsilon$ is the carrier energy above the band edge, $n=n(\hbar\omega_{\rm LO}/k_BT)$, and $\alpha$ is the dimensionless Fröhlich constant (SI; the standard definition, not printed in Giustino). Below the threshold $\varepsilon=\hbar\omega_{\rm LO}$ a carrier can only absorb phonons, so its rate is proportional to $n$ and freezes out at low temperature. Above it, spontaneous emission switches on with a steep $\sqrt{\varepsilon-\hbar\omega_{\rm LO}}$ onset. This is the low-energy end of Giustino's Fig. 15 for GaAs (Bernardi et al. 2015, Eq. (170) with Wannier interpolation): relaxation times of order a picosecond at the band bottom, a few hundred femtoseconds in the Γ valley, a sharp drop when the L valley opens at $E_L\approx0.25$ eV, and a few femtoseconds at 1.5 eV.

Adiabatic versus nonadiabatic: the Fröhlich catastrophe

Poncé et al. (2015) noticed that in polar crystals the adiabatic correction (G.167) to band extrema diverges as the broadening $\eta\to0$ on dense Brillouin-zone grids. The reason is power counting (Giustino §IX.A.1). Near an extremum the bands are parabolic, $|\varepsilon_{n\bk}-\varepsilon_{n\bk+\bq}|\approx\hbar^2q^2/2m^*$, and the long-range polar element (G Eq. 180) gives $|g|^2\propto1/q^2$. The intraband integrand of (G.167) therefore behaves as $q^{-4}$, while $d\bq=4\pi q^2dq$, and $\int dq/q^2$ diverges at small $q$.

Keeping the phonon energy cures it. In (G.166) the emission denominator at the band bottom is $-(\hbar^2q^2/2m^*+\hbar\omega_{\rm LO})$. It no longer vanishes as $q\to0$: below the polaron wave vector $q_p=(2m^*\omega_{\rm LO}/\hbar)^{1/2}$, where the kinetic energy transferred equals one phonon, it levels off at $-\hbar\omega_{\rm LO}$, so the small-$q$ integrand, $q^2\cdot q^{-2}/\hbar\omega_{\rm LO}$, stays finite. For the conduction-band minimum at $T=0$, with Rayleigh–Schrödinger denominators, a spherical zone of radius $q_D$ and a grid that resolves only $q\ge q_{\rm min}=q_D/N_q$ ($N_q$ points per direction), the two prescriptions give

$$ \Delta E^{\rm NA}=-\alpha\hbar\omega_{\rm LO}\,\frac{2}{\pi}\Big[\arctan\frac{q_D}{q_p}-\arctan\frac{q_{\rm min}}{q_p}\Big],\qquad \Delta E^{\rm A}=-\alpha\hbar\omega_{\rm LO}\,\frac{2}{\pi}\,q_p\Big(\frac{1}{q_{\rm min}}-\frac{1}{q_D}\Big). $$

The nonadiabatic shift converges to the weak-coupling polaron shift $-\alpha\hbar\omega_{\rm LO}$ once $q_{\rm min}\ll q_p$ and $q_D\gg q_p$, although slowly: the missing part falls only as $1/N_q$. The adiabatic shift grows linearly with $N_q$, without limit.

Derivation: closed forms for the polar model

Write the Fröhlich element as $|g(q)|^2=A/q^2$ with $A=\frac{e^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{4\pi}{\Omega}\frac{\hbar\omega_{\rm LO}}{2}\big(\frac1{\epsilon^\infty}-\frac1{\epsilon^0}\big)$, and use $\int d\bq/\Omega_{\rm BZ}\to(4\pi/\Omega_{\rm BZ})\int q^2dq\int d\mu/2$. The $q^2$ cancels the $1/q^2$. With $q_p^2=2m^*\omega_{\rm LO}/\hbar$ and the definition of $\alpha$ one finds $(4\pi/\Omega_{\rm BZ})A=(2/\pi)\,\alpha(\hbar\omega_{\rm LO})^2/q_p$.

Band-edge shift. At the CBM ($\bk=0$, $f=0$) the bracket of (G.166) at $T=0$ with $E\to\varepsilon$, $\Gamma\to0$ keeps only the emission term $-1/(\hbar^2q^2/2m^*+\hbar\omega_{\rm LO})=-(q_p^2/\hbar\omega_{\rm LO})/(q^2+q_p^2)$. Hence $\Delta E^{\rm NA}=-\alpha\hbar\omega_{\rm LO}(2/\pi)\int_{q_{\rm min}}^{q_D}dq\,q_p/(q^2+q_p^2)$, which is the arctangent form; with $q_{\rm min}\to0$, $q_D\to\infty$ it is $-\alpha\hbar\omega_{\rm LO}$. The adiabatic (G.167) drops $\hbar\omega_{\rm LO}$: the integrand becomes $q_p/q^2$, giving $\Delta E^{\rm A}$. The rule $q_{\rm min}=q_D/N_q$ mimics a uniform grid; a finite broadening $\eta$ plays the same regularizing role.

Scattering rate. Take $f=0$ in (G.170), so the weights are $n+1$ (emission) and $n$ (absorption). For $\bk\neq0$ the delta functions fix the angle: $\delta(\varepsilon_k\mp\hbar\omega_{\rm LO}-\varepsilon_{\bk+\bq})$ integrated over $\mu=\cos\theta$ gives $m^*/(\hbar^2kq)$ when a solution exists. Emission requires $k-\sqrt{k^2-q_p^2}<q<k+\sqrt{k^2-q_p^2}$, and $\int dq/q=2\,{\rm arcosh}(k/q_p)=2\,{\rm arsinh}\sqrt{\varepsilon/\hbar\omega_{\rm LO}-1}$. Absorption allows $\sqrt{k^2+q_p^2}-k<q<\sqrt{k^2+q_p^2}+k$, giving $2\,{\rm arsinh}(k/q_p)$. The prefactor in front of $\int dq/q$ is $(2\pi/\hbar)(\Omega/8\pi^3)\,2\pi A\,m^*/(\hbar^2k)=\Omega Am^*/(2\pi\hbar^3k)$; with the factor 2 from the logarithms it becomes $\Omega Am^*/(\pi\hbar^3k)=2\alpha\omega_{\rm LO}\,q_p/k$, and $q_p/k=\sqrt{\hbar\omega_{\rm LO}/\varepsilon}$. This is the rate formula above, the textbook result for polar-optical scattering; we checked it against direct numerical integration of (G.170).

Physical picture: a slow electron in a fast lattice

Dropping $\hbar\omega$ treats the zero-point displacements as a frozen random distortion that the electron adjusts to instantly. That is fine at short wavelengths, where a scattering event changes the electron's energy by much more than $\hbar\omega_{\rm LO}$. For $q\ll q_p$ the change, $\hbar^2q^2/2m^*$, is smaller than $\hbar\omega_{\rm LO}$: during one ionic oscillation the electron barely moves, so it cannot exploit these long-wavelength fluctuations as if they were static. Instead the ions follow the electron and build a polarization cloud of radius about $1/q_p$ (40 Å in GaAs) that travels with it, the large polaron of §A.4. The frozen-lattice formula keeps collecting energy from ever longer wavelengths of the dipole field, where its denominators vanish, and the sum diverges.

Giustino lists the remedies: principal-value integration with finite $\omega_{\bq\nu}$, taking $\omega\to0$ afterwards (Fan 1951); analytic integration near $\bq=0$ (Nery and Allen 2016); the Brillouin–Wigner form (G.166) with $\eta$ replaced by the physical linewidth $\Gamma_{n\bk}$; or full spectral functions (Kawai et al. 2014; Antonius et al. 2015). The Fröhlich part of the ZPR is not small. Botti and Marques (2013) computed $W$ including lattice screening, with a Lyddane–Sachs–Teller model dielectric function, and found ZPRs above 1 eV in LiF, LiCl, NaCl and MgO. Nery and Allen found a Fröhlich contribution of 45 meV out of 150 meV for GaN; the model below gives about 40 meV with literature parameters.

Bridge to EPP: what finite $c$ does and does not do

Everything here is electrostatic: "nonadiabatic" means keeping $\hbar\omega_{\bq\nu}$ in electron denominators, not photons. In EPP the Coulomb interaction is the longitudinal photon, and the longitudinal kernel is $c$-independent (§X.1), so the LO Fröhlich vertex that drives this divergence is not changed by retardation in EPP's sense (finite $c$) at all. Finite $c$ adds transverse-photon coupling of TO phonons for $q\lesssim q_c=\sqrt{\epsilon^\infty}\,\omega/c\sim10^{-4}$ Å$^{-1}$, inside the rose band above (EPP §7.2, §B.8). Even if that region were removed entirely, the adiabatic integral cut at $q_c$ would still give a shift of order $-0.7$ eV for GaAs. What EPP's dynamical vertex does add is the frequency dependence of the electronic screening, $\epsilon^\infty\to\hat\epsilon^{\rm e}(\bq,\omega)$, which is retardation in Giustino's sense and which his approximation (v) drops; in doped polar semiconductors, where plasmons and LO phonons mix, this could change Fröhlich shifts and lifetimes, but EPP does not derive an electron self-energy. See §Z.1.

Nonperturbative adiabatic calculations

An alternative skips perturbation theory and matrix elements. Expand $\varepsilon_{n\bk}$ to second order in the atomic displacements and average over the harmonic nuclear wavefunction with phonon numbers $\{n_{\bq\nu}\}$:

$$ \langle\varepsilon_{n\bk}\rangle_{\{n_{\bq\nu}\}}=\varepsilon_{n\bk}+\sum_\nu\int\frac{d\bq}{\Omega_{\rm BZ}}\big(n_{\bq\nu}+\tfrac12\big)\frac{\partial\varepsilon_{n\bk}}{\partial n_{\bq\nu}}, \tag{G.199}$$

with the formal definition $\partial/\partial n_{\bq\nu}\equiv\Delta_{\bq\nu}\Delta_{-\bq\nu}$; a canonical average turns $n_{\bq\nu}$ into its Bose–Einstein value. This is the starting point of Allen and Cardona (1981). Evaluating the second derivatives perturbatively gives back Allen–Heine. Evaluating them by finite differences of eigenvalues in supercells displaced along the phonon eigenvectors (frozen phonons) needs no rigid-ion approximation and can go beyond the harmonic approximation, at a high cost that Monte Carlo sampling, a single "optimum" configuration (Zacharias and Giustino 2016) or nondiagonal supercells (Lloyd-Williams and Monserrat 2015) reduce. The Williams–Lax average of the dielectric function over nuclear configurations applies the same idea to optical spectra (G Eq. 201, §A.9). Being adiabatic, (G.199) treats long-wavelength polar modes like (G.167), so one expects its Fröhlich part to grow with supercell size (our inference from the model above; the review does not discuss it).

What these formulas leave out

(G.166)–(G.170) are harmonic, at the Migdal level, with adiabatic phonons and a fixed lattice parameter, so the thermal-expansion contribution to $E_g(T)$, which measured gaps include, is not part of them. The linewidth trend of Fig. 3(b) needs states at least one phonon energy away from a band extremum, and the toy model misses the low-temperature subtleties of Allen and Nery (2017). DFT matrix elements are usually too small: the ZPR scales roughly as $(\epsilon^\infty)^{-2}$, so a 20% error in the screening becomes about 40% in the ZPR (Giustino §XII, §A.9).
