DFPT: the vertex is screened

When an ion moves, the electrons move with it. Density-functional perturbation theory computes the self-consistent result one wavevector at a time, and the answer is the bare electron–ion perturbation screened by a static dielectric matrix: the same $\epsilon=1-v\chi^0$ that a GW or BSE code builds, plus exchange and correlation.

Displace sublattice $\kappa$ by a small wave, $\Delta\tau_{\kappa\alpha p}\propto e^{i\bq\cdot\bR_p}$. Each ion carries its bare potential along, and that change alone, $\partial V^{\rm en}$, would scatter electrons far too strongly (Nordheim's model, §A.2). But the electrons respond: charge flows toward the regions the ions have made more attractive, and the Hartree and exchange–correlation potential of the induced charge cancels most of the bare change. What an electron finally feels, and what enters $g_{mn\nu}(\bk,\bq)$, is the self-consistent remainder. Giustino §III.B builds it from Kohn–Sham theory and condenses it into one line, $\partial v^{\rm KS}=(\epsilon^{{\rm H}xc})^{-1}\partial v^{\rm en}$ (G.53). As in all of Part A, units are SI and every $V$ or $v$ is the potential energy of an electron of charge $-e$.

A self-consistent potential, one wavevector at a time

The electrons obey the Kohn–Sham equation with $\hat H^{\rm KS}=-\hbar^2\nabla^2/2m_e+V^{\rm KS}$ (G.23), whose potential has three parts:

$$ V^{\rm KS}=V^{\rm en}+V^{\rm H}+V^{xc}. \tag{G.24}$$

$V^{\rm en}$ sums ionic potentials with the Coulomb tail $-(e^2/4\pi\epsilon_0)Z_\kappa/|\br|$ (G.25–26), $V^{\rm H}$ is the instantaneous Coulomb potential of the density (G.27) and $V^{xc}=\delta E^{xc}/\delta n$ (G.28). There is no vector potential anywhere: the electrons see the nuclei only through electrostatics. To first order in the displacements,

$$ V^{\rm KS}(\{\boldsymbol\tau_{\kappa p}\})=V^{\rm KS}(\{\boldsymbol\tau^0_{\kappa p}\})+\sum_{\kappa\alpha p}\frac{\partial V^{\rm KS}}{\partial\tau_{\kappa\alpha p}}\Delta\tau_{\kappa\alpha p}, \tag{G.31}$$

where the derivative is the self-consistent one: $V^{\rm H}$ and $V^{xc}$ change because the density changes. For a displacement wave with phases $e^{i\bq\cdot\bR_p}$ the summed response is $e^{i\bq\cdot\br}$ times a lattice-periodic function,

$$ \partial_{\kappa\alpha,\bq}v^{\rm KS}=\sum_p e^{-i\bq\cdot(\br-\bR_p)}\,\frac{\partial V^{\rm KS}}{\partial\tau_{\kappa\alpha}}\bigg|_{\br-\bR_p}, \tag{G.35}$$

which, weighted by the normal-mode amplitudes, becomes the $\Delta_{\bq\nu}v^{\rm KS}$ of (G.34) and the matrix element (G.38) of §A.2. Linear response does not mix wavevectors, so each $\bq$ is a separate calculation in one unit cell. Frozen phonons instead take finite differences (G.41) in a supercell commensurate with $\bq$, impractically large at long wavelengths; Giustino puts the DFPT saving at a factor proportional to $N_p^2$, typically more than $10^3$.

DFPT splits the perturbation like the potential,

$$\partial_{\kappa\alpha,\bq}v^{\rm KS}=\partial_{\kappa\alpha,\bq}v^{\rm en}+\partial_{\kappa\alpha,\bq}v^{\rm H}+\partial_{\kappa\alpha,\bq}v^{xc}. \tag{G.42}$$

The bare part is known in closed form in reciprocal space,

$$\partial_{\kappa\alpha,\bq}v^{\rm en}(\bG)=-i(\bq+\bG)_\alpha\,V_\kappa(\bq+\bG)\,e^{-i(\bq+\bG)\cdot\boldsymbol\tau_\kappa}, \tag{G.43}$$

a gradient (moving the ion by $\boldsymbol\tau$ shifts $V_\kappa(\br-\boldsymbol\tau)$) times a structure factor, with $f(\bG)=\Omega^{-1}\int_{\rm uc}d\br\,e^{-i\bG\cdot\br}f(\br)$. For a Coulomb ion $V_\kappa(\bq)\propto1/q^2$, so the $\bG=0$ term grows as $1/q$: it is the long-range field of a displaced charge, and only the displacement component along $\bq+\bG$ couples. The induced parts are linear in the induced density $\partial_{\kappa\alpha,\bq}n$,

$$\begin{align} \partial_{\kappa\alpha,\bq}v^{\rm H}(\bG)&=\Omega\,v^{\rm C}(\bq+\bG)\,\partial_{\kappa\alpha,\bq}n(\bG), \tag{G.44}\\ \partial_{\kappa\alpha,\bq}v^{xc}(\bG)&=\Omega\sum_{\bG'}f^{xc}(\bq+\bG,\bq+\bG')\,\partial_{\kappa\alpha,\bq}n(\bG'), \tag{G.45} \end{align}$$

with $v^{\rm C}(\bq)=e^2/(\epsilon_0\Omega q^2)$ the Coulomb kernel per cell and $f^{xc}=\delta^2E^{xc}/\delta n(\br)\delta n(\br')$ the xc kernel (G.46), local in the LDA.

The Sternheimer equation

The induced density needs the first-order change of the occupied orbitals. Translational symmetry fixes its form, $\partial u_{v\bk,\bq}e^{i\bq\cdot\br}$ with $\partial u$ lattice periodic, and first-order perturbation theory written as a linear system, rather than as a sum over states, is the Sternheimer equation:

$$(\hat H^{\rm KS}_{\bk+\bq}-\varepsilon_{v\bk})\,\partial u_{v\bk,\bq}=-\partial_{\kappa\alpha,\bq}v^{\rm KS}\,u_{v\bk}, \tag{G.47}$$

with $\hat H^{\rm KS}_{\bk+\bq}=e^{-i(\bk+\bq)\cdot\br}\hat H^{\rm KS}e^{i(\bk+\bq)\cdot\br}$. Only occupied states $v$ appear. There is no sum over empty bands, which Giustino names as the principal advantage over standard perturbation theory. (At $\bq=0$ the first-order eigenvalue shift $\langle u_{v\bk}|\partial_{\kappa\alpha,0}v|u_{v\bk}\rangle_{\rm uc}u_{v\bk}$ joins the right-hand side; the projectors below cancel it except in metals.) The operator on the left is ill conditioned whenever an occupied state at $\bk+\bq$ lies close to $\varepsilon_{v\bk}$, and singular at an exact degeneracy. Those directions are harmless, because rotating occupied states into each other leaves the density unchanged. Giannozzi et al. (1991) therefore keep only the component in the unoccupied manifold, $\partial\tilde u=(1-\hat P^{\rm occ}_{\bk+\bq})\partial u$:

$$(\hat H^{\rm KS}_{\bk+\bq}-\varepsilon_{v\bk})\,\partial\tilde u_{v\bk,\bq}=-(1-\hat P^{\rm occ}_{\bk+\bq})\,\partial_{\kappa\alpha,\bq}v^{\rm KS}\,u_{v\bk}. \tag{G.48}$$

Since $\hat P^{\rm occ}_{\bk+\bq}\partial\tilde u=0$, adding $\alpha\hat P^{\rm occ}_{\bk+\bq}$ to the operator leaves the solution unchanged but, with $\alpha$ larger than the valence bandwidth, removes every small eigenvalue. The density response is then

$$\partial n_{\kappa\alpha,\bq}(\br)=2N_p^{-1}\sum_{v\bk}u^*_{v\bk}\,\partial\tilde u_{v\bk,\bq}, \tag{G.49}$$

where the explicit 2 comes from time-reversal symmetry and the spin factor is already inside the $\bk$ sum. Closing the loop through (G.44) and (G.45) gives a cycle much like a ground-state SCF calculation:

  1. 1 · start
    bare ionic perturbation, $\partial v^{\rm KS}=\partial v^{\rm en}$ (G.43)
  2. 2 · Sternheimer
    solve for $\partial\tilde u_{v\bk,\bq}$, every occupied $v$ and every $\bk$ (G.48)
  3. 3 · response
    induced density $\partial n_{\kappa\alpha,\bq}$ (G.49)
  4. 4 · Hartree + xc
    $\partial v^{\rm H}$ and $\partial v^{xc}$ from $\partial n$ (G.44–45)
  5. 5 · update
    new $\partial v^{\rm KS}$ (G.42); mix and return to 2 until $\partial n$ stops changing

The converged $\partial v^{\rm KS}$ also yields the force constants (G.13) of §A.1, so every DFPT phonon code "already contains all the information necessary" for $g_{mn\nu}(\bk,\bq)$ (Giustino).

The loop sums a geometric series

To see what the loop computes, Giustino expands $\partial\tilde u$ over all Kohn–Sham states, occupied and empty, and inserts it into (G.48) and (G.49). The induced density is linear in the total perturbation,

$$\partial_{\kappa\alpha,\bq}n(\br)=\int_{\rm uc}d\br'\,\chi^0_{\bq}(\br,\br')\,\partial_{\kappa\alpha,\bq}v^{\rm KS}(\br'), \tag{G.50}$$
$$\begin{aligned}\chi^0_{\bq}(\br,\br')=N_p^{-1}\sum_{mn\bk}&\frac{f_{n\bk}-f_{m\bk+\bq}}{\varepsilon_{n\bk}-\varepsilon_{m\bk+\bq}}\\ &\times u^*_{n\bk}(\br)\,u_{m\bk+\bq}(\br)\,u^*_{m\bk+\bq}(\br')\,u_{n\bk}(\br').\end{aligned} \tag{G.51}$$

This is the independent-particle polarizability of Adler and Wiser, the $\chi^0$ that a GW or BSE code assembles from a sum over empty bands, here at $\omega=0$. Writing (G.50) symbolically as $\partial n=\chi^0\partial v^{\rm KS}$, the three pieces of (G.42) combine into a self-consistency condition and its solution:

$$\begin{align} \partial v^{\rm KS}&=\partial v^{\rm en}+(v^{\rm C}+f^{xc})\chi^0\,\partial v^{\rm KS}, \tag{G.52}\\ \partial v^{\rm KS}&=(\epsilon^{{\rm H}xc})^{-1}\partial v^{\rm en}, \tag{G.53}\\ \epsilon^{{\rm H}xc}&=1-(v^{\rm C}+f^{xc})\chi^0. \tag{G.54} \end{align}$$

Iterating (G.52) from $\partial v^{\rm KS}=\partial v^{\rm en}$, without mixing, generates the Neumann series of $(\epsilon^{{\rm H}xc})^{-1}$. With $K\equiv v^{\rm C}+f^{xc}$,

$$\partial v^{\rm KS}=\big[\,1+K\chi^0+K\chi^0K\chi^0+K\chi^0K\chi^0K\chi^0+\cdots\big]\,\partial v^{\rm en}.$$

Each power adds one bubble $\chi^0$ and one interaction line $K$. Read as diagrams, the dressed vertex is the bare vertex followed by a chain of bubbles:

Giustino's Fig. 1(d) draws the same resummation in Dyson form, $g=g^{\rm b}+vGG\Gamma g$, with the many-body polarization $P=GG\Gamma$ in place of $\chi^0$ (Giustino §V.A.3; §A.6).

Physical picture · rounds of response

Each term is one round of response: the bare field induces a density, the Hartree and xc potential of that density induces more, and so on. In a metal at long wavelength every round overcorrects, $|K\chi^0|\simeq(k_{\rm TF}/q)^2\gg1$, so the terms alternate in sign and grow. Yet the resummed answer $1/\epsilon$ is tiny, because the Fermi sea neutralizes a slowly varying perturbation almost perfectly. (G.53) is still the right answer, but the literal series diverges, and an algorithm has to reach it another way.

The usual way is mixing. With linear mixing (standard practice, not spelled out by Giustino) each cycle updates

$$\partial v_{n+1}=(1-\beta)\,\partial v_n+\beta\big[\partial v^{\rm en}+K\chi^0\,\partial v_n\big],$$

whose fixed point is (G.53) for any $\beta$. The error shrinks by $r=1-\beta\epsilon^{{\rm H}xc}$ per cycle, so the loop converges only if $0<\beta\epsilon<2$, and at $\beta=1$ the iterates are exactly the partial sums of the series. In jellium each Fourier component decouples and $v^{\rm C}\chi^0=-(k_{\rm TF}/q)^2F(q/2k_{\rm F})$, so the loop reduces to numbers. At long wavelength $\epsilon\to(k_{\rm TF}/q)^2$, and any fixed $\beta$ fails at small enough $q$: the induced charge overshoots back and forth, the "charge sloshing" familiar from ground-state SCF cycles in metals. Kerker's preconditioner (1981; literature, not in Giustino) uses $\beta(q)=\beta_0q^2/(q^2+q_0^2)$ with $q_0\approx k_{\rm TF}$. For Thomas–Fermi screening this makes $\beta\epsilon=\beta_0$ exactly; with the Lindhard function $\beta\epsilon$ stays between about $\beta_0/2$ and $\beta_0$ at every $q$, so any $\beta_0<2$ converges.

Test electron, test charge, and your $\epsilon=1-v\chi^0$

Giustino calls $\epsilon^{{\rm H}xc}$ the "test-electron" dielectric matrix, a name hinting that the density redistribution arises both from classical electrostatics ($v^{\rm C}\chi^0$) and from quantum exchange and correlation ($f^{xc}\chi^0$). The particle that feels the screened potential is itself an electron, so it also feels the xc potential of the induced density. Neglecting $f^{xc}$ gives the "test-charge" matrix, the random-phase approximation of Pines and Bohm (1952),

$$\epsilon^{\rm H}=1-v^{\rm C}\chi^0, \tag{G.55}$$

which in a plane-wave basis reads

$$\epsilon^{\rm H}_{\bG\bG'}(\bq)=\delta_{\bG\bG'}-\Omega^2\sum_{\bG''}\chi^0_{\bG''\bG'}(\bq)\,v^{\rm C}(\bq+\bG)\,\delta_{\bG\bG''}. \tag{G.56}$$

Its off-diagonal elements are the crystal local-field effects. Together with (G.38), Eq. (G.53) says that the DFPT matrix element is the bare electron–nucleus vertex screened at the "RPA + $xc$" level. For a reader who computes $\chi^0$ for 2D materials, the dictionary is short:

  • Same matrix. (G.56) is the $\epsilon_{\bG\bG'}(\bq,\omega=0)$ of a GW or BSE code, local fields included. The static $W=\epsilon^{-1}v$ of a BSE kernel uses exactly this object.
  • Different operand. $W$ screens the electron–electron interaction; DFPT screens the electron–ion perturbation, $g=\epsilon^{-1}g^{\rm b}$ in the notation of Giustino §V.A. The phonon self-energy contains one such screened vertex and the Fan–Migdal electron self-energy two (§A.6, §A.7).
  • Test electron. DFPT keeps $f^{xc}$; the usual GW $W$ is the test-charge RPA.
  • No inversion. DFPT never forms or inverts $\epsilon$. The loop solves (G.52) iteratively, one $\bq$ at a time, without empty states.
  • Two dimensions. A monolayer needs the same truncated Coulomb kernel in DFPT as in GW (implemented by Sohier, Calandra and Mauri, 2017; literature, not in either paper). For a semiconducting sheet the static screening takes the Rytova–Keldysh form $\epsilon_{\rm 2D}(q)\simeq1+r_0q$ familiar from 2D BSE (standard result, not printed in either paper). It tends to 1 as $q\to0$, so long-wavelength perturbations are barely screened, whereas in bulk $\epsilon\to\epsilon^\infty$ or $(k_{\rm TF}/q)^2$. Together with the 2D Coulomb kernel, which goes as $1/q$ instead of $1/q^2$, this shapes the 2D phonon results of §B.10, where the same $r_0$ appears.

Subtlety · what the labels mean

"Test charge" in (G.55) is shorthand for RPA. With xc present, a classical test charge would feel $\partial v^{\rm en}+v^{\rm C}\partial n$ with $\partial n$ computed including $f^{xc}$, that is $\epsilon^{-1}=1+v^{\rm C}\chi^0[1-(v^{\rm C}+f^{xc})\chi^0]^{-1}$ (standard linear-response result, not written by Giustino), which equals $(\epsilon^{\rm H})^{-1}$ only when $f^{xc}=0$. Separately, static density response is a ground-state property: with the exact functional, DFPT would give the exact static response and force constants. Using $\partial v^{\rm KS}$ as the vertex for quasiparticles is a further approximation, which Giustino makes explicit in §V.A (approximation (iii): the many-body dielectric matrix is replaced by its DFT "RPA + $xc$" counterpart; §A.6).

Jellium: Bardeen recovered

Free electrons make (G.51) analytic. With $\varepsilon_{n\bk}=\hbar^2(\bk+\bG_n)^2/2m_e-\varepsilon_{\rm F}$ in the extended-zone scheme the polarizability is diagonal,

$$\chi^0_{\bG\bG'}(\bq)=-\frac{m_ek_{\rm F}}{\pi^2\hbar^2\Omega}\,F(|\bq+\bG|/2k_{\rm F})\,\delta_{\bG\bG'}, \tag{G.59}$$

with $F$ the Lindhard function of (G.4) and $m_ek_{\rm F}/\pi^2\hbar^2$ the density of states per volume at $\varepsilon_{\rm F}$, both spins. Inserted in (G.56) without xc, it gives the Lindhard dielectric matrix,

$$\epsilon_{\bG\bG'}(\bq)=\delta_{\bG\bG'}\Big[1+\frac{k^2_{\rm TF}}{|\bq+\bG|^2}F(|\bq+\bG|/2k_{\rm F})\Big], \tag{G.60}$$

with $k_{\rm TF}=[4m_ee^2k_{\rm F}/(4\pi\epsilon_0\pi\hbar^2)]^{1/2}$. Screening the bare one-atom matrix element with its diagonal element, following (G.53), gives Bardeen's 1937 result (cf. G.61, with $N_p$ removed as explained in §A.2):

$$g_{mn\nu}(\bk,\bq)=-i\Big(\frac{\hbar}{2M_\kappa\omega_{\bq\nu}}\Big)^{1/2}\bq\cdot\mathbf e_{\kappa\nu}(\bq)\,\frac{V_\kappa(\bq)}{\epsilon(q)}.$$

Its long-wavelength limit, $V_\kappa/\epsilon\to-\tfrac23\varepsilon_{\rm F}$, is derived and plotted in §A.2. Here we look at the same screening in real space: displacing one ion along $z$ creates the bare dipole field $-\partial_zV_\kappa$, and screening multiplies each of its Fourier components by $1/\epsilon(q)$.

Derivation: the screened dipole field in real space

Use the continuum Fourier transform $V_\kappa(\bq)=-Ze^2/(\epsilon_0q^2)$; Giustino's $\Omega^{-1}$ normalization cancels in the ratio below. Displacing the ion by $\tau$ along $z$ changes the potential by $-\tau\,\partial_zV_\kappa$, whose Fourier components $-iq_zV_\kappa(\bq)$ are the $\bG=0$ term of (G.43). In jellium (G.53) divides each by $\epsilon(q)$, so the screened perturbation is $-\partial_z$ of the screened ion potential

$$V^{\rm scr}(r)=\int\!\frac{d\bq}{(2\pi)^3}\,\frac{V_\kappa(\bq)}{\epsilon(q)}\,e^{i\bq\cdot\br}=-\frac{Ze^2}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\,\frac{2}{\pi r}\int_0^\infty\!dq\,\frac{\sin qr}{q\,\epsilon(q)}.$$

For $\epsilon=1$ this is $-Ze^2/4\pi\epsilon_0r$. Along the displacement axis $\partial v/\partial\tau_z=-dV^{\rm scr}/dr$. Dividing by the bare value $-Ze^2/(4\pi\epsilon_0r^2)$ and writing $1/\epsilon=1+(1/\epsilon-1)$ gives the surviving fraction

$$S(r)=1+\frac{2}{\pi}\int_0^\infty\!dq\,\Big[\frac{1}{\epsilon(q)}-1\Big]\Big[\frac{\sin qr}{q}-r\cos qr\Big].$$

Thomas–Fermi screening, $\epsilon=1+k_{\rm TF}^2/q^2$, gives $S=(1+k_{\rm TF}r)\,e^{-k_{\rm TF}r}$ in closed form. For the Lindhard function the figure integrates numerically (trapezoid rule, $q\le12k_{\rm F}$). With the toy xc kernel, $1/\epsilon-1\to+k_{\rm TF}^2/3q^2$ at large $q$, a slowly decaying tail. The code subtracts $(k_{\rm TF}^2/3)/(q^2+4k_{\rm F}^2)$, which has the same tail, and integrates that term analytically.

Static means adiabatic

"So far we considered only the screening of static perturbations," Giustino stresses: $\partial v^{\rm en}$ was taken to be frequency independent. Physically the phonons are treated as quasistatic perturbations, "so that at each set of instantaneous atomic positions during a vibration cycle, the electrons have enough time to readjust and reach their ground state", which is the adiabatic approximation of Born and Oppenheimer. In the diagrams above, every bubble is $\chi^0$ at $\omega=0$ rather than at the phonon frequency. That is accurate when $\hbar\omega_{\bq\nu}$ is small against the electronic excitations that build $\chi^0$ (the gap of an insulator, or $\hbar qv_{\rm F}$ for intraband transitions in a metal), and it fails otherwise. Bardeen and Pines (1955) already screened the vertex with $\epsilon(q,\omega_{\bq\nu})$ (G.5, §A.2), but the first ab initio calculations of these effects came only much later (Lazzeri and Mauri, 2006, cited by Giustino; the doped-graphene case returns in his §VII and in §A.6). "Retardation" in Giustino's sense means exactly this frequency dependence of the screening. His formal framework is §IV (§A.5), and §A.6 splits the phonon self-energy into the adiabatic part that DFPT computes and a nonadiabatic remainder built from $\chi^0(\omega)-\chi^0(0)$.

Bridge to EPP · the same chain, kept dynamic

EPP resums the same bubble chain for its photon propagator, $D^{\rm e}=D^0+D^0\Pi^{\rm e,0}_{jj}D^{\rm e}$ (EPP §7, Eq. (E.388)), and its screened electron–phonon vertex has $D^{\rm e}$ where the bare one has $D^0$ (Eqs. (E.415)–(E.416), §B.8). Three things differ from DFPT. The screening is dynamic: the dielectric tensor $\hat\epsilon^{\rm e}(\bq,\omega)$ (Eqs. (E.431)–(E.432)) keeps the frequency dependence that DFPT drops and acts on transverse as well as longitudinal photons, so in EPP "retardation" also means the finite speed of light (§X.1). It is RPA without $f^{xc}$ and without crystal local fields (no $\bG,\bG'$ dependence from Eq. (E.380) on). For $c\to\infty$ its longitudinal projection becomes the scalar RPA function $1-v\Pi^{\rm e,0}_{\rho\rho}$, which at $\omega=0$ is (G.55) without local fields (§B.9). And EPP cannot take DFPT force constants as they come: which part of them may be combined with photon exchange is its double-counting rule, set out in §B.1.

Second order: the Debye–Waller matrix element

Expanding $V^{\rm KS}$ to first order while expanding the energy (G.12) to second order is "somewhat inconsistent" (Allen and Heine, 1976; Allen, 1978), so the Hamiltonian (G.1) also carries the second-order term (G.39), with

$$g^{\rm DW}_{mn\nu\nu'}(\bk,\bq,\bq')=\tfrac12\langle u_{m\bk+\bq+\bq'}|\Delta_{\bq\nu}\Delta_{\bq'\nu'}v^{\rm KS}|u_{n\bk}\rangle_{\rm uc}. \tag{G.40}$$

It needs the second derivative of the self-consistent potential, hence second-order Sternheimer equations (Gonze, 1995). Because these are involved, practical implementations express $g^{\rm DW}$ through products of the first-order $g_{mn\nu}(\bk,\bq)$, following Allen and Heine (1976) and Allen and Cardona (1981). According to Giustino, all recent DFPT calculations of electron–phonon effects use this approach (Giustino §IX.A.1; §A.7, §A.8).
